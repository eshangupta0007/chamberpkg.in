A law that requires documents to be registered has to decide what happens when they are not. The requirement is addressed to people who will often have no reason to comply and no one to make them, so the sanction cannot be a penalty on the defaulter alone; it has to touch the document, because the document is the only thing the State will later be asked to act on. There are two ways to touch it. The first is to deny it effect, so that the transaction it records does not happen in law. The second is to deny it proof, so that the transaction may have happened but cannot be established in a court. These are different instruments with different consequences, and a statute that reaches for both has to be read carefully, because they do not fail in the same way.

The Registration Act, 1908 reaches for both. Section 17, in Part III, lists the documents whose registration is compulsory. Section 49, in Part X — the Part headed "Of the Effects of Registration and Non-registration" — states what follows if one of them is not registered, and it states it three times over, once in the language of effect and twice in the language of proof. Then a proviso, inserted by Act 21 of 1929, returns part of what the section has just withheld.

What follows is an account of how much the proviso returns, how much more it returned until 24 September 2001, and what the Act now offers the person who holds an unregistered writing and the possession that was given under it.

## What section 49 withholds, and in three ways

Section 49 reads:

> "No document required by section 17 or by any provision of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (4 of 1882), to be registered shall— (a) affect any immovable property comprised therein, or (b) confer any power to adopt, or (c) be received as evidence of any transaction affecting such property or conferring such power, unless it has been registered"

The three clauses are not three statements of one idea. Clause (a) is about the operation of the document: whatever the document purports to do to the property, it does not do. Clause (b) is the same proposition applied to the one non-property power the section deals with. Clause (c) is about the use of the document in litigation, and it is wider than the first two in one respect and narrower in another. Wider, because it bars the document not merely from working the transfer but from being received as evidence of *any* transaction affecting the property — including transactions it did not itself effect. Narrower, because it is a rule about a piece of paper and not about a fact: it tells a court what it may not receive, and it says nothing about what the court may find from other material.

The distinction matters for what comes next. A proviso that relaxes clause (c) changes the law of proof. A proviso that relaxed clause (a) would change the law of transfer. Section 49 has only the first kind.

## The two escapes the proviso allows

> "Provided that an unregistered document affecting immovable property and required by this Act or the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (4 of 1882), to be registered may be received as evidence of a contract in a suit for specific performance under Chapter II of the Specific Relief Act, 1877 (1 of 1877), or as evidence of any collateral transaction not required to be effected by registered instrument."

Both escapes are from clause (c) and from nothing else. The document still does not affect the property; it is only that in two situations a court may look at it.

The first situation is a suit for specific performance. The unregistered writing may be received as evidence of the contract — not as the transfer it purports to be, but as proof that the parties agreed on terms which a court can now be asked to enforce. The relief sought is a decree directing the execution of the registered instrument that was never executed. The document is evidence of the claim, and the claim is the whole proceeding.

The second situation is a collateral transaction. The document may be received as evidence of something it incidentally records, provided that the something is not itself a transaction required by law to be effected by a registered instrument. This escape is defined by exclusion and therefore cannot be stated as a list. What it plainly cannot cover is the transaction the document was written to effect, because that transaction is not collateral to the document; it is the document's whole content.

Those are the two. Until 2001 there were three.

## The escape that was there until 2001

The compilation footnotes the proviso to record what has been taken out of it. The words removed were:

> "or as evidence of part performance of a contract for the purposes of section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (4 of 1882)"

They were omitted by section 6 of the Registration and Other Related Laws (Amendment) Act, 2001 (48 of 2001), with effect from 24 September 2001. Until that date the proviso had a third limb, and the third limb was of a different character from the other two. The first limb assists a plaintiff who is suing. The second assists anyone who needs the document for a purpose other than the one it was written for. The third assisted a defendant who was not suing at all, and who wanted the document looked at for the sole purpose of resisting the person who had signed it.

## Section 53A, and what it was for

Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, inserted by Act 20 of 1929, now reads:

> "Where any person contracts to transfer for consideration any immoveable property by writing signed by him or on his behalf from which the terms necessary to constitute the transfer can be ascertained with reasonable certainty, and the transferee has, in part performance of the contract, taken possession of the property or any part thereof, or the transferee, being already in possession, continues in possession in part performance of the contract and has done some act in furtherance of the contract, and the transferee has performed or is willing to perform his part of the contract, then, notwithstanding that ***, or, where there is an instrument of transfer, that the transfer has not been completed in the manner prescribed therefor by the law for the time being in force, the transferor or any person claiming under him shall be debarred from enforcing against the transferee and persons claiming under him any right in respect of the property of which the transferee has taken or continued in possession, other than a right expressly provided by the terms of the contract"

Three conditions are stacked before the consequence: a signed writing from which the terms can be ascertained with reasonable certainty; possession taken, or possession continued together with some act in furtherance of the contract; and performance or willingness to perform by the transferee. The consequence is not a transfer. The transferee gets no title and no right he can assert against the world; what he gets is that the transferor "shall be debarred from enforcing" against him any right in respect of the property, other than a right the contract itself gave the transferor. A proviso keeps this away from "a transferee for consideration who has no notice of the contract or of the part performance thereof". The section is a bar, and it operates only against the person who signed the writing and those claiming under him.

A bar of that kind is worth noticing on its own terms. It is a rule about who may sue whom rather than a rule about who owns what, and it is one of the places where the statute declines to resolve the title and resolves only the dispute. The person protected by it does not become an owner; he becomes someone the owner cannot remove.

The asterisks in the passage above are the compilation's mark for words no longer there, and the gap is a second omission made by the same Act of 2001. The words taken out were:

> "the contract though required to be registered, has not been registered, or"

That omission was made by section 10 of Act 48 of 2001, again with effect from 24 September 2001. Before it, section 53A said in its own voice what the third limb of the proviso to section 49 said from the other side: the bar applies although the contract, which should have been registered, was not.

## The four amendments of 24 September 2001

The Act of 2001 made four changes that have to be read together, and the order in which they appear in the two statutes conceals how tightly they fit.

First, it inserted sub-section (1A) into section 17 of the Registration Act:

> "The documents containing contracts to transfer for consideration, any immovable property for the purpose of section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (4 of 1882) shall be registered if they have been executed on or after the commencement of the Registration and Other Related laws (Amendment) Act, 2001 (48 of 2001) and if such documents are not registered on or after such commencement, then, they shall have no effect for the purposes of the said section 53A."

Second, it narrowed the exemption in section 17(2)(v), which now reads:

> "any document other than the documents specified in sub-section (1A) not itself creating, declaring, assigning, limiting or extinguishing any right, title or interest of the value of one hundred rupees and upwards to or in immovable property, but merely creating a right to obtain another document which will, when executed, create, declare, assign, limit or extinguish any such right, title or interest"

The words "any document other than the documents specified in sub-section (1A)" were substituted for the words "any document". Both this substitution and the insertion of sub-section (1A) were made by section 3 of the amending Act.

Third, it omitted the third limb of the proviso to section 49. Fourth, it omitted the registration clause from section 53A itself.

## The two classes of writing the amendment closed on

Section 53A speaks of a person who "contracts to transfer for consideration any immoveable property by writing signed by him". That description covers two quite different pieces of paper, and before 2001 the Registration Act treated them differently.

The first is a document that purports to transfer. An instrument of sale of tangible immovable property of a hundred rupees or more must, under section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act, be made by a registered instrument; if it is not registered, section 49 denies it effect under clause (a) and proof under clause (c). Such a document was always "required to be registered", and it was for this class that the deleted words in section 53A and the deleted limb of the proviso did their principal work: the paper failed as a transfer, and the statute then allowed the holder to use it as the signed writing that founded the bar.

The second is a document that does not purport to transfer. Section 54 of the Transfer of Property Act says of a contract for sale that "It does not, of itself, create any interest in or charge on such property." A document of that description fell squarely inside the exemption in section 17(2)(v) as it then stood — not itself creating any right, title or interest, but merely creating a right to obtain another document which would create one. Clauses (b) and (c) of section 17(1) therefore did not apply to it, it was not compulsorily registrable, and section 49, which bites only on a document "required by section 17 or by any provision of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (4 of 1882), to be registered", did not reach it at all.

The Act of 2001 closed both. For the first class, it removed the saving that had let an unregistered transfer deed be used as the writing under section 53A. For the second, it made the agreement compulsorily registrable for the first time and attached to non-registration a consequence of its own: such documents "shall have no effect for the purposes of the said section 53A". Neither class can now found the bar unless the writing is on the register.

There is a further consequence that sub-section (1A) did not have to state. An agreement falling within it is now a document required by section 17 to be registered, and section 49 operates on every document of that description. An unregistered agreement to sell therefore attracts not only the loss of effect written into sub-section (1A) but the three withholdings in section 49 as well — and with them the proviso, whose surviving first limb is the reason such a contract can still be proved at all.

## The door left open, and the clock behind it

The first limb of the proviso to section 49 was not touched. An unregistered document affecting immovable property, required to be registered and not registered, may still "be received as evidence of a contract in a suit for specific performance". A person who paid the price and took possession under a writing that was never registered has not lost his contract. He has lost the use he could make of it while sitting still.

The difference between the escape that went and the escape that stayed is the difference between a defence and a claim, and the law of limitation treats the two as nothing alike. A bar under section 53A had no period attached to it; it was available whenever the transferor came, and it was available for as long as the possession and the willingness lasted. A suit for specific performance is governed by Article 54 of the Schedule to the Limitation Act, 1963, which prescribes three years, running from

> "The date fixed for the performance, or, if no such date is fixed, when the plaintiff has notice that performance is refused."

And section 3(1) of that Act requires a court to apply the period whether or not anyone invokes it:

> "Subject to the provisions contained in sections 4 to 24 (inclusive), every suit instituted, appeal preferred, and application made after the prescribed period shall be dismissed, although limitation has not been set up as a defence."

So the practical effect of the 2001 amendment, for a writing executed after it came into force, is to convert a protection that did not expire into a remedy that does. The holder of an unregistered agreement who is in undisturbed possession has no occasion to sue; nothing is being demanded of him, and the expense and the risk of a suit are real. The three years run anyway. If the transferor comes afterwards, the document that would once have been enough to stop him is now evidence admissible only in a proceeding that has become time-barred.

It should be said plainly that this is a defensible choice and not an oversight. The register exists so that a person dealing with land can learn from a public record who has a claim on it, and a bar operating in favour of an unregistered holder is invisible to exactly that person. The 1929 settlement protected possession at the cost of the register's completeness; the 2001 settlement protects the register at the cost of the holder's inaction. What is worth stating is where the cost falls, because it falls on the party least likely to know that a clock has started.

## Which transaction is "collateral"

The second limb of the proviso remains, and the question is whether it carries any part of the weight the third limb used to carry. It does not, for a textual reason rather than a doctrinal one.

The limb permits the document to be received "as evidence of any collateral transaction not required to be effected by registered instrument". A transaction is collateral to the document if it is something other than what the document was made to do. Where a transferee relies on a signed agreement to show that he holds possession under a contract of sale, the contract of sale is the document's entire subject. The possession is not collateral to it; the possession is its performance. The limb would have to be read as covering the principal transaction in order to help, and the word "collateral" is there to exclude precisely that reading.

## The consequence that is not in Part X

There is now a second consequence of non-registration, and it does not sit with the first. Section 49 is in Part X, which the Act titles "Of the Effects of Registration and Non-registration". The consequence created in 2001 — "they shall have no effect for the purposes of the said section 53A" — is in section 17, in Part III, "Of Registrable Documents", inside the sub-section that creates the obligation.

The drafting is not hard to explain. Section 49 withholds effect and proof in general terms, and what the Act of 2001 wanted to withhold was effect for the purpose of one section of a different statute. That could not be done by adding a clause to section 49 without unsettling clauses (a) to (c), and it could not be done by amending section 53A alone, because section 53A does not know which documents the Registration Act requires to be registered. Putting the consequence next to the obligation was the economical course.

It leaves the Act stating the results of non-registration in two Parts, only one of which is headed for the purpose, and a reader who goes to Part X for the effects of non-registration will find three of them and not the fourth.

## Documents executed before the commencement

Sub-section (1A) applies to documents "executed on or after the commencement" of the amending Act, and makes the loss of effect turn on their not being registered "on or after such commencement". It therefore says nothing about a writing executed before 24 September 2001. For such a writing the exemption in section 17(2)(v) has to be read as it stood, without the carve-out, and the deleted words in section 53A and in the proviso to section 49 have to be read as in force at the relevant time.

Twenty-five years on, that is not a historical remark. Possession held under an agreement executed in the 1990s is possession held under the earlier rule, and the two regimes continue to apply side by side according to the date on the paper. The amending Act fixed its own cut-off and did nothing else about the documents on the other side of it, which is the correct way to legislate a change of this kind and also the reason the older position still has to be known.

## The Chapter the proviso still names

One feature of the proviso is older than 2001. It permits the document to be received as evidence of a contract "in a suit for specific performance under Chapter II of the Specific Relief Act, 1877 (1 of 1877)". The Act of 1877 is not in force. The statute that governs specific performance is the Specific Relief Act, 1963, which also places specific performance in a Chapter II, headed "Specific Performance of Contracts". The compilation marks the point with an editorial footnote reading "See now the Specific Relief Act, 1963 (47 of 1963)."

A footnote is not an amendment, and the proviso has never been corrected. What keeps it working is section 8(1) of the General Clauses Act, 1897:

> "Where this Act, or any Central Act or Regulation made after the commencement of this Act, repeals and re-enacts, with or without modification, any provision of a former enactment, then references in any other enactment or in any instrument to the provision so repealed shall, unless a different intention appears, be construed as references to the provision so re-enacted."

The reference in the proviso therefore reads, by operation of that rule, as a reference to the corresponding provisions of the Act of 1963. This is an ordinary piece of statutory housekeeping and it works; the reason for noting it is that the Act of 2001 opened the proviso in order to delete the closing words from it and left the dead citation in the middle of it alone. Amending legislation attends to the policy in front of it and not to the text around it, and the consequence is a proviso that names a Chapter of a repealed Act in the clause on which, since 2001, everything now turns.

## What the amendment decided

The Registration Act, 1908 withholds three things from a document that should have been registered and was not. Its proviso restores two, and before 24 September 2001 it restored three. The third restoration was the one that worked without a suit, and the Act of 2001 removed it from the proviso, removed its counterpart from section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, and at the same time brought the agreement to sell — which had been outside compulsory registration altogether — inside section 17, and stated in the same sub-section that an unregistered one has no effect for the purposes of section 53A.

The scheme that results is coherent. Registration is the condition of the bar, the bar is otherwise unavailable, and a person who wants the protection of his contract must either register the writing or sue upon it. What the scheme no longer contains is any provision for the case the 1929 words were written for: a holder in possession under a signed agreement, who has paid and is willing to perform, and who has done nothing wrong except leave the paper unregistered and the possession undisturbed. On the text as it stands, the writing is still good evidence of his contract, and the contract is still enforceable — in a suit, for three years. After that the paper proves a contract that cannot be sued upon, and section 49 continues to provide, as it has since 1908, that the document does not affect the property.
