A register of documents is useful only if it is current. Its purpose is to let a person who is about to deal with property find out, from a public record, what claims already exist on it; and that purpose fails in proportion to the gap between the moment a transaction is made and the moment it appears on the register. A document executed in January and registered in December does not merely arrive late. For eleven months it makes the register wrong — not incomplete in some general way, but wrong about the one thing the person searching it needs to know, which is the present state of the title.

So a registration statute must put a time limit on registration, and the limit is not a formality. It is the provision that makes the register worth searching. But the limit cannot be absolute either, because registration is not one act. Execution is done by the parties, at a time and place of their choosing; registration requires them, or their agents, to attend a public office during its hours, in a particular district, and to satisfy an officer there of matters that may take more than one attendance. A statute that fixed a date and allowed nothing for the distance between these two events would defeat transactions for reasons having nothing to do with their merits.

The Registration Act, 1908 deals with this in Part IV, which it heads "Of the Time of Presentation". The Part runs from section 23 to section 27. It fixes one period, provides three different ways of moving it, and prices two of them. Part VI then adds a second condition, inside the same period, with a second price. What the Act does not contain anywhere — and this is the feature that separates it from almost every other procedural time limit in Indian law — is a clause allowing anybody to excuse a delay on sufficient cause, or a power in any court to let in a document after the outer limit has passed.

## The four months, and what they run from

Section 23 states the rule:

> "Subject to the provisions contained in sections 24, 25 and 26, no document other than a will shall be accepted for registration unless presented for that purpose to the proper officer within four months from the date of its execution"

Three things in that sentence are worth separating. The period is four months. It runs from execution, not from any later event such as the payment of consideration or the delivery of possession. And the consequence is addressed to the officer rather than to the party: no document "shall be accepted for registration" unless it was presented in time. Section 23 does not say that a late document is void, or that it loses its effect, or that it may not be relied on. It says that the office will not take it. The consequences of its not being registered are then supplied by section 49, in a different Part, and they are supplied in the same terms for every unregistered document, whether it was never presented at all or presented a day late.

A proviso deals with copies of decrees and orders. Such a copy may be presented within four months from the day the decree or order was made, or, where it is appealable, within four months from the day on which it becomes final. The alternative is sensible for a reason that will matter later: an appealable decree may be altered on appeal, and a register that recorded the original would be recording something that had ceased to be true. The proviso therefore allows the clock to wait for finality, and it is the one period in the Part that is permitted to wait for a proceeding to end.

Section 27 removes wills from the scheme entirely: a will "may at any time be presented for registration or deposited". The reason is structural rather than indulgent. A will has no effect until the testator dies and can be revoked at any moment until then, so there is nothing about it that a register needs to be current on. The exception confirms what the rule is for.

## Two conditions, not one

Reading Part IV alone, it is easy to think that the four months governs a single act — getting the paper to the counter. It does not. Section 34, in Part VI, imposes a second condition and attaches it to the same period:

> "Subject to the provisions contained in this Part and in sections 41, 43, 45, 69, 75, 77, 88 and 89, no document shall be registered under this Act, unless the persons executing such document, or their representatives, assigns or agents authorised as aforesaid, appear before the registering officer within the time allowed for presentation under sections 23, 24, 25 and 26"

So the Act requires two things inside the window: that the document be presented, and that the executants appear. Section 34(2) adds that appearances "may be simultaneous or at different times", which concedes that the second condition is a sequence of events and not a single one. Section 34(3) says what the officer does once they appear — enquire whether the document was executed by those who purport to have executed it, satisfy himself as to their identity, and, where someone appears as a representative, assign or agent, satisfy himself of that person's right to appear.

The two conditions can fail independently, and they fail for different reasons. Presentation is within the control of whoever holds the document; one person can accomplish it. Appearance depends on the executants, who by the time it matters may have no interest in cooperating, and whose attendance the holder cannot compel by his own act. Part VII exists because of this, and it is worth noticing how indirect its machinery is. Section 36 does not empower the registering officer to summon anybody. Where a person presenting or claiming under a document "desires the appearance of any person whose presence or testimony is necessary for the registration of such document", the registering officer "may, in his discretion, call upon such Officer or Court as the State Government directs in this behalf to issue a summons" requiring that person to attend at a named time; and under section 37 that officer or Court, "upon receipt of the peon's fee payable in such cases", issues and serves it. So the holder cannot compel attendance, the registering officer cannot compel it either, and the request has to travel to a third authority and back. Every step of that runs inside the same four months.

Section 34(5) provides that nothing in the section applies to copies of decrees or orders. That follows from the nature of the document: a decree has no executants to appear, and the officer has nothing to enquire into. A copy of a decree is therefore subject to the presentation condition and not the appearance condition — the only class of document in the Act of which that is true.

## The three ways the period moves

Part IV provides three reliefs, and they are built on three different theories. The differences between them are more instructive than the similarity.

**Section 24 is arithmetic.** Where several persons execute a document at different times, it "may be presented for registration and re-registration within four months from the date of each execution". The section mentions no application, no officer's direction and no fine — the three things the next section mentions. It is not an indulgence at all; it is a correction of a premise. Section 23 assumes a document has one date of execution, and where it has several the period simply attaches to each: a document executed by three persons over five months has three dates from each of which four months run. What the section does not do is state which of those periods governs the document as a whole, and the mechanism it appears to contemplate is visible in the pair of words it uses together, "presented for registration and re-registration", which points to successive presentations rather than to a single deadline enlarged.

**Section 25 is excuse, priced.** It is the only provision in the Act that addresses delay as delay:

> "If, owing to urgent necessity or unavoidable accident, any document executed, or copy of a decree or order made, in India is not presented for registration till after the expiration of the time hereinbefore prescribed in that behalf, the Registrar, in cases where the delay in presentation does not exceed four months, may direct that, on payment of a fine not exceeding ten times the amount of the proper registration-fee, such document shall be accepted for registration."

Four elements are stacked. A cause of a specified kind. A delay not exceeding four months. A direction by the Registrar, which is discretionary — "may direct". And a payment, capped at ten times the registration fee. Sub-section (2) adds that an application for the direction may be lodged with a Sub-Registrar, "who shall forthwith forward it to the Registrar to whom he is subordinate".

**Section 26 is not an extension at all.** For a document purporting to have been executed by all or any of the parties outside India, the registering officer may accept it for registration if satisfied that it was so executed and

> "that it has been presented for registration within four months after its arrival in India"

The differences from section 25 are complete. There is no requirement of urgent necessity or unavoidable accident; no fine beyond the proper registration fee; no ceiling expressed as a period of delay; and the decision belongs to the registering officer rather than to the Registrar. What section 26 does is substitute a different measure. The document is still outside the period in section 23 — the section opens by describing one that "is not presented for registration till after the expiration of the time hereinbefore prescribed" — but the question the officer is directed to ask is not how long the delay has been. It is whether the document was executed abroad and whether it reached the office within four months of reaching the country. A deed executed outside India and brought here three years later is as eligible, on those two findings, as one brought here in a month; and the officer, the section says, "may" accept it, so the discretion survives even when both findings are made.

Put together, the Part treats the premise, the trigger and the excuse as three separate repairs. Section 24 fixes the premise that a document has one date. Section 26 fixes the trigger for a document that could not have been presented from where it was executed. Only section 25 confronts a party who could have presented the document in time and did not, and it is the only one that charges for the answer.

## What "urgent necessity or unavoidable accident" asks

The formula in section 25 is not the one Indian procedural law usually employs. Section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963 allows an appeal or an application — not a suit, and not an application under Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure — to be admitted after the prescribed period if the appellant or applicant "satisfies the court that he had sufficient cause" for the delay. That is a general standard, open at the top, and administered by the court that has the proceeding. Section 25 does not use it. "Urgent necessity or unavoidable accident" names two conditions, and both are narrower than sufficient cause in a particular way: each describes something that acted on the party, rather than a reason the party can give.

"Unavoidable accident" is the more familiar of the two. It asks whether something happened that the party could not have prevented, and it excludes a choice, however reasonable. "Urgent necessity" is the odder expression, because necessity ordinarily explains why a person did something, not why they failed to. Read in a provision about non-presentation, it must mean a competing demand of such urgency that attending to it displaced the presentation — which is still a fact about circumstances pressing on the party and not an assessment of whether their conduct was excusable. Neither limb invites the question whether the delay was bona fide, or short, or harmless, which is the question "sufficient cause" invites.

That narrowness matters because of what sits beside it. A standard as open as sufficient cause can afford to be unbounded in time, since the court applying it can take the length of the delay into account as part of the cause. Section 25 is bounded instead: the Registrar has no power at all once the delay exceeds four months, however good the reason. The Act makes the test harder and the ceiling absolute, and does both in one sentence.

## The price, twice

Section 34 does not merely attach appearance to the presentation window. It gives appearance its own relief, in terms deliberately parallel to section 25:

> "Provided that, if owing to urgent necessity or unavoidable accident all such persons do not so appear, the Registrar, in cases where the delay in appearing does not exceed four months, may direct that on payment of a fine not exceeding ten times the amount of the proper registration fee, in addition to the fine, if any, payable under section 25, the document may be registered."

The same cause, the same four months, the same ceiling of ten times the fee, the same officer — and the closing words, "in addition to the fine, if any, payable under section 25", which make the two charges cumulative. Section 34(4) repeats the forwarding mechanism of section 25(2) for applications under the proviso.

So a document that was presented late and whose executants also appeared late attracts both fines, and the maximum exposure is twenty times the proper registration fee. The drafting is explicit about this, which tells us the cumulation is intended rather than an accident of two parallel provisions sitting in different Parts. It follows from the structure: there are two conditions, each can be missed, and each miss is separately priced.

Two further provisions complete the arrangement. Section 69(1)(d) empowers the Inspector-General to make rules "regulating the amount of fines imposed under sections 25 and 34, respectively" — so the amount within the statutory cap is a matter of published rule rather than of the Registrar's unguided choice. And section 70 provides:

> "The Inspector-General may also, in the exercise of his discretion, remit wholly or in part the difference between any fine levied under section 25 or section 34, and the amount of the proper registration fee."

The remission power has a floor, and the floor is the registration fee. The Inspector-General may give back the whole of the penalty and no part of the price. That is a precise statement of what the fine is for: it is not compensation for the document's lateness, which could sensibly be remitted in full, but a surcharge above a fee that remains payable whatever happens. A document admitted late still pays for its registration like any other.

## Who decides, and who does not

Section 23 addresses the "proper officer". Sections 25 and 34 address the Registrar. The distinction is carried through consistently, and it decides something practical: the Sub-Registrar before whom documents are ordinarily presented has no power to admit a late one. Both section 25(2) and section 34(4) make him a conduit — an application "may be lodged" with him, and he "shall forthwith forward it" to the Registrar to whom he is subordinate. The word is "shall", so he cannot refuse to forward it, and "forthwith", so he cannot sit on it while the remaining period runs out.

What is absent from the whole arrangement is a court. Nothing in sections 23 to 26 or in section 34 gives a civil court any power over the period. Part XII gives a court a role after a refusal, and that role is examined below, but the decision whether to admit a document presented out of time is placed entirely inside the registration department — discretionary with the Registrar, rule-bound as to amount, and reviewable as to the fine by the Inspector-General.

This is a deliberate allocation and it is defensible. The question whether a document should be taken on the register eight months after execution is not a question about the rights of the parties to it; it is a question about the integrity of a public record, and the officer who keeps the record is the natural person to decide it. But the allocation has a consequence that is easy to miss, which is that there is no forum in which the merits of a late document can be urged. A party with an unanswerable explanation and a document a day outside the eight months has nobody to explain it to.

## The outer limit

Collecting the periods gives the following. For a document executed in India, presentation must be within four months of execution under section 23, and the Registrar may admit it where "the delay in presentation does not exceed four months" under section 25. Once the delay exceeds four months, the condition on which section 25 operates has failed, and the section confers no power. The outer limit for presentation is therefore eight months from execution, and the Act contains no provision — no proviso, no rule-making power, no saving — by which a document may be presented after it.

That is a strong statement and it is worth saying what it rests on. The words "four months" appear in Part IV in section 23 and its proviso, in section 23A, in section 24, in section 25 and in section 26, and nowhere else in the Act. The expression "sufficient cause" does not appear in the Act at all. Neither does any power to condone, extend or enlarge a period. The only provision that frees a document from the time of presentation altogether is section 27, and it is confined to wills.

For appearance, the position is slightly more complicated, and the Act does not resolve it expressly. Section 34(1) requires appearance "within the time allowed for presentation under sections 23, 24, 25 and 26" — which, where section 25 has been invoked, is the extended time rather than the original four months. The proviso then permits a direction where "the delay in appearing does not exceed four months". On that reading the appearance window can run four months beyond whatever presentation window applied, and the Act nowhere states a single combined figure. What it does state, and states twice, is the shape of the relief: one defined cause, one capped period, one priced direction, and nothing after that.

One provision runs on a different clock, and it is instructive that it is not an extension either. Section 23A allows re-registration where a document was accepted for registration from a person not duly empowered to present it. A person claiming under such a document may present it for re-registration within four months of first becoming aware that the registration was invalid, and on re-registration the document is "deemed to have been duly registered for all purposes from the date of its original registration". Here the period runs from knowledge, because the defect is one the claimant had no means of discovering earlier; and the document is restored to its original date, because it did reach the register in time and the fault was in how it got there. The section is a repair of a mis-presentation, not a dispensation from the time limit, and it is the only place in the Act where a period begins when somebody finds something out.

## What the refusal machinery does, and what it cannot

Part XII deals with refusal to register, and at first reading it looks like a route back for a document kept out by the clock. Section 71(1) requires a Sub-Registrar refusing registration to make an order of refusal, record his reasons in Book No. 2, endorse "registration refused" on the document, and give a copy of the reasons to any person executing or claiming under it. Section 71(2) then bars any registering officer from accepting such a document "unless and until, under the provisions hereinafter contained, the document is directed to be registered". Section 72 gives an appeal to the Registrar, within thirty days, against a Sub-Registrar's refusal on any ground other than denial of execution, and the Registrar "may reverse or alter such order". Section 76(2) provides that no appeal lies from the Registrar's own order under section 72 or section 76. Section 77 then allows a suit, within thirty days of the refusal, for a decree directing the document to be registered.

A refusal because the four months have run is a refusal on a ground other than denial of execution, so the section 72 appeal is formally available, and so, after it, is the section 77 suit. But what those provisions can produce is limited by the words in which they produce it. Section 72(2) provides that where the Registrar's order directs registration and the document is presented within thirty days of that order, the Sub-Registrar shall obey it, and the registration "shall take effect as if the document had been registered when it was first duly presented for registration". Section 75(3) says the same of a registration following the Registrar's order after an enquiry. Section 77(2) applies sub-sections (2) and (3) of section 75 to documents presented under a decree.

The operative words are "when it was first duly presented". The whole machinery restores a document to the date of its first due presentation, and it is built on the assumption that there was one — that the document reached the office in time and was turned away for a reason that turns out to be wrong. Where the refusal was itself for lateness, there is no due presentation to relate back to, and on the text the relief has nothing to fasten on. A court asked under section 77 to decree registration of a document presented in the tenth month is being asked to direct an officer to do what section 23 forbids him to do and section 25 gives nobody the power to permit.

The enquiry the Registrar is directed to make points the same way. Section 74 requires him, where execution is denied, to enquire whether the document has been executed and

> "whether the requirements of the law for the time being in force have been complied with on the part of the applicant or person presenting the document for registration, as the case may be, so as to entitle the document to registration"

Section 75(1) then states the condition on which he may act: "If the Registrar finds that the document has been executed and that the said requirements have been complied with, he shall order the document to be registered." The time for presentation is one of the requirements of the law, so the condition on which the power to order registration depends is not satisfied by a document outside it. None of this is stated in terms anywhere in the Act, and the point is a reading of how the provisions fit rather than a proposition the Act announces. But it is the reading the words support, and it is the one the relation-back language was evidently drafted on.

So the Part XII remedies are remedies against a wrong refusal, not against a late presentation. They correct the officer; they do not extend the Act. A party whose document was refused because the Sub-Registrar misread the district, or wrongly doubted an agent's authority, or demanded something the Act does not require, has an appeal, a suit, and a relation back that undoes the delay the refusal caused. A party whose document was refused because it arrived too late has the same forms and nothing in them to ask for.

## Why it cannot be repaired later

There is one further asymmetry, and it is the sharpest thing in the scheme. Section 47 provides:

> "A registered document shall operate from the time from which it would have commenced to operate if no registration thereof had been required or made, and not from the time of its registration."

Registration does not give a document its date. The document operates from when it would have operated anyway, so a deed registered in the fourth month and a deed registered in the eighth operate identically, and registration adds nothing to the transaction's timing. What registration does is make the document capable of operating at all, because section 49 denies a document required to be registered any effect on the immovable property it comprises, any power to confer an authority to adopt, and any reception as evidence of a transaction affecting the property, "unless it has been registered". The surviving proviso allows it to be received as evidence of a contract in a suit for specific performance, or as evidence of a collateral transaction not required to be effected by registered instrument — which is to say that the document can prove an agreement and cannot work a transfer.

Nothing in section 49 distinguishes between a document that was never presented and a document presented in the ninth month with an impeccable explanation. The section operates on the fact of non-registration, and after the eighth month that fact is permanent, because no provision in the Act can alter it. The consequence of missing a procedural period is therefore not a procedural consequence at all. It is the loss of the transfer, for good, and the reduction of the instrument to evidence of a promise — enforceable, if at all, in a suit for specific performance, for which Article 54 of the Schedule to the Limitation Act, 1963 prescribes three years.

That is a severe outcome for an eight-month default, and the severity is the point. The Act is not punishing carelessness; it is protecting the register from documents whose subject matter has had time to be dealt with again. Eight months is long enough for the property to have been sold to someone who searched the register and found nothing. Once that is possible, admitting the earlier document is no longer a matter between the parties to it.

## What the scheme decides

The Registration Act fixes four months, imposes two conditions inside them, and then provides for failure in three different ways: by giving each executant his own period, by substituting arrival in India for execution where the document was made abroad, and by allowing the Registrar to admit a document up to four months late on a defined cause and a capped fine, with a second such allowance, cumulatively priced, for late appearance. The amount of each fine is governed by rules; the penalty above the registration fee may be remitted by the Inspector-General; the registration fee itself may not.

What the Act withholds is a general discretion. There is no sufficient cause clause, no condonation power, no court with jurisdiction over the period, and no provision of any kind that operates after the outer limit. The refusal and appeal machinery in Part XII, which looks like a route back, turns throughout on the words "duly presented", and a document that was never duly presented has nothing for it to restore.

The design is coherent once the object is identified. A time limit that could be relaxed on a good enough reason would be a limit on careless parties only, and the person it exists to protect — the stranger who searches the register before dealing with the property — would have no way of knowing whether some document was still capable of being let in. A ceiling that cannot be moved tells him something a discretion never could, which is that after eight months from execution, nothing further is coming. The cost of saying that is borne by the party with the unanswerable excuse and the document one day too old, and the Act, having set up a Registrar to hear excuses and priced the ones it will take, declines to hear that one at all.
