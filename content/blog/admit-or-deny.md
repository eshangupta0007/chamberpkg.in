title: "Admit or Deny: The Thirty Days Section 330 Gives a Document, and the One Thing Not Disputing It Dispenses With"
excerpt: "Section 330 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 requires the parties to admit or deny the genuineness of every document in a list 'soon after supply of such documents and in no case later than thirty days after such supply', relaxable only by an order recording reasons. Having built the election, the section is careful about what the election buys: sub-section (3) operates where genuineness 'is not disputed', and dispenses with proof of the signature and nothing else. Three consequences follow from reading the sub-sections in that order. The clock runs from supply, which the Sanhita provides for in only two situations. Failing to deny within thirty days is not, in the words of sub-section (3), the same state as not disputing. And an undisputed document is still not proof of what it says — which leaves the certificate under section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 exactly where it was."

Proving a document is not one operation. It is three, and they are separable in a way that the phrase "proving a document" conceals.

The first question is whether the paper is what it purports to be — whether it is the record of the thing it says it is the record of, rather than something manufactured afterwards. The second is whether the person whose name is at the foot of it put it there. The third is whether what it says is true. These are independent. A genuine document signed by the right officer may state something false; a forgery may state something accurate; and a document whose author cannot be identified may be perfectly reliable about the matter it records. A system of proof that collapses the three into a single enquiry is not being efficient. It is answering one question and reporting the answer as though it settled the other two.

A code of criminal procedure has a strong reason to economise on the first two. Most of the documents in a criminal trial are not contentious in either respect. The post-mortem report is the post-mortem report; the seizure memo was written by the officer who signed it; the bank statement came from the bank. Calling a witness to say so consumes a day of the court's time and a day of the witness's in order to establish something nobody proposed to contest, and it does this hundreds of times in every court in the country. The saving available from dispensing with that exercise is very large, and no principle is violated by taking it, because nothing in the best-evidence tradition requires a party to prove what the other side does not dispute.

But the saving has to be paid for in some currency, and there is only one currency available. The court cannot know in advance which documents are uncontested. Only the parties know, and the only way to find out is to ask them and treat the answer as binding. So a provision of this kind necessarily converts a party's silence, or a party's answer, into a substitute for evidence. That is a legitimate device, and it is also the point at which the whole arrangement becomes delicate, because the value of what is given up depends entirely on how well the party understood, at the moment of answering, what he was answering about.

Section 330 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 is the provision that makes this exchange in a criminal trial. It is headed "No formal proof of certain documents", and it is three sub-sections and three provisos long. What is worth noticing about it is not that it makes the exchange — every modern procedural code makes some version of it — but how precisely it limits what the exchange delivers, and how much of the surrounding machinery it leaves untouched. The section is considerably narrower than its marginal heading suggests, and the practical consequences run in both directions.

## The three questions, and the one sub-section (3) answers

Start with the operative provision, because the duty in sub-section (1) is only worth performing if sub-section (3) does something with the answer.

Sub-section (3) reads: "Where the genuineness of any document is not disputed, such document may be read in evidence in any inquiry, trial or other proceeding under this Sanhita without proof of the signature of the person by whom it purports to be signed". A proviso follows: "Provided that the Court may, in its discretion, require such signature to be proved."

The condition is non-dispute of genuineness. The consequence is that the document may be read in evidence without proof of the signature. That is the entire dispensation. It is not a dispensation from relevance, from admissibility, from the rules about secondary evidence, or from anything bearing on the truth of the contents.

It is worth seeing what exactly has been removed, because the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 contains the requirement in terms. Section 65 of the Adhiniyam provides: "If a document is alleged to be signed or to have been written wholly or in part by any person, the signature or the handwriting of so much of the document as is alleged to be in that person's handwriting must be proved to be in his handwriting." That is the obligation sub-section (3) lifts. Section 66 of the Adhiniyam states the corresponding rule for electronic signatures — "Except in the case of a secure electronic signature, if the electronic signature of any subscriber is alleged to have been affixed to an electronic record, the fact that such electronic signature is the electronic signature of the subscriber must be proved" — and sub-section (3) of section 330 does not mention electronic signatures at all.

So the architecture is this. Of the three questions with which this note began, sub-section (3) answers the second, and answers it by agreement rather than by evidence. The first question — genuineness — is not answered by sub-section (3); it is the *condition* of sub-section (3) operating, which means the parties have answered it themselves. And the third question, the truth of the contents, is not touched anywhere in the section.

That last point is the one most often lost in practice, and it is lost because the shortcut feels larger than it is. A medical report whose genuineness is not disputed comes on the record without anybody proving the doctor's signature. It does not thereby become proof of the opinion it contains. Whether the opinion can be acted on is a question of the law of evidence — in the case of an expert, of section 39 of the Adhiniyam, under which the opinions of persons specially skilled in the relevant field are relevant facts — and of what weight a court can give to an opinion whose author has not been examined. Section 330 makes the paper admissible without a formal witness. It does not make the paper right.

## Where the list comes from, and when the clock starts

Sub-section (1) is the machinery. It reads: "Where any document is filed before any Court by the prosecution or the accused, the particulars of every such document shall be included in a list and the prosecution or the accused or the advocate for the prosecution or the accused, if any, shall be called upon to admit or deny the genuineness of each such document soon after supply of such documents and in no case later than thirty days after such supply".

Four features of that sentence matter.

The first is that it is symmetrical. The duty is on "the prosecution or the accused" and attaches to documents filed by either. There is no asymmetry of the kind found in some procedural provisions, where the obligation binds the party with the resources to discharge it and spares the other. Both sides list, and both sides answer.

The second is that the obligation runs to "each such document", not to the bundle. An answer that admits or denies the list as a whole does not discharge it, and a court asked later to treat a document as undisputed has to be able to point to the answer given about that document.

The third is the trigger. The thirty days run from "supply of such documents", not from the filing of the list, not from the framing of the charge, and not from any step the defence takes. The outer limit is attached to an event that occurs near the beginning of the proceedings.

The fourth is the phrase that precedes the outer limit. The parties are to be called upon to answer "soon after supply of such documents and in no case later than thirty days after such supply". Thirty days is a ceiling, not an entitlement. The primary direction is "soon after", and the thirty days operate only to fix the point beyond which "soon after" has certainly been exceeded.

## The two places the Sanhita provides for supply

Because the clock runs from supply, it is worth asking where in the Sanhita supply is provided for. The answer is that there are two provisions, and they do not cover every trial.

Section 230 governs cases instituted on a police report. The Magistrate "shall without delay, and in no case beyond fourteen days from the date of production or appearance of the accused, furnish to the accused and the victim (if represented by an advocate) free of cost, a copy of each of the following" — and the list that follows is the police report, the first information report recorded under section 173, the statements recorded under sub-section (3) of section 180 of all persons the prosecution proposes to examine, the confessions and statements recorded under section 183, and any other document or relevant extract forwarded to the Magistrate with the police report under sub-section (6) of section 193.

Section 231 governs cases instituted otherwise than on a police report where the offence is triable exclusively by the Court of Session. There the Magistrate issuing process under section 227 "shall forthwith furnish to the accused, free of cost, a copy of each of the following" — the statements recorded under section 223 or section 225 of all persons examined by the Magistrate, the statements and confessions recorded under section 180 or section 183, and any documents produced before the Magistrate on which the prosecution proposes to rely. Its second proviso repeats the deeming found in section 230: "Provided further that supply of documents in electronic form shall be considered as duly furnished."

Those are the two. A summons case instituted on complaint, and a warrant case instituted on complaint that is not exclusively triable by a Court of Session, have no corresponding provision for supply. In those trials there is no statutory event from which the thirty days of section 330(1) can be counted, because the section counts from a supply the Sanhita has not provided for. This is not a drafting accident so much as a consequence of attaching a general rule of evidence to a step that exists only in part of the Sanhita's procedure. In such a trial the court will have to fix the occasion for the election itself, and the thirty-day outer limit will attach to whatever occasion it fixes.

Three further features of section 230 bear on the same calculation.

The first concerns the manner of supply. The second proviso permits the Magistrate, where a document is voluminous, to furnish copies through electronic means or to direct that the accused "will only be allowed to inspect it either personally or through an advocate in Court". The third proviso then states: "Provided also that supply of documents in electronic form shall be considered as duly furnished." Read with section 330(1), electronic transmission starts the thirty days. Inspection in court, which the second proviso offers as an alternative to a copy, is not described as supply at all, and it is not obvious what starts the clock where that course is taken.

The second is that supply under section 230 starts more than one clock, and the other one is longer. Section 262(1) gives the accused in a warrant case so instituted sixty days to apply for discharge, counted "from the date of supply of copies of documents under section 230". So the same event sets running a thirty-day period in which the accused must say whether he disputes the genuineness of the prosecution's documents, and a sixty-day period in which he may apply to be discharged on the footing that those documents disclose no case against him. The election about the documents falls due at the halfway point of the window for contending that they come to nothing. Nothing prevents both from being done in order, and the sequence is not absurd — genuineness and sufficiency are different questions, and a party may well concede the first while attacking the second. It is simply worth seeing that the Sanhita has arranged for the concession to be made first.

In a sessions trial the two clocks are not even anchored to the same event. Section 250(1) gives the accused sixty days to apply for discharge "from the date of commitment of the case under section 232", while the thirty days of section 330(1) continue to run from supply. Commitment follows supply, so in that class of case the document election will ordinarily have closed before the discharge window opens.

The third is the position of the victim. Section 230 directs supply to the accused and to "the victim (if represented by an advocate)". Section 330(1) calls upon "the prosecution or the accused or the advocate for the prosecution or the accused" to answer. The victim receives the documents and is not among those called upon to admit or deny them. The supply provision and the election provision describe different sets of people, and a victim's advocate who answers about a document is answering about something the section does not ask him.

## The relaxation, and what it is a relaxation of

The first proviso to sub-section (1) reads: "Provided that the Court may, in its discretion, relax the time limit with reasons to be recorded in writing."

Three things follow from the way this is drafted.

It is a judicial discretion, and it is conditioned. The court may relax, but only with reasons recorded in writing. The requirement of written reasons is the only control the proviso contains, and it is a real one: a relaxation granted without recorded reasons is not a relaxation the proviso authorises.

It is a relaxation of "the time limit" and of nothing else. The duty to answer in respect of each document survives the relaxation. What the proviso moves is the date.

And it is drafted without any threshold. The proviso does not require sufficient cause, or an application, or an absence of negligence. It requires a discretion exercised for recorded reasons. A procedural code that intended the thirty days to be hard would not ordinarily have left the relaxing power in this form, and the absence of a threshold is the clearest textual indication that the outer limit was meant to be administered rather than enforced.

## Failure to deny is not, on the text, the same as non-dispute

Here is the feature of the section that repays the closest reading, and it turns on the fact that sub-sections (1) and (3) are not written in the same vocabulary.

Sub-section (1) imposes a duty to "admit or deny the genuineness" within a period. Sub-section (3) attaches its consequence to a state of affairs: "Where the genuineness of any document is not disputed". The section nowhere says that a failure to answer within thirty days amounts to non-dispute, and it nowhere provides that a document not denied within thirty days shall be deemed undisputed. There is no deeming provision, and there is no sub-section stating the consequence of default.

This matters because the two formulations can come apart in ordinary cases. A party who files no response at all within thirty days has failed to perform the duty in sub-section (1). Whether he has thereby brought about the state described in sub-section (3) — genuineness "not disputed" — is a separate question on which the section is silent. So is the converse: a party who denies genuineness in his answer has plainly disputed it, but a party who disputes genuineness for the first time at the stage of evidence, having said nothing earlier, has not complied with sub-section (1) and has nonetheless, on the face of it, disputed.

It would be wrong to present this as settling anything. Courts may well read the two sub-sections together and treat an unanswered list as an undisputed one; that is a natural reading, it gives the thirty days a function, and a provision designed to save court time would be substantially defeated if silence left everything open. The point is narrower and it is textual. The Sanhita has written a duty with a deadline in one sub-section and a consequence keyed to a different description in another, and it has not joined them. Where a statute wants silence to operate as admission it is usually capable of saying so, and the Adhiniyam itself shows what that drafting looks like. The proviso to section 67 of the Adhiniyam dispenses with the attesting witness for a registered document "unless its execution by the person by whom it purports to have been executed is specifically denied" — the word "specifically" doing precisely the work that section 330 leaves undone, by fixing the quality of denial required and so fixing, by implication, what counts as a failure to deny.

## The form the State Government may provide

Sub-section (2) reads: "The list of documents shall be in such form as the State Government may, by rules, provide."

The structure here is familiar from other provisions of the new codes. A duty is imposed in one sub-section and the instrument through which it is to be performed is left to subordinate legislation in the next, with no provision stating what happens in the interval. Three observations are safe.

The duty in sub-section (1) is not expressed to depend on the rules. It applies where a document "is filed before any Court", and the obligation to include particulars in a list and to answer about each of them is complete without the form.

Sub-section (2) is permissive as to the making of the rules — "may, by rules, provide" — and mandatory as to the form once they exist. Until rules are made in a given State, the sub-section furnishes no form, and the list is whatever the court directs or the parties produce.

Whether a particular State has notified rules under sub-section (2) is a question of fact in that State, and one that a practitioner should verify locally rather than assume. What can be said from the text alone is that the Sanhita does not make the election wait for the form, and that a list in any intelligible form satisfies sub-section (1) where no form has been prescribed.

## Electronic records, where the Adhiniyam does not bend

The interaction between section 330 and the proof of electronic records is the point at which the narrowness of sub-section (3) has the largest practical effect, and it is worth setting out carefully because the two provisions sit in different enactments.

Section 63 of the Adhiniyam governs the admissibility of electronic records. Sub-section (1) opens with an override — "Notwithstanding anything contained in this Adhiniyam" — and provides that information contained in an electronic record which is printed on paper, stored, recorded or copied in optical or magnetic media or semiconductor memory, or otherwise stored, recorded or copied in any electronic form, shall be deemed to be also a document and shall be admissible "without further proof or production of the original", if the conditions mentioned in the section are satisfied. Sub-section (2) sets out those conditions. Sub-section (4) then provides: "In any proceeding where it is desired to give a statement in evidence by virtue of this section, a certificate doing any of the following things shall be submitted along with the electronic record at each instance where it is being submitted for admission".

Two features of that machinery are relevant here.

The override in section 63(1) is an override of the Adhiniyam, not of the Sanhita. It settles the relationship between section 63 and the rest of the law of evidence; it says nothing about section 330, which is in a different statute. Conversely, section 330(3) dispenses with proof of the signature of the person by whom the document "purports to be signed", and that is not the thing section 63(4) requires. The certificate under section 63(4) is not proof of a signature; it is the instrument by which the statutory conditions in section 63(2) are established, and it is required "at each instance" the record is submitted for admission.

The consequence is that non-dispute of genuineness under section 330 does not supply the certificate. A party who does not deny the genuineness of a printout has not thereby conceded that the conditions in section 63(2) were satisfied, and has not provided the court with the document that section 63(4) requires to be submitted along with the electronic record. These are different objections, resting on different provisions in different enactments, and the loss of one does not carry the other with it.

The same reasoning applies to section 66 of the Adhiniyam. Section 330(3) removes the requirement of proving a signature. It does not mention the proof of an electronic signature, which section 66 requires in terms except in the case of a secure electronic signature.

## The expert the second proviso keeps out, and the three regimes beside it

The second proviso to sub-section (1) reads: "Provided further that no expert shall be called to appear before the Court unless the report of such expert is disputed by any of the parties to the trial."

This is a prohibition, not a permission, and it is addressed to the court. Its effect is that the attendance of an expert is conditioned on a prior election by one of the parties: the court may not call him unless somebody has disputed his report. What is worth setting out is how unusual the proviso looks when placed beside the three neighbouring provisions that deal with the same problem, all of them within six sections of it.

Section 329 deals with reports of Government scientific experts. Sub-section (1) makes such a report usable "as evidence in any inquiry, trial or other proceeding under this Sanhita". Sub-section (2) provides: "The Court may, if it thinks fit, summon and examine any such expert as to the subject-matter of his report." That is a pure discretion in the court, exercisable without any election by a party. Sub-section (3) then allows an expert who is summoned and unable to attend personally, unless expressly directed to appear personally, to depute a responsible officer conversant with the facts to depose on his behalf.

Section 332 deals with evidence of a formal character. Sub-section (1) allows it to be given by affidavit, "subject to all just exceptions". Sub-section (2) provides: "The Court may, if it thinks fit, and shall, on the application of the prosecution or the accused, summon and examine any such person as to the facts contained in his affidavit." Here the party has an entitlement. An application by either side obliges the court to summon the deponent, and no prior dispute is required.

Section 336 deals with documents or reports prepared by a public servant, scientific expert or medical officer where the author is transferred, retired or dead, cannot be found or is incapable of deposing, or where securing his presence is likely to cause delay. In those cases the court "shall secure presence of successor officer of such public servant, expert, or officer who is holding that post at the time of such deposition to give deposition on such document or report". A proviso then repeats the condition found in section 330: "Provided that no public servant, scientific expert or medical officer shall be called to appear before the Court unless the report of such public servant, scientific expert or medical officer is disputed by any of the parties of the trial or other proceedings". A second proviso permits the successor's deposition through audio-video electronic means.

So within one chapter the Sanhita operates three different regimes for the attendance of the author of a document. For a deponent of formal evidence under section 332, either party may compel attendance on application. For a Government scientific expert under section 329, attendance is at the court's discretion. For an expert under the second proviso to section 330, and for the classes named in section 336, attendance is prohibited unless the report has been disputed. The three cannot be reconciled by reference to the importance of the evidence, because the class whose attendance is hardest to obtain — the expert whose report goes to the central fact — is the class whose attendance is conditioned on an election made early, on the papers.

The two prohibitions are also not identically worded, and the difference is visible on the face of the text. Section 330's proviso speaks of a report "disputed by any of the parties to the trial". Section 336's speaks of one "disputed by any of the parties of the trial or other proceedings". On the words used, section 336's condition extends to proceedings that are not trials, and section 330's second proviso does not — although section 330(3), the sub-section that actually dispenses with proof, applies to "any inquiry, trial or other proceeding under this Sanhita".

## A word the section turns on and does not define

The whole of this machinery turns on whether a document or a report has been "disputed". The Sanhita uses the word in that sense in exactly two places — the second proviso to section 330(1) and the proviso to section 336 — together with the cognate expression in section 330(3), and it does not define it. Section 2 contains no definition of dispute or of disputing, and the other appearances of the word in the Sanhita are in a different sense altogether, in the provisions of Chapter XI concerning the subject of dispute as to immovable property.

Nothing in the section therefore states what a dispute must consist of. It does not say whether the dispute must be of genuineness alone, or may extend to the contents; whether it must be in writing; whether it must disclose a ground; or whether a dispute may be entered conditionally, pending inspection of material not yet supplied. Nor does it say whether a dispute once entered may be withdrawn, or whether a party who has admitted genuineness may resile on discovering something that was not apparent from the copy supplied to him.

These are not fanciful questions. They are the questions that arise on the first date on which a list is taken up, and the section answers none of them. In their absence the matter falls to be worked out case by case, which is a tolerable position for a provision of marginal importance and a less comfortable one for a provision that controls whether the author of the central document in a criminal trial will be examined at all.

## Three things that follow at the Bar

The first concerns the diary, and it has to be done before the file has been read with any care. The thirty days of section 330(1) run from supply, and in a police-report case supply is itself due within fourteen days of production or appearance under section 230. The outer limit therefore falls early, often before the charge, and on the text it falls whether or not the brief has been studied. The practical answer is to treat the date of supply as a limitation date and to record it when the copies are received — including the date of electronic transmission, which the third proviso to section 230 deems to be due furnishing. Where the copies came by way of inspection in court rather than by supply, that too should be recorded, because the occasion from which the thirty days are to be counted will have to be fixed by the court.

The second concerns the content of the answer, and it is a drafting point. Because sub-section (3) dispenses with proof of the signature and nothing further, an answer that admits genuineness should say so in terms that leave the rest intact: that the genuineness of the document is not disputed, that no admission is made as to the truth of its contents, and, where the document is an electronic record, that the certificate required by section 63(4) of the Adhiniyam is not dispensed with and the conditions in section 63(2) are not admitted. None of those reservations is a favour the court grants; they are the consequence of what section 330 does and does not say, and the only reason to put them on the record is that a bare admission of genuineness is often read afterwards as more than it was.

The third concerns the one election that cannot be retrieved. For most documents, the consequence of not disputing genuineness is modest — the signature need not be proved, and the proviso to sub-section (3) leaves the court a discretion to require even that. For an expert's report, and for a report falling within section 336, the consequence is different in kind: the second proviso to section 330(1) prohibits the expert from being called at all unless the report is disputed. Where there is any prospect of wanting to cross-examine the author of a report, the dispute has to be entered within the thirty days, and it has to be entered before the material that would justify it — the recordings, the chain of custody, the underlying data — has necessarily come to hand. That is an uncomfortable position to be placed in, and it follows from the way the second proviso is framed rather than from anything the defence has done. The only protection available is to recognise which documents in the list are of that character on the day the list arrives, and to answer accordingly.
