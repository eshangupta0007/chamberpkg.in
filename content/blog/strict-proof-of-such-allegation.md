title: "Strict Proof of Such Allegation: The Two Decisions Order XLVII Asks a Court to Make, and the Only One It Makes Appealable"
excerpt: "Section 114 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 creates the power of review and states no ground for it; the grounds sit in Order XLVII Rule 1, in the First Schedule, which a High Court may under section 122 annul, alter or add to. Within that rule the Code treats the two possible outcomes of a review application unequally and consistently. To reject one, Rule 4(1) requires only the Court's opinion that there is not sufficient ground, and Rule 7(1) makes that order unappealable. To grant one, Rule 4(2) requires previous notice to the opposite party and, on the ground of new evidence, strict proof of the allegation, and Rule 7(1) exposes the grant to objection at once. The asymmetry is defensible, and it falls hardest on the one class of applicant section 114 was most obviously written for."

A decree ends a suit. The court that passed it has, at that moment, no further occasion to think about it: the record is complete, the findings are recorded, and whatever is wrong with them is ordinarily wrong for somebody else to correct. Review is the departure from that. It asks the court which passed the decree to take the matter up again, and it asks this of the same court rather than a higher one, which means the question it raises is not whether the decision was right but whether the court should be allowed to say it was not.

Everything particular about review follows from that. An appeal is a transfer of the question to another forum, and the appellate court owes nothing to the view taken below. A review is not a transfer at all. The decree stays where it is, in the court that made it, and the only thing that can justify disturbing it is something that was not before the court when it decided — or something so plain on the record that the court can be shown to have overlooked it without anybody reopening the merits. Any wider ground and review becomes an appeal to oneself, which is both useless and endless.

The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 keeps review inside those limits by a method that is easy to miss on a first reading, because it distributes the subject across two places and makes the second one do all the work. And within that second place it does something more interesting still. A review application admits of exactly two outcomes — the court rejects it, or the court grants it — and the Code does not treat those two outcomes as mirror images. It makes one of them cheap to arrive at and impossible to appeal, and the other procedurally burdened and open to challenge at once.

## Where the grounds actually live

Part VIII of the Code is headed "REFERENCE, REVIEW AND REVISION", and the three sections under it are drafted in a chain. Section 113 opens:

> "113. Reference to High Court.—Subject to such conditions and limitations as may be prescribed, any Court may state a case and refer the same for the opinion of the High Court, and the High Court may make such order thereon as it thinks fit:"

Section 114 then opens by referring back to that qualification rather than restating it:

> "114. Review.—Subject as aforesaid, any person considering himself aggrieved—
>
> (a) by a decree or order from which an appeal is allowed by this Code, but from which no appeal has been preferred,
>
> (b) by a decree or order from which no appeal is allowed by this Code, or
>
> (c) by a decision on a reference from a Court of Small Causes,
>
> may apply for a review of judgment to the Court which passed the decree or made the order, and the Court may make such order thereon as it thinks fit."

Read by itself, that section confers a remarkably open power. It identifies who may apply, by three descriptions of the decree or order complained of. It identifies the forum, which is the court that passed the decree. And it states the consequence in the widest available formula — "the Court may make such order thereon as it thinks fit", the same words section 113 uses for a reference. What it does not contain is a single ground. Nothing in section 114 says what an applicant must show.

The words that supply the omission are the three at the beginning. "Subject as aforesaid" carries forward section 113's "Subject to such conditions and limitations as may be prescribed", and "prescribed" is a defined term. Section 2(16) provides:

> "(16) “prescribed” means prescribed by rules;"

So the conditions on which a review may be had are to be found in the rules, which is to say in the First Schedule, and in Order XLVII. That placement is not a matter of drafting convenience. Section 122 empowers the High Courts, other than the Court of a Judicial Commissioner, to make rules after previous publication regulating their own procedure and that of the civil courts under their superintendence, and to do so in terms that extend to the Schedule itself — the section allows them by such rules to "annul, alter or add to all or any of the rules in the First Schedule". (The clause as printed in the official compilation carries a misprint in the words describing the subordinate courts, which is why it is summarised here rather than set out.)

The entire substantive content of review therefore sits in a part of the Code that a High Court may alter, while the bare power sits in a part it may not. Whoever reads section 114 and stops has read the half of the subject that says least.

## The three grounds, and the one the Code took back

Order XLVII Rule 1(1) repeats section 114's three descriptions of the aggrieved applicant and then adds what section 114 left out:

> "1. Application for review of judgment.—(1) Any person considering himself aggrieved—
>
> (a) by a decree or order from which an appeal is allowed, but from which no appeal has been preferred,
>
> (b) by a decree or order from which no appeal is allowed, or
>
> (c) by a decision on a reference from a Court of Small Causes,
>
> and who, from the discovery of new and important matter or evidence which, after the exercise of due diligence was not within his knowledge or could not be produced by him at the time when the decree was passed or order made, or on account of some mistake or error apparent on the face of the record or for any other sufficient reason, desires to obtain a review of the decree passed or order made against him, may apply for a review of judgment to the Court which passed the decree or made the order."

Three grounds, then, and they are of unequal kinds. The first is about material: new and important matter or evidence, qualified twice over by the requirement that due diligence would not have produced it and that it was unavailable at the time the decree was passed. The second is about the record: a mistake or error apparent on the face of it. The third is a residuary formula — "any other sufficient reason" — which on its face could mean anything the court thought sufficient.

The way the Code has since dealt with that third expression is instructive, because it shows the drafting method rather than any theory. When Parliament wished to close one use of it, by the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1976, it did not narrow the words. It left them where they were and excised a single candidate:

> "Explanation.—The fact that the decision on a question of law on which the judgment of the Court is based has been reversed or modified by the subsequent decision of a superior Court in any other case, shall not be a ground for the review of such judgment."

That is a precise exclusion and it tells us what the Code thinks review is not for. A judgment resting on a proposition of law that a superior court afterwards disapproves in some other litigation is, in the ordinary sense, wrong. The Explanation says that this is not enough. The decree stands on the law as it was understood when the court decided; a later correction of that understanding, arrived at in a case to which the applicant was not a party, does not travel backwards into his decree. Review is addressed to what was wrong with the decision at the time it was made, on the material then available, and not to everything that has since made it look wrong.

## The judge the rule insists upon

The same conception explains a rule that otherwise looks like a mere allocation of court business:

> "5. Application for review in Court consisting of two or more Judges.—Where the Judge or Judges, or any one of the Judges, who passed the decree or made the order a review of which is applied for, continues or continued attached to the Court at the time when the application for a review is presented, and is not or are not precluded by absence or other cause for a period of six months next after the application from considering the decree or order to which the application refers, such Judge or Judges or any of them shall hear the application, and no other Judge or Judges of the Court shall hear the same."

The direction is in two halves and the second is the stronger. The judge who passed the decree "shall hear the application"; and no other judge of that court "shall hear the same". Only two things displace it — that the judge is no longer attached to the court, or that absence or other cause precludes consideration for a period of six months after the application. Short of those, the identity of the mind that decided and the mind that reviews is treated by the Code as part of what makes the proceeding a review at all.

That coheres with the Explanation. If review were about correctness, the last person to hear it would be the author of the decree. Because review is about whether something was missed or was unavailable, the author is the right person: he alone knows what was before him, and he is the one who can say that a matter now produced was not. It also disposes of a familiar misuse. An application presented in the hope that a differently constituted bench will take a different view of the same material is not asking for a review; Rule 5 refuses it the different bench, and the Explanation refuses it the changed view of the law.

## Rule 4, where the two outcomes part company

Everything so far is about the threshold. Rule 4 is about what the court does with the application once it has it, and it is here that the Code stops being even-handed:

> "4. Application where rejected.—(1) Where it appears to the Court that there is not sufficient ground for a review, it shall reject the application.
>
> (2) Application where granted.—Where the Court is of opinion that the application for review should be granted, it shall grant the same:
>
> Provided that—
>
> (a) no such application shall be granted without previous notice to the opposite party, to enable him to appear and be heard in support of the decree or order, a review of which is applied for; and
>
> (b) no such application shall be granted on the ground of discovery of new matter or evidence which the applicant alleges was not within his knowledge, or could not be adduced by him when the decree or order was passed or made, without strict proof of such allegation."

Set the two sub-rules side by side. For a rejection the Code requires one thing: that it appear to the court that there is not sufficient ground. There is no notice requirement, because there is nobody who needs to be heard in order to leave a decree standing. There is no proof requirement, because nothing is being found. The operative words are "it shall reject", so once the court reaches that opinion the order follows, and the opinion is reached on the application as presented.

For a grant the Code requires two further things, and both are directed at the party who already has the decree. The first is previous notice, and the proviso states its purpose — to enable the opposite party "to appear and be heard in support of the decree or order", which is one of the few occasions in the Code on which a party is given a hearing for the purpose of defending something he has already won. The second applies to the first ground only, and it is the strongest expression in the Order: an application on the new-evidence ground shall not be granted "without strict proof of such allegation".

That phrase decides how such an application has to be made, and it is routinely ignored. The allegation to be strictly proved is not that the evidence is important, nor that it would change the result. It is the negative one — that the matter was not within the applicant's knowledge, or could not be adduced by him, when the decree was passed. A negative of that kind is proved by an account of what was done: what search was made, when, where, why it failed, and what intervening event or disclosure produced the material now relied on. An application that asserts the conclusion and annexes the document has not addressed the proviso at all, and the court is forbidden to grant it. Rule 3 makes the point about form:

> "3. Form of applications for review.—The provisions as to the form of preferring appeals shall apply, mutatis mutandis, to applications for review."

So a review application is drawn as a memorandum of appeal is drawn, with its grounds stated, and the grounds then have to be carried by evidence to the standard Rule 4(2)(b) sets. The pairing is exacting: the form of an appeal, the proof of a trial.

## The asymmetry, repeated where it matters most

If Rule 4 made rejection easier and granting harder, one might expect the Code to compensate on the other side — to let the disappointed applicant take his refusal upwards while leaving the successful one alone. It does the opposite:

> "7. Order of rejection not appealable. Objections to order granting application.—(1) An order of the Court rejecting the application shall not be appealable; but an order granting an application may be objected to at once by an appeal from the order granting the application or in an appeal from the decree or order finally passed or made in the suit."

The two asymmetries therefore run the same way. The order that is easier to make is the one that cannot be appealed; the order that is harder to make can be challenged twice over, at once or at the end. The appeal Rule 7(1) contemplates is not left to inference either. Order XLIII Rule 1, which lists the orders from which an appeal lies, includes at clause (w):

> "(w) an order under rule 4 of Order XLVII granting an application for review."

So the grant has a place in the Code's own catalogue of appealable orders, and the rejection has none. Rule 6 points in the same direction for a divided bench:

> "6. Application where rejected.—(1) Where the application for a review is heard by more than one Judge and the Court is equally divided, the application shall be rejected."

And Rule 9 closes the subject against repetition:

> "9. Bar of certain application.—No application to review an order made on an application for a review or a decree or order passed or made on a review shall be entertained."

A single consistent preference is at work across all four rules, and it is not a preference for the party who holds the decree. It is a preference for the decree. Every provision that distributes burden in this Order does so in favour of the proposition that a concluded adjudication remains concluded: an absence of sufficient ground ends the application, an equal division ends it, a grant requires notice and strict proof and is exposed to objection, and nothing that happens on a review may itself be reviewed. One pass, and the presumption throughout is against the pass succeeding.

The Code's one indulgence is also revealing, because of the narrow thing it addresses:

> "(2) Where the application has been rejected in consequence of the failure of the applicant to appear, he may apply for an order to have the rejected application restored to the file, and, where it is proved to the satisfaction of the Court that he was prevented by any sufficient cause from appearing when such application was called on for hearing, the Court shall order it to be restored to the file upon such terms as to costs or otherwise as it thinks fit, and shall appoint a day for hearing the same."

There is a remedy against a rejection for non-appearance, and it is a good one — on sufficient cause proved, the court "shall order it to be restored". There is none against a rejection on the merits. The Code is willing to reopen a review that was never heard, and unwilling to revisit one that was.

## The class for whom a refusal is the end

Section 114 describes three classes of applicant, and the asymmetry does not fall on them equally.

An applicant under clause (a) has a decree from which an appeal is allowed but has not preferred one. If his review is rejected he has lost nothing that the Code gave him, because the appeal was always there; the rejection is unappealable under Rule 7(1), but the decree itself is not.

An applicant under clause (b) is in a different position entirely. His is "a decree or order from which no appeal is allowed by this Code". For him the arithmetic of the Order is complete and closed. No appeal lies from the decree, by the terms of the clause under which he applies. No appeal lies from the rejection of his review, by Rule 7(1). And no second application may be entertained, by Rule 9. The only thing the Code leaves him is revision, and section 115 is not a substitute:

> "115. Revision.—(1) The High Court may call for the record of any case which has been decided by any Court subordinate to such High Court and in which no appeal lies thereto, and if such subordinate Court appears—
>
> (a) to have exercised a jurisdiction not vested in it by law, or
>
> (b) to have failed to exercise a jurisdiction so vested, or
>
> (c) to have acted in the exercise of its jurisdiction illegally or with material irregularity,
>
> the High Court may make such order in the case as it thinks fit:"

Those three grounds are about jurisdiction and the manner of its exercise. They are not the grounds of Order XLVII Rule 1, and a litigant whose complaint is that important material was unavailable to the court will not often be able to recast it as one of them. The proviso narrows the section further:

> "Provided that the High Court shall not, under this section, vary or reverse any order made, or any order deciding an issue, in the course of a suit or other proceeding, except where the order, if it had been made in favour of the party applying for revision would have finally disposed of the suit or other proceedings."

Two further features of section 115 complete the position. Sub-section (2) provides:

> "(2) The High Court shall not, under this section, vary or reverse any decree or order against which an appeal lies either to the High Court or to any Court subordinate thereto."

That is the provision which leaves revision open to the clause (b) applicant at all: it is available to him precisely because no appeal lies from his decree, and it would be closed to him if one did. But the opening words of sub-section (1) impose a limit of their own. The power is over "any case which has been decided by any Court subordinate to such High Court". Where the decree or order falling under clause (b) is the High Court's own, there is no court of revision above it under this section, and the arithmetic of Order XLVII is then complete without any residue at all.

So the structural position is this. Review exists chiefly for the decree that cannot be appealed — that is the case in which nothing else will do, and clause (b) of section 114 says so expressly. It is in exactly that case that the Code has made refusal both cheap to obtain and immune from appeal. The provision is at its least forgiving where it is most needed, and nothing in the Order acknowledges the point.

## The clock, which the two statutes do not synchronise

A separate difficulty is created by the Limitation Act, 1963, and it is entirely a matter of arithmetic. The Schedule to that Act is set out in three columns — the description of the proceeding, the period of limitation, and the time from which the period begins to run — so its entries are read across rather than quoted as sentences. Article 124, in the Third Division, describes the proceeding as one "For a review of judgment by a court other than the Supreme Court"; the period is thirty days; and the period runs from "The date of the decree or order".

Thirty days, then, running from the date of the decree and not from any later event. The appeal periods are in the Second Division, immediately before. Article 116 deals with appeals "Under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (5 of 1908)" and divides them by forum: clause (a), "to a High Court from any decree or order", carries ninety days, and clause (b), "to any other court from any decree or order", carries thirty. Both run from the date of the decree or order. Article 117, for an appeal "From a decree or order of any High Court to the same Court", carries thirty days from the same starting point.

Put Article 124 against Article 116 and the consequence for a clause (a) applicant becomes plain, and it varies with the forum of appeal. Where the appeal lies to a court other than a High Court, the review period and the appeal period are the same thirty days and they run from the same date. A litigant who spends those days on a review has spent his appeal period on it. If the review is then rejected, the rejection is unappealable under Rule 7(1) and the appeal he did not prefer is out of time. He must apply for condonation of the delay, and his explanation for it is that he was pursuing a remedy the Code gave him — which may or may not be accepted, and is in any case a discretion he need never have invited.

Where the appeal lies to a High Court the position is the reverse. Ninety days against thirty leaves room: a review may be presented, heard and rejected, and the appeal still preferred within time. The choice between the two remedies is therefore safe in one forum and dangerous in another, for reasons that have nothing to do with the merits of either. Neither the Code nor the Act draws attention to it; the point exists only in the gap between Article 116(a) and Article 116(b), and the Code's own silence about the interaction of the two remedies leaves it where it is.

One more article matches Rule 7(2), and the correspondence is exact. Article 122 covers an application "To restore a suit or appeal or application for review or revision dismissed for default of appearance or for want of prosecution or for failure to pay costs of service of process or to furnish security for costs"; the period is thirty days; and it runs, not from the decree, but from "The date of dismissal".

So the restoration remedy that Rule 7(2) creates has its own period, running from the dismissal rather than the decree. That is the one point at which the review clock restarts.

## The courts in which most of this Order does not apply

One qualification has to be entered, and it is substantial. Order L Rule 1 provides that the provisions it specifies shall not extend to courts constituted under the Provincial Small Cause Courts Act, 1887, or to courts exercising the jurisdiction of a Court of Small Causes. Among the provisions it specifies is this:

> "Order XLVII, rules 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 (review);"

Rule 2 was repealed in 1956, so the operative exclusions are rules 3, 5, 6 and 7. What survives in a court of small causes is therefore Rule 1, which states the grounds, Rule 4, which distributes the burden between rejection and grant, and Rules 8 and 9. What does not survive is the form requirement borrowed from appeals, the direction that the same judge hear the application, the rule about an equally divided bench, and — most significantly — Rule 7, which contains both the bar on appealing a rejection and the restoration remedy for a dismissal in default.

That is a different arrangement of the same subject, and the difference is not cosmetic. In those courts the asymmetry described above is produced by Rule 4 alone, without Rule 7 to complete it on the appellate side. It is also worth keeping clause (c) of section 114 distinct from all of this. That clause contemplates a review of "a decision on a reference from a Court of Small Causes" — a decision given on a reference made by such a court, not a decree passed by it in a suit of its own — so the court whose decision is sought to be reviewed under clause (c) is not the court that Order L Rule 1 takes most of this Order away from.

## What a grant actually produces, and what Rule 1(2) prevents

Two further rules repay attention because they tend to be assumed rather than read. The first concerns what follows a successful application:

> "8. Registry of application granted, and order for re-hearings.—When an application for review is granted, a note thereof shall be made in the register and the Court may at once re-hear the case or make such order in regard to the re-hearing as it thinks fit."

A grant is therefore not, in itself, the setting aside of the decree. It is an order that the case be re-heard, and the Code leaves the court to decide whether to re-hear it at once or to give directions. The applicant who has succeeded under Rule 4(2) has won the right to be heard again on a decree that still stands, and the opposite party's remedy under Rule 7(1) is against that order, immediately or at the end.

The second is a jurisdictional filter at the threshold that applications regularly overlook:

> "(2) A party who is not appealing from a decree or order may apply for a review of judgment notwithstanding the pendency of an appeal by some other party except where the ground of such appeal is common to the applicant and the appellant, or when, being respondent, he can present to the Appellate Court the case on which he applied for the review."

The general rule is permissive: another party's appeal does not by itself bar a review by a party who is not appealing. But the two exceptions remove most of the cases in which the question arises. If the ground of the pending appeal is common to the applicant and the appellant, the review is not available, because the appellate court is already seised of the very point. And if the applicant is a respondent in that appeal who can present his case to the appellate court, it is not available either, because he has a forum. Both exceptions apply the same principle: review is for the litigant who has no other route to the point, and the existence of a route he can use defeats the application without anybody reaching its merits.

## Three things that follow at the Bar

The first is about drafting. An application on the new-evidence ground has to be built around the negative allegation, because Rule 4(2)(b) forbids a grant without strict proof of it. The document must therefore do two distinct things — state grounds in the form Rule 3 borrows from appeals, and lay an evidentiary foundation for the assertion that the material was not within knowledge or could not be adduced. The second half is the one that is usually missing, and its absence is not a defect of form. It is a bar on the order sought.

The second is about sequence, and it is settled before anything is drafted. Where the appeal from the decree lies to a court other than a High Court, the thirty days of Article 116(b) and the thirty days of Article 124 are the same thirty days. A review taken first consumes the appeal period, and its rejection cannot be appealed. Where the appeal lies to a High Court, the ninety days of Article 116(a) accommodate a review attempt with time to spare. The decision which remedy to take first is therefore a function of the appellate forum, and it has to be made at once, on the date of the decree, before the file has been read with any care.

The third is about what review is for, and it bears on advice as much as on drafting. Order XLVII is constructed around a single preference, visible in Rule 4, in Rule 6(1), in Rule 7(1) and in Rule 9: the concluded decree stays. The Explanation to Rule 1 and the direction in Rule 5 mark the boundary from the other side — not a changed view of the law, not a differently constituted bench, but something that was missing or unavailable when the court decided. An application that cannot be stated in those terms is unlikely to be granted however strong it looks on the merits, and for a decree falling under clause (b) of section 114 the attempt is the litigant's last. That is a reason to be careful about which complaints are brought to this Order, and not a reason to bring more of them.
