Article 311 is the express provision, and what it provides is a procedure rather than a ground: an inquiry, notice of the charges, a hearing on them. The second proviso then withdraws the procedure in three cases, and the three are not guarded alike. The ground decided by the dismissing authority itself requires a reason in writing and is declared final by clause (3); the ground decided by the President or the Governor requires no recorded reason and is declared final by nothing. A state that employs people to administer it must be able to dismiss them. The power is not in doubt and does not need defending; an administration that could not remove an officer who was corrupt, incapable or disloyal would not be an administration. What needs defending is the opposite proposition — that the power should be hard to exercise — and the case for it has nothing to do with the officer's interests. A civil service that can be emptied at the convenience of whoever currently governs is a civil service that will act in the interests of whoever currently governs. The protection of tenure is a protection of the permanence of the administration, and the person it benefits is a byproduct of the arrangement rather than its object.

There are two ways to make the power hard to exercise, and they are not variants of one another. A constitution can restrict the grounds, so that an officer may be removed only for stated causes and not otherwise. Or it can restrict the procedure, so that an officer may be removed for any cause at all, but only after certain steps have been taken. The first limits what the government may decide. The second limits only how it must go about deciding.

The Constitution of India takes the second route and does not take the first. Article 311 prescribes a procedure and names no ground, which means that nothing in it prevents a dismissal for a reason the Constitution would not have accepted had it been asked to state one. The whole of the protection is therefore carried by the steps, and the interest of the article lies in the three cases where the steps are dispensed with — and in who decides that they are.

## What Article 310 provides, and why Article 311 has to be in the Constitution

Article 310(1) states the baseline: "Except as expressly provided by this Constitution, every person who is a member of a defence service or of a civil service of the Union or of an all-India service or holds any post connected with defence or any civil post under the Union holds office during the pleasure of the President", with the corresponding provision for a State and the pleasure of the Governor.

The doctrine of tenure at pleasure is English, and in England it is subject to legislation. Parliament there can qualify it by ordinary statute, and the courts cannot refuse to give that statute effect. In *Union of India v. Tulsiram Patel*, (1985) 3 SCC 398, decided on 11 July 1985 by a Constitution Bench of five Judges, Madon J. set out the difference in two sentences. In India the pleasure doctrine "has received constitutional sanction by being enacted in Article 310(1)". And "Unlike in the United Kingdom in India it is not subject to any law made by Parliament" but is subject "only to what is expressly provided by the Constitution".

That distinction decides the architecture of the whole subject. Article 309 permits Acts of the appropriate Legislature to regulate "the recruitment, and conditions of service of persons appointed, to public services and posts", and permits rules to be made in the interim by the President or the Governor. But Article 309 opens with the words "Subject to the provisions of this Constitution", and the Court drew the consequence: "Article 309 is, however, not such an exception. It does not lay down any express provision which would derogate from the amplitude of the exercise of pleasure under Article 310(1)."

So a statute or a service rule may prescribe how a disciplinary proceeding is to be conducted, and may do so in far greater detail than the Constitution does. What it cannot do is create a security of tenure that the Constitution has not created. Any protection a civil servant has against the pleasure in Article 310(1) must be found in the Constitution itself, and in the case of dismissal, removal and reduction in rank it is found in one article. The Court recorded as much: clauses (1) and (2) of Article 311 "impose restrictions upon the exercise by the President or the Governor of a State of his pleasure under Article 310(1)", and "Article 311 is thus an exception to Article 310", described in *Parshottam Lal Dhingra v. Union of India*, [1958] SCR 820, as operating as a proviso to Article 310(1) though set out in a separate article.

An article of that kind carries an unusual weight. It is not one safeguard among several, with statute and rules supplying the rest. It is the entire stock, and whatever it does not contain does not exist elsewhere.

## Two protections, and what the first one leaves out

Article 311 is headed "Dismissal, removal or reduction in rank of persons employed in civil capacities under the Union or a State", and it contains two protections which do not cover the same ground.

Clause (1) is about the identity of the authority: "No person who is a member of a civil service of the Union or an all-India service or a civil service of a State or holds a civil post under the Union or a State shall be dismissed or removed by an authority subordinate to that by which he was appointed."

The reference point is the appointing authority, not the authority on whom the power of dismissal has been conferred by rules. The protection is therefore structural and automatic: it does not ask whether the decision was right, or whether the officer was heard, but only whether the person who signed the order stood at or above the level of the person who had appointed him.

One phrase is absent from clause (1), and its absence is the first thing to notice about the article. The heading names three actions — dismissal, removal, reduction in rank. Clause (1) names two. A reduction in rank may therefore be ordered by an authority subordinate to the appointing authority without infringing clause (1) at all, and the contrast is internal to the article: clause (2), immediately below, names all three.

Clause (2) is about the procedure: "No such person as aforesaid shall be dismissed or removed or reduced in rank except after an inquiry in which he has been informed of the charges against him and given a reasonable opportunity of being heard in respect of those charges".

Three elements are stated, and they are the whole of what the Constitution requires. There must be an inquiry. The person must be informed of the charges. He must have a reasonable opportunity of being heard in respect of those charges. There is no requirement that the charges be of a particular kind, no requirement that the conduct alleged be of a stated gravity, and no requirement that the penalty bear any relation to what is proved. Clause (2) regulates the route to the order and says nothing about its content.

## Where the inquiry came from

The provision has a traceable ancestor, and the comparison is instructive because the Constitution did not simply copy it.

Section 240 of the Government of India Act, 1935 held civil servants at His Majesty's pleasure and then qualified it twice. Sub-section (2) provided that such a person could not be dismissed by an authority subordinate to that by which he was appointed. Sub-section (3) provided that he could not be dismissed or reduced in rank until he had been given "a reasonable opportunity of showing cause against the action proposed to be taken in regard to him". A proviso withdrew the second safeguard in two cases: where the dismissal or reduction followed conduct which had led to a conviction on a criminal charge, and where the authority empowered to dismiss was satisfied, for some reason to be recorded in writing, that it was not reasonably practicable to give the person an opportunity of showing cause.

*Tulsiram Patel* sets the two provisions side by side and states the net effect: the safeguards in Article 311 "are very much the same as those under section 240 with this difference that while Article 311 also affords safeguards against removal from service section 240 did not". The proviso to section 240(3) was reproduced, and then "an additional clause, namely, clause (c) has been added thereto. A provision similar to clause (3) of Article 311 was also absent from the Government of India Act, 1935." The Court's summary of the comparison is the sentence worth carrying forward: "Thus, while on the one hand Article 311 enlarges the protection afforded to civil servants, on the other hand it increases by one the number of cases in which that protection can be withdrawn."

Two features of the present article are therefore Indian additions rather than inheritances: the third exception, and the clause that makes one of the exceptions final. Both of them subtract.

## The stage that was read in, written down, and struck out

The words "a reasonable opportunity of showing cause against the action proposed to be taken in regard to him" carried a construction before the Constitution was written, and the construction survived it, because Article 311(2) as originally enacted repeated the same phrase.

In *Khem Chand v. Union of India*, [1958] SCR 1080, the Supreme Court held that an opportunity of that kind necessarily came in stages. The passage *Tulsiram Patel* quotes from it reasons from the words rather than from any general principle: the government servant must be able to say, if that be his case, "that he has not been guilty of any misconduct to merit any punishment at all", and also that the particular punishment proposed is more drastic than he deserves; and "Both these pleas have a direct bearing on the question of punishment and may well be put forward in showing cause against the proposed punishment." An opportunity to answer the charge and an opportunity to answer the proposed penalty were two different things, and the phrase was read as requiring both.

Clauses (2) and (3) of Article 311 were then substituted by section 10 of the Constitution (Fifteenth Amendment) Act, 1963, with effect from 5 October 1963. The substituted clause (2) replaced "showing cause" with the language of inquiry, charges and hearing which the article now carries, and added at the end the second stage: the person could not be dismissed, removed or reduced in rank "and where it is proposed, after such inquiry, to impose on him any such penalty, until he has been given a reasonable opportunity of making representation on the penalty proposed, but only on the basis of the evidence adduced during such inquiry". *Tulsiram Patel* describes the exercise accurately: the substituted clause, it says, "merely reproduced the substance of what was held" in *Khem Chand*.

That is an ordinary and unremarkable sequence. A court construes a constitutional phrase; Parliament, amending the provision for other reasons, writes the construction into the text so that it no longer depends on the construction.

Thirteen years later the same words were taken out. Section 44 of the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976 did two things to clause (2). By sub-clause (a) it omitted the passage just quoted in full. By sub-clause (b) it replaced the opening words of the existing proviso with a new proviso, so that what had been a single proviso became the second of two, and the first now reads: "Provided that where it is proposed after such inquiry, to impose upon him any such penalty, such penalty may be imposed on the basis of the evidence adduced during such inquiry and it shall not be necessary to give such person any opportunity of making representation on the penalty proposed".

The two operations are not symmetrical. The omission removed the hearing on penalty. The substitution kept the evidentiary tether — the penalty must still rest on the evidence adduced during the inquiry — and then stated in terms that no opportunity to be heard on the penalty is necessary. So the Constitution now carries an express negative on the point, which is a stronger position than silence would have been: a court cannot read the second stage back in from the words "reasonable opportunity of being heard in respect of those charges", because the proviso immediately afterwards says it is not necessary.

The amendment is also unusual in what it did to the article's structure. A requirement which had reached the Constitution as a judicial construction of a phrase, and had then been codified, was deleted thirteen years after being codified; and the deletion was effected not by restoring the earlier, broader phrase but by narrowing the new one and adding a proviso to foreclose the old reading. The same Act, by section 46, inserted Part XIVA and the power to establish administrative tribunals for service disputes — a provision whose jurisdictional clause has its own history, and is not the subject here.

## Three doors, three deciders

The second proviso to clause (2) withdraws the inquiry in three cases. The withdrawal is complete: *Tulsiram Patel* holds that once clause (2) is excluded by the Constitution itself, the principles of natural justice, including the rule of *audi alteram partem*, go with it, and Article 14 cannot be used to reinstate what the Constitution has excluded.

The three cases are drafted differently from one another, and the differences are the article's real content.

Clause (a) applies "where a person is dismissed or removed or reduced in rank on the ground of conduct which has led to his conviction on a criminal charge". The trigger is a fact established elsewhere, by a court, after a trial. No authority has to certify a state of affairs; the conviction either exists or it does not, and the ground on which the penalty rests is the conduct that led to it. This is the only one of the three in which the material that replaces the inquiry is itself the product of a judicial proceeding.

Clause (b) applies "where the authority empowered to dismiss or remove a person or to reduce him in rank is satisfied that for some reason, to be recorded by that authority in writing, it is not reasonably practicable to hold such inquiry". The decider here is the disciplinary authority — the same authority that will pass the order of penalty. The clause does impose a condition of form: the reason must be recorded in writing.

Clause (c) applies "where the President or the Governor, as the case may be, is satisfied that in the interest of the security of the State it is not expedient to hold such inquiry". The decider is the highest executive authority. There is no requirement that any reason be recorded, in writing or at all.

Set out in that order, the guards do not increase with the gravity of what is being dispensed with or with the breadth of the ground relied on. They run the other way. The narrowest and most verifiable ground is tied to a court's judgment. The middle ground, resting on the dismissing authority's own assessment of practicability, carries a writing requirement. The widest ground, resting on an assessment of what the security of the State makes inexpedient, carries none — and it is the ground on which the executive's satisfaction is least capable of being tested against anything, because "security of the State" is nowhere defined and "not expedient" sets no standard.

## What "shall be final" was for

Clause (3) of Article 311 attaches to one of the three doors and not the others: "If, in respect of any such person as aforesaid, a question arises whether it is reasonably practicable to hold such inquiry as is referred to in clause (2), the decision thereon of the authority empowered to dismiss or remove such person or to reduce him in rank shall be final."

The clause is keyed to clause (b). It says nothing about clause (a), where finality would add nothing, and nothing about clause (c), where the satisfaction is the President's or the Governor's. So the one ground on which the Constitution confers express finality is the ground on which the authority certifies the necessity of its own shortcut.

What that finality does was settled in *Tulsiram Patel*, and the holdings are worth taking in sequence.

First, the recorded reason belongs to the file and not to the person. "The constitutional requirement in clause (b) is that the reason for dispensing with the inquiry should be recorded in writing. There is no obligation to communicate the reason to the government servant."

Second, the finality operates inside the administration with full force. "As clause (3) of Article 311 makes the decision of the disciplinary authority on this point final, the question cannot be agitated in a departmental appeal, revision or review." A civil servant dismissed under clause (b) is not left without a departmental remedy; the Court recorded that "There are two remedies open to him, namely, departmental appeal and judicial review", and held that where the circumstances which made an inquiry impracticable have since passed, "If the situation has changed when the appeal or revision is heard, the government servant can claim to have an inquiry held in which he can establish that he is not guilty of the charges on which he has been dismissed, removed or reduced in rank". What he may not do in that appeal, by reason of clause (3), is contend that the inquiry was wrongly dispensed with in the first place. The appeal is open on the charge and closed on the shortcut.

Third — and this is the part of the judgment that explains the design — the Court identified whom the writing is for: the obligation to record the reason "is provided in clause (b) so that the superiors of the disciplinary authority may be able to judge whether such authority had exercised its power under clause (b) properly or not with a view to judge the performance and capacity of that officer for the purposes of promotion etc."

Read together, those three propositions describe a safeguard that is real but faces in an unexpected direction. There is a written record. It is not given to the person whose service has ended, and it cannot be challenged by him in the departmental hierarchy. It exists so that the authority's own superiors may form a view of how he exercises his powers, which they will take into account in deciding his advancement. The discipline on the use of clause (b) is a discipline of career appraisal.

That is not a criticism of the Court's reading, which follows from the text. Clause (b) requires the reason to be recorded and does not require it to be given to anybody; clause (3) makes the decision final; and a provision that requires a writing which nobody affected may see and which no departmental authority may reconsider must be for the benefit of somebody else in the office. The Court simply said who.

Clause (c) carries no finality clause and does not appear to need one. *Tulsiram Patel* records that the service rules made under or referable to Article 309 "generally provide for a right of appeal except in those cases where the order of dismissal, removal or reduction in rank is passed by the President or the Governor of a State", for the sufficient reason that there is no higher authority to appeal to. Where the inquiry has been dispensed with under clause (c) but the penalty is imposed by the disciplinary authority, the Court held that "The satisfaction of the President or the Governor cannot be challenged in appeal or revision but the government servant can in appeal or revision ask for an inquiry to be held into his alleged conduct unless even at the time of the appeal or revision, the interest of the security of the State makes it inexpedient to hold such an inquiry", adding that "no such right would be available to a government servant where the order imposing penalty has been made by the President or the Governor of a State". So the ground on which the Constitution declares nothing final arrives at much the same departmental destination as the ground on which it does, by a different road. In the one case the text forecloses the question. In the other the hierarchy runs out.

## Where the finality stops

Clause (3) says "shall be final" without qualification, and *Tulsiram Patel* held that the finality has one boundary.

Where a civil servant is dismissed, removed or reduced in rank under clause (b) and approaches the High Court under Article 226 or the Supreme Court under Article 32, "the court will interfere on grounds well established in law for the exercise of power of judicial review in matters where administrative discretion is exercised", and will consider whether clause (b) was properly applied. On the clause itself the Court was explicit: "The finality given by clause (3) of Article 311 to the disciplinary authority's decision that it was not reasonably practicable to hold the inquiry is not binding upon the court."

The content of that review is narrow but not nominal. The court examines the relevance of the reasons: it considers the situation which, according to the disciplinary authority, led it to conclude that an inquiry was not reasonably practicable, and if it finds the reasons irrelevant the recording of satisfaction is an abuse of the power conferred by clause (b). A charge of *mala fides* may be examined. And the writing itself is enforced through the court's procedure rather than through any duty of disclosure: if the reasons have not been communicated and the matter comes before the court, "the court can direct the reasons to be produced, and furnished to the government servant and if still not produced, a presumption should be drawn that the reasons were not recorded in writing and the impugned order would then stand invalidated", a presumption rebuttable by a satisfactory explanation for the non-production.

The Court also observed that it would in any event "be better for the disciplinary authority to communicate to the government servant its reason for dispensing with the inquiry because such communication would eliminate the possibility of an allegation being made that the reasons have been subsequently fabricated" — an observation which identifies the obvious vulnerability of a document that need not be shown to anyone at the time it is made.

So the structure, once all the pieces are in place, runs like this. The Constitution requires an inquiry. It permits the inquiry to be dispensed with on a ground of which the dismissing authority is the judge. It requires that authority to write down why. It forbids the writing from being questioned in the departmental hierarchy, and does not require it to be shown to the person affected. And the only forum in which the dispensation can in fact be examined is the one clause (3) does not mention, under articles that are nowhere referred to in Article 311.

## The persons the article does not reach

Article 310(1) covers members of defence services and persons holding posts connected with defence in the same breath as civil servants. Article 311 does not. Its heading speaks of "persons employed in civil capacities", and clause (1) extends only to a member of a civil service of the Union, a member of an all-India service, a member of a civil service of a State, or the holder of a civil post under the Union or a State.

The consequence is structural rather than incidental. For the armed forces the pleasure in Article 310(1) stands unqualified by any express provision of the kind Article 311 supplies, and Article 33 separately empowers Parliament to determine by law the extent to which the rights conferred by Part III are restricted or abrogated in their application to the members of the Armed Forces and of the Forces charged with the maintenance of public order, so as to ensure the proper discharge of their duties and the maintenance of discipline among them. The class in which discipline is most insisted upon is the class to which the procedural safeguard was not extended at all.

## The second safeguard, and the power to switch it off

Part XIV contains one further check on disciplinary action, in its second chapter, and it is addressed to a body outside the department.

Article 320(3) provides that "The Union Public Service Commission or the State Public Service Commission, as the case may be, shall be consulted" on the matters listed, and clause (c) of the list is "on all disciplinary matters affecting a person serving under the Government of India or the Government of a State in a civil capacity, including memorials or petitions relating to such matters".

An external body is therefore brought into the disciplinary process by the Constitution itself, and not by the rules of the department concerned. Two features of the provision limit what that is worth.

The first is that the consultation can be dispensed with by the executive. The proviso to clause (3) permits the President, as respects the all-India services and other services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union, and the Governor as respects services and posts in connection with the affairs of a State, to "make regulations specifying the matters in which either generally, or in any particular class of case or in any particular circumstances, it shall not be necessary for a Public Service Commission to be consulted". The authority that is being checked is the authority empowered to narrow the check, and it may do so generally, by class of case, or by circumstance. Clause (5) subjects those regulations to a measure of legislative control: they must be laid for not less than fourteen days before each House of Parliament or of the State Legislature, and are "subject to such modifications, whether by way of repeal or amendment", as the House or Houses may make during the session in which they are laid.

The second is that what the Commission supplies is advice. Article 323 requires the Commission’s annual report to be presented to the President or the Governor, and a copy of it to be laid before the legislature "together with a memorandum explaining, as respects the cases, if any, where the advice of the Commission was not accepted, the reasons for such non-acceptance". A provision that requires the reasons for departing from advice to be explained is a provision which assumes that departing from it is permissible. The consultation is a procedural step whose outcome does not bind, and whose requirement may be removed by regulation for a class of cases.

## What the arrangement assumes

Part XIV’s protection of the permanence of the civil service rests on one device: it requires a procedure before the three gravest penalties, and it makes that requirement constitutional so that no legislature can reduce it. That is a real protection and it has real consequences; the inquiry contemplated by clause (2) is not a formality, and an order passed without one where none of the exceptions applies is bad.

But the protection is of a particular kind, and the kind matters. It is procedural, so it does not ask whether the officer deserved what he received. It is excluded in three cases by a single proviso, and the exclusion takes natural justice with it. One of the three cases is worked by the authority that will impose the penalty, on a question — practicability — which is a question about that authority's own circumstances. On that question the Constitution expressly makes its decision final, and the reason it must record is a document the person dismissed need not be shown and the departmental hierarchy may not revisit.

What such an arrangement assumes is that the officer applying the exception is kept honest by his own superiors, in the ordinary course of assessing him for promotion. That is the assumption the Supreme Court identified, and it is not an unreasonable one about a career service. It is simply an assumption about administrative virtue inside a department, placed at the point where a protection against that department's power is supposed to operate.

The one external check the article does not mention is the one that has actually done the work. Nothing in Article 311 refers to Article 226 or Article 32, and clause (3) is drafted as though no court were in view. That the finality it declares is not a finality against a court was established by a decision of five Judges nine years after the Act that last amended the article, and it was established on general principles of judicial review rather than on anything in the words. A safeguard whose operative limit had to be supplied from outside the provision is a safeguard whose draftsman did not supply one.
