Every code of criminal procedure has to answer a question that the law of offences never reaches: while an investigation is running and no charge has been framed, who holds the body of the person arrested. There are only two possible custodians — the investigating agency, or the court — and the choice between them decides the practical character of the investigation. A person in police custody is available for interrogation at the investigator's convenience. A person in judicial custody is not, or not without the court's leave.

The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 answered that question in a single sub-section with a proviso attached. The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 answers it in two sub-sections, and the division of labour between them is different. The change is small on the page and large in effect, and it was made at a point when the question it settles was pending before a larger Bench of the Supreme Court.

## What the Code provided

Section 167(2) of the Code read:

> "The Magistrate to whom an accused person is forwarded under this section may, whether he has or has not jurisdiction to try the case, from time to time, authorise the detention of the accused in such custody as such Magistrate thinks fit, for a term not exceeding fifteen days in the whole; and if he has no jurisdiction to try the case or commit it for trial, and considers further detention unnecessary, he may order the accused to be forwarded to a Magistrate having such jurisdiction."

Paragraph (a) of the proviso, substituted in its final form by section 13 of Act 45 of 1978 with effect from 18 December 1978, read:

> "the Magistrate may authorise the detention of the accused person, otherwise than in the custody of the police, beyond the period of fifteen days, if he is satisfied that adequate grounds exist for doing so, but no Magistrate shall authorise the detention of the accused person in custody under this paragraph for a total period exceeding,—
>
> (i) ninety days, where the investigation relates to an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life or imprisonment for a term of not less than ten years;
>
> (ii) sixty days, where the investigation relates to any other offence..."

Two limits sat in that structure and they did different work. The sub-section limited the *quantity* of custody a Magistrate could authorise before the proviso was reached: fifteen days in the whole, of either kind, as the Magistrate thought fit. The proviso limited the *kind* of custody available after that: detention beyond fifteen days could be authorised, but only "otherwise than in the custody of the police".

Nothing in either limb said in terms that police custody must be taken in the first fifteen days. That proposition had to be constructed, and it was constructed out of the phrase just quoted.

## The construction built on five words

In *Central Bureau of Investigation v. Anupam J. Kulkarni*, decided on 8 May 1992 and reported at (1992) 3 SCC 141, the question was put directly: could a person produced before a Magistrate under section 167(1) still be remanded to police custody after the expiry of the initial period of fifteen days. The Court held that he could not. Its reasoning turned on the words of the proviso, which it identified as decisive:

> "Now comes the proviso inserted by Act No. 45 of 1978 which is of vital importance in deciding the question before us. This proviso comes into operation where the magistrate thinks fit that further detention beyond the period of fifteen days is necessary and it lays down that the magistrate may authorise the detention of the accused person otherwise than in the custody of the police beyond the period of fifteen days."

The holdings that followed were stated as propositions:

> "After the expiry of the first period of fifteen days the further remand during the period of investigation can only be in judicial custody."

and

> "There cannot be any detention in the police custody after the expiry of first fifteen days even in a case where some more offences either serious or otherwise committed by him in the same transaction come to light at a later stage."

The Court was explicit that within the fifteen days the character of the custody could be changed more than once, from police to judicial or the reverse, and that the aggregate was what mattered. It was equally explicit that the bar did not apply where the same person was involved in a different case arising out of a different transaction; there, a fresh arrest and a fresh order under section 167(2) were available.

*Kulkarni* read into the Code a rule about *when* police custody could be taken. The Code itself contained only a rule about *how much*, plus a rule about what was available afterwards. The bridge between the two was the five words of the proviso.

## The disagreement, and the reference

That construction held for three decades. On 7 August 2023, in *V. Senthil Balaji v. State represented by Deputy Director*, a Bench of the Supreme Court declined to concur in it. The relevant passage is under a heading framed as a question — does section 167(2) of the CrPC, 1973 restrict a police custody only to the first 15 days of remand — and it answers in the negative:

> "With due respect, we are unable to concur with the views expressed in Anupam J. Kulkarni (supra) to the effect that a police custody shall only be within the first 15 days of remand. Nowhere under Section 167(2) of the CrPC, 1973 such a stipulation is found either directly or indirectly. The words such as 'time to time', 'such custody', and 'in the whole' mentioned under Section 167(2) of the CrPC, 1973 have not been properly taken note of and interpreted."

The Court's own reading of the relationship between the sub-section and the proviso was the opposite of *Kulkarni*'s:

> "Once the period is given as 60 days or 90 days as the case may be, to an investigating agency, in tune with the proviso, Section 167(2) of the CrPC, 1973 by even normal interpretation facilitates a police custody spanning over the said period, but 'whole' being for 15 days."

Because *Kulkarni* had been endorsed by a larger Bench — the judgment identifies *Budh Singh v. State of Punjab*, (2009) 9 SCC 266 — the Bench could not simply depart from it. It recorded its agreement with *Central Bureau of Investigation v. Vikas Mishra*, (2023) 6 SCC 49 — which, the judgment notes, "actually dealt with the issue of counting the days" rather than this question — and referred the point onward:

> "we are inclined to refer the larger issue of the actual import of Section 167(2) of the CrPC, 1973 as to whether the 15 days period of custody in favour of the police should be only within the first 15 days of remand or spanning over the entire period of investigation - 60 or 90 days, as the case may be, as a whole."

Two readings were therefore on the table in August 2023. On the first, the fifteen days had to be taken inside the first fifteen. On the second, they could be taken anywhere inside sixty or ninety. The difference between them is not the amount of police custody but its placement, and placement is what decides whether an investigator who has not yet found what he is looking for can obtain custody again in the eighth week.

## What the Sanhita provides

The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 is Act No. 46 of 2023 and bears the date 25 December 2023 — four months and eighteen days after the reference. Its section 187 corresponds to section 167 of the Code. Sub-section (2) reads:

> "The Magistrate to whom an accused person is forwarded under this section may, irrespective of whether he has or has no jurisdiction to try the case, after taking into consideration whether such person has not been released on bail or his bail has been cancelled, authorise, from time to time, the detention of the accused in such custody as such Magistrate thinks fit, for a term not exceeding fifteen days in the whole, or in parts, at any time during the initial forty days or sixty days out of detention period of sixty days or ninety days, as the case may be, as provided in sub-section (3), and if he has no jurisdiction to try the case or commit it for trial, and considers further detention unnecessary, he may order the accused to be forwarded to a Magistrate having such jurisdiction."

Sub-section (3), which takes the place of paragraph (a) of the old proviso, reads:

> "The Magistrate may authorise the detention of the accused person, beyond the period of fifteen days, if he is satisfied that adequate grounds exist for doing so, but no Magistrate shall authorise the detention of the accused person in custody under this sub-section for a total period exceeding—
>
> (i) ninety days, where the investigation relates to an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life or imprisonment for a term of ten years or more;
>
> (ii) sixty days, where the investigation relates to any other offence, and, on the expiry of the said period of ninety days, or sixty days, as the case may be, the accused person shall be released on bail if he is prepared to and does furnish bail, and every person released on bail under this sub-section shall be deemed to be so released under the provisions of Chapter XXXV for the purposes of that Chapter."

Set the two schemes side by side and the differences are these.

## The five words are gone

The phrase "otherwise than in the custody of the police" does not appear in section 187(3). It does not appear anywhere in the Sanhita. The limb of the old proviso that restricted the *kind* of custody available after fifteen days has been removed, and nothing has been put in its place that names police custody as the thing excluded.

This matters for a reason that has nothing to do with whether *Kulkarni* was rightly decided. *Kulkarni* was an exercise in construction, and the material it construed was those five words. A court asked the same question under the Sanhita does not have that material. Sub-section (3) authorises "the detention of the accused person, beyond the period of fifteen days" without qualifying the custody at all.

It does not follow that police custody is available under sub-section (3). The limit is still there; it has simply moved and changed form. Police custody remains confined by sub-section (2), which caps custody of the Magistrate's choosing at fifteen days in the whole and places those days inside a stated window. What is no longer there is an express prohibition. Under the Code, a Magistrate who authorised police custody on day twenty was doing something the proviso forbade in terms. Under the Sanhita he is doing something that exceeds a window and an aggregate fixed in a different sub-section, which is a conclusion to be reached by reading the two together rather than a prohibition to be read off the page.

## A different limit is inserted in their place

What the Sanhita puts in place of the kind-limit is a time-limit, and it goes into sub-section (2) rather than sub-section (3): the fifteen days may be taken "in the whole, or in parts, at any time during the initial forty days or sixty days".

So the aggregate is untouched. Fifteen days of custody of the Magistrate's choosing remains the ceiling, exactly as under section 167(2), and *Kulkarni*'s proposition that the character of the custody may change within the aggregate is consistent with the new words as well. What has changed is the window inside which the aggregate may be drawn down. Under *Kulkarni* it was the first fifteen days. Under section 187(2) it is the first forty or the first sixty.

That is neither of the two readings that were before the Court in August 2023. It is a third position between them: wider than the first fifteen days, narrower than the whole of sixty or ninety. The express words "or in parts" settle, for the Sanhita, the question whether the fifteen days may be fragmented — they may — and they do so without waiting for the reference to be answered.

## Which window belongs to which offence

Sub-section (2) says "the initial forty days or sixty days out of detention period of sixty days or ninety days". Sub-section (3) then fixes the outer periods in the order ninety, then sixty. The two sub-sections describe the same two classes of offence in opposite sequence, and a reader who matches the pairs positionally across them will get the answer backwards.

The arithmetic resolves it. Taken in the order sub-section (2) itself uses, forty belongs to sixty and sixty belongs to ninety. Each window is two-thirds of its outer period, which is the only pairing on which the two fractions are the same. The alternative — forty days of window inside a ninety-day period, sixty inside sixty — would make the window for the graver class of offence proportionately *smaller* than for the lesser, and in the second pairing would make the window and the outer period identical, which would leave the words "initial" and "out of" doing nothing. So the shorter window attaches to the shorter period, and the sixty-day window of section 187(2) sits inside the ninety-day period of section 187(3)(i).

It is an unhappy way to legislate a number. The sub-section that creates the window does not name the offences; it borrows them from a later sub-section by the formula "as the case may be", and that later sub-section lists them the other way round.

## The ten-year formula was restated, not changed

Section 167(2), proviso (a)(i) spoke of an offence punishable with "imprisonment for a term of not less than ten years". Section 187(3)(i) says "imprisonment for a term of ten years or more".

In *Rajeev Chaudhary v. State (N.C.T.) of Delhi*, decided on 4 May 2001 and reported at (2001) 5 SCC 34, the Court construed the older formula. The question was whether an offence under section 386 of the Indian Penal Code, punishable with imprisonment "which may extend to ten years", fell within clause (i) and so attracted ninety days. The Court held that it did not:

> "In this context, the expression 'not less than' would mean imprisonment should be 10 years or more and would cover only those offences for which punishment could be imprisonment for a clear period of 10 years or more."

The gloss the Court adopted — "10 years or more" — is the very phrase the Sanhita now uses. On the Court's own reasoning the test is unchanged: an offence whose maximum is ten years but whose minimum is less is not an offence punishable with ten years or more, because the sentence may lawfully be shorter. The drafting change substitutes the construction for the words construed. It does not disturb the construction, and the sixty-day period continues to govern offences carrying a maximum of ten years and no minimum.

## Two smaller changes in the same section

Sub-section (2) now requires the Magistrate to act "after taking into consideration whether such person has not been released on bail or his bail has been cancelled". There is no counterpart in section 167(2). The clause names a consideration and attaches no consequence to either answer: the Magistrate is not told what follows if the person has been released on bail, nor what follows if bail has been cancelled. It states a matter to be taken into account, and leaves the account to be taken.

Sub-section (4) carries forward the requirement of production in person for police custody, and adds a proviso with no ancestor in the Code:

> "Provided further that no person shall be detained otherwise than in police station under police custody or in prison under judicial custody or a place declared as prison by the Central Government or the State Government."

The distinction between police custody and judicial custody therefore survives in the Sanhita; it is stated here by reference to the place of detention. What has gone is not the distinction but its use as a limit on what sub-section (3) may authorise.

## What is left open

Three things are not stated in section 187 and will have to be worked out.

The first is the number of parts. "In parts" has no numeral attached to it, and nothing in the section fixes a minimum duration for a part or a maximum number of them. Fifteen days may evidently be taken as one block; whether they may be taken as fifteen separate single days spread across a sixty-day window, each requiring a fresh production and a fresh order with reasons recorded under sub-section (7), is a question the words do not answer.

The second is the start of the window. The section says "the initial forty days or sixty days out of detention period of sixty days or ninety days" — the window is measured inside the detention period, and the detention period is the one sub-section (3) caps. From what event that period runs is not stated in section 187 any more than it was stated in section 167, and the point was settled for the old section by construction rather than by text.

The third is the interaction with the entitlement to release under sub-section (3). Once the ninety or sixty days expire the person "shall be released on bail if he is prepared to and does furnish bail". A window of forty or sixty days sits wholly inside that period, so on the face of it the two do not collide. But Explanation I provides that notwithstanding the expiry of the period in sub-section (3) the accused shall be detained in custody so long as he does not furnish bail, and a person who has not furnished bail is still in custody after day sixty or ninety. Whether a part of the fifteen days may be sought against such a person — one whose entitlement to release has accrued but has not been acted on — is not addressed. The window has closed by then on any reading of sub-section (2); the question is whether the closure is absolute or merely runs out with the period.

## The sequence is the point

None of this depends on a view about whether police custody ought to be confined to the first fifteen days. The point is narrower and it is about how the question came to be answered.

A rule about the timing of police custody was derived in 1992 from five words in a proviso. Thirty-one years later a Bench of the Supreme Court said the derivation was wrong, could not say so with final effect because a larger Bench had endorsed it, and referred the point for authoritative decision. Four months and eighteen days after that, Parliament enacted a section which removes the five words and inserts an express answer matching neither of the positions that had been argued.

The reference has not lost its subject. Section 531(1) of the Sanhita repeals the Code, but section 531(2)(a) provides that an investigation pending immediately before 1 July 2024 — the Sanhita was brought into force on that date by notification S.O. 850(E) dated 23 February 2024 — "shall be disposed of, continued, held or made, as the case may be, in accordance with the provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 ..., as in force immediately before such commencement ..., as if this Sanhita had not come into force". For those investigations the question referred remains live and the answer still matters.

For everything investigated since, it does not. The answer is in section 187(2), and it is forty days or sixty.
