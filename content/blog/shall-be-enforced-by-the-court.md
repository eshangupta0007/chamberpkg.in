excerpt: "Until 2018 the Specific Relief Act, 1963 asked four questions in four places: section 10 asked whether damages would be inadequate, section 14 whether the contract fell in an excluded class, section 16 whether the plaintiff was personally disentitled, and section 20 whether, on these facts, the court ought to decree performance at all. The Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018 substituted section 10 so that performance 'shall be enforced by the court' subject to three sections, and substituted section 20 out of existence. The grounds section 20 held — unforeseen hardship, unfair advantage, inequitable circumstances — were not carried into the new section 14, which has four clauses and none of them is any of those. Section 9 does not take them either, because hardship arising from a contract that is neither void nor voidable is not a ground under the law of contracts. The Act is now shorter and more decided, and it no longer says what a court is to do when enforcing a lawful contract would work an injustice the contract did not price."

Every system that enforces contracts has to choose a primary remedy for breach, and there are only two candidates. The court can compel the promisor to do what he promised, or it can require him to pay for not doing it. The choice looks like a question about remedies and is in fact a question about obligations. If the primary remedy is money, a contract is a priced option: the promisor may break it and pay, and the law's only interest is in fixing the price. If the primary remedy is performance, a contract is an obligation to do the thing, and money enters only where the thing cannot be had.

English equity, from which the Indian law descends, chose the first and admitted the second as an exception. Specific performance was available where damages would not do, and because the remedy came from equity it came with the attribute equitable remedies have: it was discretionary. A plaintiff who established his contract and his own performance had not thereby established his entitlement. He had established that the court might grant him a decree.

The Specific Relief Act, 1963 inherited both features and kept them in separate places. The inadequacy threshold sat in section 10. The discretion sat in section 20. The Specific Relief (Amendment) Act, 2018 removed both. What it did not do is say where the second of them went, and that omission is the subject of this note.

## The four questions the Act asked in 1963

Read as it stood before 2018, Chapter II of the Act put four questions to a suit for specific performance, and put each of them in its own section.

Section 10 asked whether the contract was of a kind the court would enforce in specie at all:

> "Except as otherwise provided in this Chapter, the specific performance of any contract may, in the discretion of the court, be enforced— (a) when there exists no standard for ascertaining actual damage caused by the non-performance of the act agreed to be done; or (b) when the act agreed to be done is such that compensation in money for its non-performance would not afford adequate relief."

The Explanation to the section then did most of the practical work. Unless the contrary were proved, the court was to presume that the breach of a contract to transfer immovable property could not be adequately relieved by compensation in money, and that the breach of a contract to transfer movable property could be, except where the property was not an ordinary article of commerce, or was of special value or interest to the plaintiff, or consisted of goods not easily obtainable in the market, or was held by the defendant as the plaintiff's agent or trustee. The threshold was therefore met by presumption in the ordinary suit on an agreement to sell land, and contested only where the defendant could rebut it.

Section 14 asked whether the contract fell within an excluded class. Section 16 asked whether the plaintiff was personally disentitled. And section 20, which carried the heading "Discretion as to decreeing specific performance", asked the fourth question:

> "(1) The jurisdiction to decree specific performance is discretionary, and the court is not bound to grant such relief merely because it is lawful to do so; but the discretion of the court is not arbitrary but sound and reasonable, guided by judicial principles and capable of correction by a court of appeal."

Sub-section (2) then named three cases in which the court might properly exercise the discretion against a decree: where the terms of the contract, the conduct of the parties at the time of entering into it, or the circumstances in which it was entered into were such that the contract, though not voidable, gave the plaintiff an unfair advantage over the defendant; where performance would involve some hardship on the defendant which he did not foresee, whereas non-performance would involve no such hardship on the plaintiff; and where the defendant entered into the contract in circumstances which, though not rendering it voidable, made it inequitable to enforce performance. Two Explanations bounded the enquiry. The first provided that mere inadequacy of consideration, or the mere fact that the contract was onerous or improvident, was not to be deemed an unfair advantage or a hardship. The second provided that hardship was to be judged as at the time of the contract, except where it had resulted from an act of the plaintiff after it.

Sub-sections (3) and (4) pointed the other way. The court might properly exercise the discretion in favour of a decree where the plaintiff had done substantial acts or suffered losses in consequence of a contract capable of specific performance; and want of mutuality — the circumstance that the contract was not enforceable at the instance of the party resisting the decree — was not by itself to be a reason for refusing performance to the party seeking it.

The architecture is worth stating plainly, because the amendment altered it rather than merely editing it. The first three questions were about the contract and about the plaintiff. The fourth was about the defendant's position and the equities between the two, and it was the only question in the chapter that permitted a court to refuse a decree to a plaintiff who had satisfied every condition the Act imposed on him.

## What section 10 says now, and what its list leaves out

Section 3 of the amending Act substituted section 10 in its entirety. The section now reads, in full:

> "10. The specific performance of a contract shall be enforced by the court subject to the provisions contained in sub-section (2) of section 11, section 14 and section 16."

Three things have happened in that sentence.

The first is the change of register. "May, in the discretion of the court, be enforced" has become "shall be enforced by the court". The marginal note has changed with it, from "Cases in which specific performance of contract enforceable" to "Specific performance in respect of contracts", which is a heading for a rule rather than for a list of cases.

The second is the disappearance of the adequacy enquiry. The two clauses of the old section are gone, and so are the Explanation and the two presumptions it carried. The point is easy to understate, because adequacy of damages appeared three times in the chapter and the same amendment removed all three: the threshold in section 10, the exclusion in old section 14(1)(a) for "a contract for the non-performance of which compensation in money is an adequate relief", and the second of the three conditions in old section 14(3)(c). After 1 October 2018 the Act does not ask, at any stage of a suit for specific performance, whether money would be an adequate substitute. The test survives elsewhere in the Act — section 38(3)(c) still makes it a ground for a perpetual injunction, and the 2018 Act did not touch section 38 — but it has gone from the chapter that governs the remedy it used to gate.

The third is the list. The new section is made subject to three provisions — sub-section (2) of section 11, section 14, and section 16 — and the Act contains others that bear on whether a decree may be made. Section 9 preserves to the defendant any ground of defence available to him "under any law relating to contracts". Section 12(1) provides, in terms as mandatory as the new section 10's own, that "the court shall not direct the specific performance of a part of a contract" except as that section provides. Sections 20A and 41 bar injunctions in defined cases. None of these is named.

Nothing follows from the omission as a matter of construction, because section 10 says "subject to" and not "subject only to", and a provision does not cease to operate because another provision has failed to mention it. What follows is a drafting consequence rather than a legal one. A section that opens an enquiry by naming the provisions that qualify it presents itself as a map of the qualifications, and this one is not a map. The three sections it names are the three that exclude the claim by reference to the contract itself or to the plaintiff's own position. Section 9, which is not named, is the gateway through which every defence arising from the law of contracts enters the suit. Section 12, also not named, governs what the decree may contain once the claim survives.

There is a smaller curiosity in the same sentence. Section 10 is made subject to sub-section (2) of section 11 — the provision that a contract made by a trustee in excess of his powers or in breach of trust cannot be specifically enforced — and not to sub-section (1), which deals with contracts whose performance is wholly or partly the performance of a trust. Section 4 of the amending Act altered sub-section (1) too, substituting "contract shall" for "contract may, in the discretion of the court". So the Act now contains two mandatory commands to enforce, a general one in section 10 and a particular one for trust contracts in section 11(1), and the general one is expressly subject only to the negative half of the section that contains the particular one.

## The grounds that went out with section 20

Section 10 of the amending Act substituted new sections for section 20. Nothing in the new sections 20, 20A, 20B or 20C reproduces any part of the old one. The three cases in sub-section (2) — unfair advantage, unforeseen hardship, inequitable circumstances — are gone, together with the two Explanations that confined them, the direction in sub-section (3) that substantial acts or losses by the plaintiff were a reason to decree, and the rule in sub-section (4) that want of mutuality was not by itself a reason to refuse.

The new section 14 does not take them up. It reads:

> "14. The following contracts cannot be specifically enforced, namely:— (a) where a party to the contract has obtained substituted performance of contract in accordance with the provisions of section 20; (b) a contract, the performance of which involves the performance of a continuous duty which the court cannot supervise; (c) a contract which is so dependent on the personal qualifications of the parties that the court cannot enforce specific performance of its material terms; and (d) a contract which is in its nature determinable."

Four clauses, and none of them is hardship, unfair advantage or inequity. Each is a statement about the contract — what has been done under it, what its performance would require of the court, what it depends on, whether it is terminable. None is a statement about what performance would now cost the defendant.

Section 9 of the principal Act does not take them either, and the reason is worth being exact about. That section permits the defendant to "plead by way of defence any ground which is available to him under any law relating to contracts". Hardship that the defendant did not foresee, arising under a contract that is neither void nor voidable, is not such a ground. It did not make the contract unenforceable at law; it made a court of equity unwilling to compel it. That is precisely why the 1963 Act had to state it in a separate section, and why deleting that section does not leave it standing somewhere else.

So on the text, after 1 October 2018, a defendant who wishes to resist a decree on the ground that performance would ruin him, in a contract that was lawful when made and remains lawful, has no clause of the Act to point to. Whether a court retains a residual power of the kind section 20(1) described is a question the amendment does not address in terms, and it should be said plainly that the amendment does not foreclose it either: it deletes a statement that the jurisdiction is discretionary without stating that it is not. But two features of the drafting suggest that the removal was intended as a removal rather than a relocation. The first is that section 20(1) was the Act's own description of the nature of the jurisdiction, and a jurisdiction described nowhere is harder to assert than one described in a sub-section. The second is section 9 of the amending Act — not to be confused with section 9 of the principal Act just discussed — which is easy to overlook and says this:

> "For sub-heading 'Discretion and powers of Court' occurring after section 19, the sub-heading 'Substituted performance of contracts, etc.' shall be substituted."

The Act no longer has a part headed discretion. It has a part headed substituted performance, and substituted performance is not a discretion. It is a self-help remedy exercisable by a party without reference to the court, and, as the next section shows, exercising it destroys the claim to performance rather than preserving it.

It is also worth noticing which side the deletions fell on. Sub-sections (2)(a) to (c) were the defendant's; sub-sections (3) and (4) were the plaintiff's. A plaintiff who had taken possession, spent money or suffered loss on the strength of the contract could formerly point to sub-section (3) as a statutory reason for a decree, and a plaintiff met with the objection that the contract was not enforceable against him could point to sub-section (4). Both are gone. The removal of a discretion section removes the directions that guided the discretion in both directions, and the plaintiff, whom the amendment otherwise favours, has lost two provisions that were his.

## Section 14, and the exception that went without the rule

Old section 14 had three sub-sections. Sub-section (1) listed four excluded classes; sub-section (2) dealt with contracts to refer differences to arbitration; sub-section (3) provided that notwithstanding clauses (a), (c) and (d) of sub-section (1), the court might enforce performance in three groups of cases. The new section has no sub-sections at all.

The mapping repays attention. Old clause (c), the contract determinable in its nature, survives as new clause (d), unchanged. Old clause (d), the contract involving a continuous duty the court cannot supervise, survives as new clause (b). Old clause (a), adequacy of compensation, is gone. Old clause (b) survives only in part: the new clause (c) retains the contract "so dependent on the personal qualifications of the parties", and drops the contract which "runs into such minute or numerous details", the dependence on the "volition of the parties", and the residual words "or otherwise from its nature is such". New clause (a), the contract under which substituted performance has already been obtained, has no predecessor.

Old sub-section (2) is gone. It had provided that "[s]ave as provided by the Arbitration Act, 1940 (10 of 1940), no contract to refer present or future differences to arbitration shall be specifically enforced", a reference to a statute repealed in 1996 and still standing in the Act in 2018. The same amending Act corrected the Act's other reference to that repealed statute, substituting the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 for the Arbitration Act, 1940 in section 25. The Act therefore carried a dead reference in two places; one was corrected and the other was deleted along with the sub-section that held it.

Old sub-section (3) is gone, and this is the deletion with the sharpest practical edge. Its clause (c) allowed the court to enforce "a contract for the construction of any building or the execution of any other work on land", notwithstanding clauses (a), (c) and (d) of sub-section (1), where three conditions were fulfilled: that the work was described in the contract in terms sufficiently precise to enable the court to determine its exact nature; that the plaintiff had a substantial interest in performance, of such a nature that compensation in money would not be adequate relief; and that the defendant had, in pursuance of the contract, obtained possession of the whole or part of the land.

The bar that clause lifted was the continuous-duty bar, and that bar survives as new section 14(b). The provision that lifted it does not. A construction contract whose performance the court cannot supervise is therefore now excluded by section 14(b) with nothing in the Act to take it out again. The other heads of old sub-section (3) — contracts to execute a mortgage or furnish security, to take up and pay for debentures, to execute a formal deed of partnership where the business had been commenced, and to purchase a partner's share in a firm — were needed mainly to escape the adequacy exclusion in old clause (a), and that exclusion has itself been deleted; so for them the loss of the exception matters much less. For building contracts it matters, because the rule they needed rescuing from is still there.

Section 5 of the amending Act did something else at the same time, and it pulls against the bar it left standing. Besides substituting section 14 it inserted section 14A, which provides that in any suit under the Act, where the court considers it necessary to get expert opinion to assist it on any specific issue involved in the suit, it may engage one or more experts and direct them to report to it, may require any person to give information to the expert or to provide access to documents, goods or property for inspection, and shall receive the opinion or report as part of the record, with the expert liable to be examined in open court. The fee is fixed by the court and borne by the parties in the proportion it directs.

The bar in section 14(b) is not that the duty is continuous but that it is one "which the court cannot supervise". Section 14A gives the court an instrument of supervision it did not previously have in terms. Whether a court can now supervise what it could not supervise in 2018 is therefore a question the two provisions put to each other, and the amendment inserted both without saying how they interact. The narrower of the two possible answers is that section 14A assists on issues within a suit and does not convert an unsupervisable duty into a supervisable one. The wider is that the bar in clause (b) is expressed as a statement about the court's capacity, and a provision that enlarges that capacity necessarily narrows the bar.

A smaller loss travelled with sub-section (3). Its clause (c)(ii) contained the only statutory formulation anywhere in the Act of a plaintiff's "substantial interest" in performance, and it was the third of the three places in which adequacy of money relief had operated as a test.

## Averment and proof under section 16

Section 7 of the amending Act made three changes to section 16, and the two that concern clause (c) are changes of a single word each. In clause (c), "who fails to aver and prove" became "who fails to prove". In clause (ii) of the Explanation, "the plaintiff must aver performance of, or readiness and willingness to perform, the contract according to its true construction" became "must prove".

The result is that the Act now requires the plaintiff to prove his readiness and willingness twice, in the clause and again in the Explanation, and to plead it nowhere. It does not follow that the pleading is dispensed with. Order VI Rule 2(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 requires every pleading to contain a statement in concise form of the material facts on which the party pleading relies, and readiness and willingness to perform is a material fact of a claim for specific performance, not an incident of its proof. What the amendment removes is a special statutory requirement to aver, not the general one.

Whether it removes anything else is a question the text leaves open, and the two readings available have different consequences at different stages. On the narrower reading, the amendment deletes a redundancy: the Act said twice what the Code says once, and the duplication has been tidied away. On the wider reading it does more, because a statutory direction that the plaintiff "must aver" is the kind of provision a court can treat as a condition of the claim, so that a plaint lacking the averment is defective by force of the Act itself. After the amendment the plaint is not defective by force of the Act; it is at most defective under the Code, where the ordinary consequence is an opportunity to amend rather than rejection. Nothing in the amendment indicates which reading was intended.

The third change to section 16 is of a different order and is not a change of a word. Clause (a) was not amended but substituted. It had read "who would not be entitled to recover compensation for its breach"; it now reads "who has obtained substituted performance of contract under section 20". A general personal bar, which excluded any plaintiff who could not have sued for damages, has been replaced by a specific one directed at a single situation. The Act did not add the new bar to the old; it put it in the old one's place.

## "In addition to": what is left of section 21

Section 11 of the amending Act is one line long. In section 21(1), for the words ", either in addition to, or in substitution of," the words "in addition to" were substituted. Nothing else in section 21 was touched.

What remains untouched matters more than what was changed. Sub-section (2) still provides that if, in such a suit, the court decides that specific performance ought not to be granted, but that there is a contract between the parties which has been broken by the defendant, and that the plaintiff is entitled to compensation for the breach, "it shall award him such compensation accordingly". Sub-section (5) still provides that no compensation shall be awarded under the section unless the plaintiff has claimed it in his plaint, with a proviso that where he has not, the court "shall, at any stage of the proceeding, allow him to amend the plaint on such terms as may be just". And the Explanation still provides that the circumstance that the contract has become incapable of specific performance does not preclude the court from exercising the jurisdiction the section confers.

Read together, the sub-sections no longer sit comfortably. Sub-section (1) permits the plaintiff to claim compensation only in addition to performance. Sub-section (2) presupposes a court that has decided performance ought not to be granted, and directs it to award compensation. Sub-section (5) makes a claim in the plaint the condition of any such award. The Explanation contemplates the jurisdiction being exercised in a case where performance has become impossible, which is the one case in which an additional claim can only ever operate as a substitute.

The reconciliation is available, and it is narrower than the deleted words suggest. A plaintiff may still claim compensation; he must frame the claim as additional to performance rather than as an alternative to it; and because sub-section (2) operates precisely when performance is refused, a claim framed as additional does the work that an alternative claim used to do. The amendment has changed the form of the prayer and not the availability of the award. Where it bites is on the plaintiff who asks for damages instead of performance and nothing else, and the proviso to sub-section (5) — mandatory in form, and exercisable at any stage — limits even that.

Section 21(2) has, however, been narrowed by something other than its own words. Its premise is a court deciding that performance "ought not to be granted", and under the new section 10 the occasions for such a decision are confined to the cases in which the Act still permits the decree to be withheld. A provision whose field depends on a discretion stated elsewhere contracts when that discretion is removed, although nothing in the provision itself has been altered.

## Substituted performance, and the cross-reference inside it

The new section 20 replaces a discretion of the court with an option of a party. Sub-section (1) gives the party who suffers by a breach, except as otherwise agreed, "the option of substituted performance through a third party or by his own agency", and the right to recover the expenses and costs actually incurred from the party in breach. Sub-section (2) forbids substituted performance unless the party suffering the breach has given written notice of not less than thirty days calling on the party in breach to perform within the time specified, and adds a proviso that the expenses and costs are not recoverable unless he has in fact had the contract performed. Sub-section (3) provides that where he has had it performed after giving notice, "he shall not be entitled to claim relief of specific performance against the party in breach". Sub-section (4) preserves his claim to compensation.

The remedy is therefore terminal rather than cumulative, and the Act says so three times over: in section 20(3), in section 14(a), and in the substituted section 16(a). A party who uses it has his money and not his performance.

One drafting point should be recorded because it affects the operation of the sub-sections rather than their elegance. The notice is required by sub-section (2). Sub-section (3) refers to the contract having been performed "after giving notice under sub-section (1)". There is no notice under sub-section (1). The condition in sub-section (3) is keyed to a provision that does not contain it, and the only reading that gives the sub-section work to do is one that treats the reference as being to the notice sub-section (2) requires.

## The infrastructure sections, and the one that is not about infrastructure

Sections 20A and 20B are expressly about a defined class of contracts. Section 20A bars a court from granting an injunction "in a suit under this Act involving a contract relating to an infrastructure project specified in the Schedule, where granting injunction would cause impediment or delay in the progress or completion of such infrastructure project", and its Explanation supplies the meaning of "infrastructure project" for that section, for section 20B and for the new clause (ha) of section 41. Section 20B requires the State Government, in consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court, to designate one or more civil courts as Special Courts to try suits under the Act in respect of such contracts.

The bar is written twice, and the second writing is wider than the first. Section 20A operates only in a suit under the Specific Relief Act. The new section 41(ha) provides that an injunction cannot be granted "if it would impede or delay the progress or completion of any infrastructure project or interfere with the continued provision of relevant facility related thereto or services being the subject matter of such project", and section 41 governs the grant of injunctions generally. The wider bar takes its central term from the Explanation to the narrower one, and the Schedule that defines the term is amendable by the Central Government by notification under section 20A(2), subject to the laying procedure in section 20A(3). The class of contracts in which no injunction may be granted is therefore fixed by the Act in form and expandable by the executive in substance.

Section 20C sits with those two sections and is not confined as they are:

> "20C. Notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, a suit filed under the provisions of this Act shall be disposed of by the court within a period of twelve months from the date of service of summons to the defendant: Provided that the said period may be extended for a further period not exceeding six months in aggregate after recording reasons in writing for such extension by the court."

The subject is "a suit filed under the provisions of this Act", not a suit relating to an infrastructure project. The Act's only disposal deadline is placed among three sections otherwise addressed to a defined class of contracts, and reaches every suit under the Act. What it does not do is attach anything to the eighteenth month. The twelve months may be extended only on reasons recorded in writing, and by not more than six months in aggregate; nothing is said about what follows if the eighteen months pass, and the Code which the section opens by overriding supplies no answer either. A direction to a court to dispose of a suit within a period, with no consequence attached to the period, operates as a standard of administration rather than as a right of a party.

## Which suits the amended sections govern

Section 1(2) of the amending Act left commencement to notification and permitted different dates for different provisions. The provisions were brought into force with effect from 1 October 2018, a date the Supreme Court recorded in 2022 as "the appointed date on which the amended provisions would come into effect".

The question that then arose is not answered anywhere in the amending Act. Nothing in it says whether the new provisions speak to contracts made after commencement, breaches committed after it, or suits instituted after it, and the three connecting factors give three different answers in any suit on an agreement of earlier date. The silence is the whole of the difficulty, and it is a structural difficulty rather than an accidental one: a provision that converts a discretionary remedy into an entitlement is procedural in its form, because it addresses what a court shall do, and substantive in its effect, because it withdraws from a defendant a defence he had when he contracted. Each characterisation carries its own presumption, and they point in opposite directions.

A Bench of three Judges answered it on 25 August 2022, in *Katta Sujatha Reddy v. Siddamsetty Infra Projects Pvt. Ltd.*, Civil Appeal Nos. 5822 and 5823 of 2022, decided by Ramana CJ, Krishna Murari and Hima Kohli JJ., holding that "the 2018 amendment to the Specific Relief Act is prospective and cannot apply to those transactions that took place prior to its coming into force". The connecting factor it chose was the transaction.

That judgment no longer stands. On 8 November 2024, in *M/s Siddamsetty Infra Projects Pvt. Ltd. v. Katta Sujatha Reddy*, 2024 INSC 861, a Bench of Chandrachud CJ, Pardiwala and Manoj Misra JJ. allowed review petitions in the same appeals, held that the errors apparent on the face of the record it had identified went "to the root of the reasoning on both the issues of limitation and specific performance", recalled the judgment of 25 August 2022 and restored the High Court's decree of 23 April 2021. The review did not decide the question of prospectivity. It proceeded on the opposite footing as an assumption, observing that "[e]ven assuming that the grant of relief of specific performance continued to be discretionary to a suit instituted before the date of the amendment", the earlier Bench had erred in its analysis of whether the discretion ought to have been exercised.

The position that results should be stated without gloss. The only authority that had decided which transactions the amended sections govern was recalled, and the judgment that recalled it expressly declined to decide the point, assuming the answer for the purpose of disposing of the review. An advocate asked today which version of section 10 governs a suit on an agreement of 2015 has a statute that does not say, and a judgment on the point that has been recalled.

## What the amendment left unprovided for

The 1963 Act asked four questions and kept each in its own section. The 2018 Act kept three of the sections and emptied the fourth. In the space where the fourth question stood it put substituted performance, which is not an answer to that question and not addressed to a court at all: it is a remedy a party takes for himself, before any suit, and taking it ends his claim to performance.

The result is an Act that is shorter, more decided, and easier to apply. Performance shall be enforced; three sections can stop it; and whether money would have done instead is no longer asked. For a plaintiff holding an agreement to sell and able to prove his own readiness, that is a straightforward improvement.

What the Act no longer contains is a provision addressed to the case the old section 20 existed for: a contract that was lawful when made, that remains lawful, that the plaintiff has performed his part of, and whose enforcement would now fall on the defendant with a weight neither party priced. In 1963 the Act named three versions of that case, bounded them with two Explanations, and declared the discretion capable of correction by a court of appeal. A court that encounters it in 2026 has section 9, which speaks of grounds under the law of contracts, and a section 14 of four clauses, none of which is about the defendant at all.

There is a serious case for narrowing what the old section permitted. A discretion stated in open terms gives a defendant a further issue on which to resist, and the three cases in sub-section (2) were open enough to be pleaded in almost any suit on an old agreement. But the way to narrow a discretion is to narrow its stated grounds, and what was done instead was to delete the section that stated them. The grounds did not move to section 14. They are not in section 9. On the text as it now stands, they are nowhere, and a submission that used to have a sub-section to cite now has only the proposition that courts of equity have always done this — which is an argument about the history of the jurisdiction, addressed to a statute that has just removed the only sentence describing it.
