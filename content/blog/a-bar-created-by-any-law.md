A court that has framed issues has decided what the suit is about. It has not yet decided the order in which to take what it has framed, and that second decision is not a matter of convenience. It determines how much of the case gets tried, how much evidence is recorded, and — if the first decision is wrong — whether the trial has to be held a second time.

Two considerations pull against each other here, and they do not reconcile. The first is economy. If a suit is barred, or if the court cannot entertain it at all, then every day spent recording evidence on the merits is wasted, and the party made to lead that evidence has been put to an expense the law never required of it. On this view a court that can see a short answer should take it, and take it first.

The second consideration is the cost of being wrong at the end. A suit disposed of on one point produces findings on one point. If the appellate court takes a different view of that point, there is nothing on the record for it to decide the suit upon, and the case goes back for a trial that could have been held once. Economy at the trial stage is therefore conditional. It is a saving only if the view taken of the single point survives the appeal, and a point worth taking is one on which the appellate court may differ.

The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 has answered this question twice, in opposite directions, and the present answer is the later of the two. Order XIV Rule 2 as it now stands is not an unqualified preference for either value. It states a default and then names, exhaustively, the two cases in which the default gives way.

## The rule the Code used to contain

The rule before the amendment of 1976 was short, and it is worth reading in the form in which the Supreme Court set it out in *Nusli Neville Wadia v. Ivory Properties*, decided on 4 October 2019:

> "2. Where issues both of law and of fact arise in the same suit, and the Court is of opinion that the case or any part thereof may be disposed of on the issues of law only, it shall try those issues first, and for that purpose may, if it thinks fit, postpone the settlement of the issues of fact until after the issues of law have been determined."

Three features of that text decide its operation. The condition is the court's own opinion that the case, or any part of it, may be disposed of on the issues of law only. The consequence, once that opinion is formed, is expressed as "shall try those issues first" — not a liberty but a direction. And the class of issues eligible for the treatment is unrestricted as to subject: any issue of law would do, provided the case or part of it could be disposed of on it.

So the old rule chose economy, chose it in mandatory language, and placed no limit on which questions of law could be taken first. The only limit was the one built into the condition, and that limit had to be worked out by the courts.

## The ceiling the Supreme Court read into it

It was worked out early. In *Major S. S. Khanna v. Brig. F. J. Dillon*, decided on 14 August 1963 and reported at AIR 1964 SC 497, the trial court had tried as a preliminary issue, at the defendant's own request, whether a suit by one partner against another in respect of advances from partnership assets was maintainable. It held the suit not maintainable. To reach that holding it had assumed the truth of the defendant's version of the facts and the falsity of the plaintiff's.

The Supreme Court held the course impermissible, and stated the limit in a sentence the Court has since treated as unaffected by the change in the rule:

> "The jurisdiction to try issues of law apart from the issues of fact may be exercised only where in the opinion of the Court the whole suit may be disposed of on the issues of law alone, but the Code confers no jurisdiction upon the Court to try a suit on mixed issues of law and fact as preliminary issues."

And on why:

> "Normally all the issues in a suit should be tried by the Court; not to do so, especially when the decision on issues even of law depend upon the decision of issues of fact, would result in a lop-sided trial of the suit."

The reasoning does not depend on the words of the rule so much as on what a preliminary issue is for. The economy a preliminary issue buys comes entirely from not trying the facts. An issue whose answer depends on facts therefore buys nothing: either the court records the evidence, in which case the trial has happened and the issue was not preliminary, or it assumes the facts, in which case the decision rests on an assumption and will not hold. The trial court in *Khanna* had taken the second course, and the Court described what it had done as acting illegally and with material irregularity in the exercise of its jurisdiction.

What *Khanna* did not do — because the rule as it then stood gave no occasion for it — was limit the preliminary issue by subject matter. Any pure question of law capable of disposing of the whole suit qualified.

## What the amendment of 1976 inverted

Order XIV Rule 2 was substituted by section 64 of the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1976, with effect from 1 February 1977. The substituted rule reads:

> "2. Court to pronounce judgment on all issues.—(1) Notwithstanding that a case may be disposed of on a preliminary issue, the Court shall, subject to the provisions of sub-rule (2), pronounce judgment on all issues.
>
> (2) Where issues both of law and of fact arise in the same suit, and the Court is of opinion that the case or any part thereof may be disposed of on an issue of law only, it may try that issue first if the issue relates to—
>
> (a) the jurisdiction of the Court, or
>
> (b) a bar to the suit created by any law for the time being in force,
>
> and for that purpose may, if it thinks fit, postpone the settlement of the other issues until after that issue has been determined, and may deal with the suit in accordance with the decision on that issue."

Two of the three features have been altered, and altered in the same direction. The condition has not.

The direction is now in sub-rule (1), and it points the other way. The court shall pronounce judgment on all issues, and it shall do so notwithstanding that the case may be disposed of on a preliminary issue. The clause beginning "notwithstanding" is doing real work: it concedes that a case may be capable of disposal on one issue and directs the court to decide the rest anyway.

The old mandate has become a liberty. Where the conditions of sub-rule (2) are met the court "may try that issue first". Nothing compels it to. A trial court that declines to take a pure question of jurisdiction first, and tries the suit out, has not disobeyed the rule.

And the liberty is confined by subject matter for the first time. The issue of law must relate to the jurisdiction of the court, or to a bar to the suit created by any law for the time being in force. The list has two entries and no residuary clause.

In *Wadia* the Court described the change and the reason for it:

> "A significant departure has been made in the amended provisions contained in Order XIV Rule 2. Now it mandates the Court to pronounce judgment on all issues notwithstanding that a case may be disposed of on a preliminary issue. The intendment is to avoid remand in the appealable case for deciding the other issues."

That identifies the value the amendment preferred, and it is the second of the two with which this note began. The Code now treats the second trial as the more serious cost, and guards against it by requiring findings on everything.

## The remand the rule is designed to prevent

What the rule guards against is identified elsewhere in the Code in terms. Order XLI Rule 23 provides:

> "23. Remand of case by Appellate Court.—Where the Court from whose decree an appeal is preferred has disposed of the suit upon a preliminary point and the decree is reversed in appeal, the Appellate Court may, if it thinks fit, by order remand the case, and may further direct what issue or issues shall be tried in the case so remanded..."

The rule is addressed to one situation only: a suit disposed of upon a preliminary point, and a decree reversed. Its companion, Rule 23A, inserted by section 87 of the same amending Act of 1976, gives the appellate court the same power where the decree under appeal was passed otherwise than on a preliminary point and a re-trial is considered necessary. The amending Act therefore worked on both ends of the problem at once: it narrowed the occasions on which a trial court may dispose of a suit on a preliminary point, and it put the appellate power of remand on the same footing whether or not the decree was passed on such a point.

For a litigant the consequence is straightforward. A preliminary issue decided in the defendant's favour, if the appellate court takes a different view of it, converts into a remand, and the remand produces the trial the preliminary issue was meant to avoid — held later, often before a different presiding officer. The saving is therefore contingent on the point holding on appeal.

## The limit the amendment did not touch

The amendment restricted the class of eligible issues. It did not relax the older requirement that the issue be one of law, and the words "on an issue of law only" carry the same weight in the new rule as "on the issues of law only" carried in the old.

This was put beyond argument in *Wadia*. The reference before the three-Judge Bench concerned section 9A of the Code as it applied in Maharashtra — a State provision, since deleted, which required that where an objection to the court's jurisdiction to entertain the suit was taken at the hearing of an application for granting or setting aside interim relief, the court determine that issue as a preliminary issue before granting or setting aside the relief — but the Court dealt with Order XIV Rule 2 in its own terms, and held:

> "Two full-fledged trials by leading evidence are not contemplated in CPC, one of the preliminary issue and another on other issues. Until and unless the question is pure of the law, it cannot be decided as a preliminary issue. In our opinion, a mixed question of law and fact cannot be decided as a preliminary issue, either under Section 9A or under Order XIV Rule 2 CPC. Before or after its amendment of CPC concerning both provisions, the position is the same."

The last sentence is the operative one for present purposes. The 1976 amendment changed the default and closed the list; it did not alter the ceiling *Khanna* had identified thirteen years earlier. A defendant seeking a preliminary trial must therefore clear two independent gates, and the gates are of different kinds. The first is a question of classification: is this issue one that relates to jurisdiction, or to a statutory bar? The second is a question of proof: can it be answered without recording evidence? An issue may pass the first and fail the second, and most of the issues that are worth raising do precisely that.

## Why clause (b) is narrower than it looks

Clause (b) appears to be the generous one. Clause (a) names a single subject, the jurisdiction of the court. Clause (b) names a whole class — every bar to a suit created by any law for the time being in force — and so takes in statutory notice provisions, want of sanction, non-compliance with a condition precedent, a statutory ouster of the civil court's cognisance, and limitation.

The practical operation is the reverse, and the reason is the second gate. Jurisdiction, in the ordinary case, is determinable from materials the court already has. Pecuniary jurisdiction turns on the valuation stated in the plaint. Territorial jurisdiction turns on where the property is or where the cause of action is pleaded to have arisen. Jurisdiction over the subject matter turns on the nature of the relief claimed. None of these ordinarily requires evidence, because the plaintiff's own pleading supplies the facts, and the defendant who disputes jurisdiction is usually disputing the legal consequence of those facts rather than the facts themselves.

The statutory bars are not like that. Almost every one of them has a factual trigger. Whether a notice was given, and when, and in what terms, is a fact. Whether sanction was obtained is a fact. Whether a condition precedent was performed is a fact. And whether a suit is within time depends on when the right to sue accrued, which in many civil suits is itself the central disputed fact.

So the clause that names the entire universe of statutory bars yields fewer preliminary trials than the clause that names one thing. The breadth of clause (b) is breadth of category; the gate that matters is the one about evidence, and the categories in clause (b) sit on the wrong side of it.

## Limitation: the clearest bar, and the hardest preliminary issue

Limitation is the sharpest illustration, because its own statute states the bar in unusually strong terms. Section 3(1) of the Limitation Act, 1963 provides:

> "3. Bar of limitation.—(1) Subject to the provisions contained in sections 4 to 24 (inclusive), every suit instituted, appeal preferred, and application made after the prescribed period shall be dismissed, although limitation has not been set up as a defence."

Three things follow from that sub-section. The consequence is dismissal, and it is mandatory. It operates on the institution of the suit, not on the conduct of the defence. And it binds the court independently of the pleadings: the suit shall be dismissed although limitation has not been set up as a defence, which makes the question one the court must reach whether or not any party asks it to.

Nothing in the language of Order XIV Rule 2(2)(b) excludes such a provision. A statute that directs that a suit instituted after the prescribed period shall be dismissed creates a bar to the suit, and it is a law for the time being in force. Limitation passes the first gate without difficulty.

It is at the second gate that it ordinarily stops. In *Ramesh B. Desai v. Bipin Vadilal Mehta*, decided on 11 July 2006 and reported at (2006) 5 SCC 638, the Supreme Court put the point in two sentences:

> "A plea of limitation cannot be decided as an abstract principle of law divorced from facts as in every case the starting point of limitation has to be ascertained which is entirely a question of fact. A plea of limitation is a mixed question of law and fact."

The reasoning is structural rather than evidentiary. The Limitation Act prescribes periods; it does not prescribe starting points in the abstract. The third column of the Schedule is headed "Time from which period begins to run", and what it sets against each description of suit is an event — the date of the deed, the date of dispossession, the date when the money became due, the date when the right to sue accrued. The sections to which section 3(1) is expressly made subject, sections 4 to 24, then supply the circumstances in which the running of time is postponed, extended, excluded or computed afresh — legal disability under section 6, time spent prosecuting a proceeding bona fide in a court without jurisdiction under section 14, fraud or mistake under section 17, an acknowledgment in writing under section 18, a payment on account of a debt under section 19. Every one of these is a fact to be pleaded and proved.

A limitation point is therefore a composite. The period is law and is rarely in dispute. The event from which the period runs is fact and is frequently the whole dispute. A court invited to decide limitation as a preliminary issue is being invited either to take evidence on that event — which is not a preliminary trial — or to assume it, which is what the trial court did in *Khanna* and what the Supreme Court said it had no jurisdiction to do.

The result is an inversion worth stating plainly. Section 3(1) makes limitation the court's own duty, owed irrespective of the defence. Order XIV Rule 2 makes it, in most suits, the last thing the court decides rather than the first.

## The other short route, and its narrower aperture

A defendant who cannot obtain a preliminary issue may think of Order VII Rule 11, which operates earlier still. Clause (d) provides for rejection of the plaint "where the suit appears from the statement in the plaint to be barred by any law".

The phrase "barred by any law" closely tracks clause (b) of Order XIV Rule 2(2), and so the class of bars is much the same. The difference is in the seven words that precede it. The bar must appear *from the statement in the plaint*. That is not a rule about what kinds of questions may be taken early; it is a rule about which document the court may look at.

In *Ramesh B. Desai* the Court stated the consequence:

> "The principle is, therefore, well settled that in order to examine whether the plaint is barred by any law, as contemplated by sub-rule (d) of Order VII Rule 11 CPC, the averments made in the plaint alone have to be seen and they have to be assumed to be correct. It is not permissible to look into the pleas raised in the written statement or to any piece of evidence."

The Court was there applying what had been held in *Popat and Kotecha Property v. State Bank of India Staff Association*, (2005) 7 SCC 510, that clause (d) applies only where the statement made by the plaintiff in the plaint, without any doubt or dispute, shows that the suit is barred by any law in force; and it quoted its own earlier order in *Balasaria Construction Pvt. Ltd. v. Hanuman Seva Trust*, decided on 8 November 2005, that a suit could not be dismissed as barred by limitation without proper pleadings, the framing of an issue of limitation and the taking of evidence.

So the two short routes are closed at the same place by different means. Order XIV Rule 2(2) admits a limitation issue into its category and then excludes it wherever the answer needs facts. Order VII Rule 11(d) admits it as a bar and then confines the court to the plaint, which is the one document in which a plaintiff with a limitation problem will have pleaded around it. A defendant whose point depends on dates established by the written statement, by documents, or by evidence has no short route at all — not because the point is weak, but because neither provision has a mechanism for receiving the material on which it rests.

The exception is the case where the plaintiff's own averments settle the matter. Where the plaint itself discloses the accrual date and the suit is on its face beyond the prescribed period, with nothing pleaded to save it, the bar appears from the statement in the plaint and the issue is one of law on admitted facts. That case exists and is worth looking for before the application is drafted, because it is the only version of the point that either provision is equipped to decide.

## What this means for the order in which a trial court works

Read together, Rule 2(1) and Rule 2(2) produce a sequence that is more rigid than the language of liberty in sub-rule (2) suggests.

The court's duty at the end of the trial is fixed by sub-rule (1): judgment on all issues. The exception in sub-rule (2) operates earlier, at the stage of deciding what to try first, and it is available only where the issue falls in one of two categories and can be answered without evidence. A trial court that disposes of a suit on an issue outside those categories has not merely exercised a discretion differently; it has decided the suit in a manner sub-rule (1) directed it not to, and the decree it passes carries that defect into the appeal, where Order XLI Rule 23 supplies the remedy of remand.

This also explains the form an order refusing a preliminary issue usually takes. The court refusing it is generally not saying the point is bad. It is saying that the point cannot be decided in the form in which it has been presented, and that the issue will be framed and tried along with the rest. That is not a loss on the question of limitation or of bar. It is a ruling on sequence, and the point survives it intact.

## Three things that follow at the Bar

**Classify the point before applying, and classify it under both gates.** An application under Order XIV Rule 2(2) has to establish two things: that the issue relates to the jurisdiction of the court or to a bar to the suit created by a law in force, and that it can be answered on admitted facts or on the pleadings without evidence. The first is usually easy to show and is not what the application turns on. Where the second cannot be shown, the application will be dismissed and will have told the plaintiff, months before the trial, exactly which fact the defence most needs. Where the point is a pure question of law on admitted facts, say so in terms, identify the admissions by reference to the paragraphs that contain them, and ask for the issue to be tried first on that basis alone.

**For limitation, work from the plaint before working from the point.** Both available routes are closed to material outside the plaintiff's own pleading — Order VII Rule 11(d) by its express words, and Order XIV Rule 2(2) in practical effect, because the accrual date is a fact. The question to ask first is therefore not whether the suit is barred but whether the plaint shows that it is barred. If the accrual date appears in the plaint and nothing is pleaded to save the period, there is an application worth making. If the date has to be established from documents or from evidence, the issue should be framed and tried with the others, and the time spent on an application that cannot succeed is better spent on the pleading of the accrual date in the written statement.

**Plead limitation even where no preliminary trial is possible, and plead the date.** Section 3(1) of the Limitation Act obliges the court to dismiss a suit instituted after the prescribed period although limitation has not been set up as a defence, so the point is not lost by want of pleading in the sense that an ordinary defence is lost. But the court can only perform that duty on the material before it, and the fact on which it depends — the date from which the period runs — is one the plaintiff has no interest in establishing. A written statement that pleads the bar without pleading the date, or pleads the date without pleading the article relied on, leaves the court holding a duty it has no means of discharging, and leaves the defendant arguing at the stage of final judgment a question he could have had framed as an issue at the outset.
