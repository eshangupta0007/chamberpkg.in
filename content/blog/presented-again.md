A Bill that has passed a legislature is not yet law. Something further has to happen to it, and in every constitution descended from the Westminster arrangement what has to happen is that someone outside the legislature assents. The requirement looks like a formality and is not one. An assent that could never be refused would not need to be provided for; an assent that could be refused without limit would mean that the legislature does not finally legislate.

So a constitution that requires assent has to answer a second question, and the second is harder than the first. Not whether the head of State may refuse — every constitution in this family allows refusal in some form — but when the refusal stops. A legislature that can be told to think again has lost nothing. A legislature that can be told to think again indefinitely has lost the power to decide. The weight of the arrangement rests on the point at which the Bill stops moving back and forth and becomes law, and on who is in a position to postpone that point.

The Constitution of India answers this question three separate times: for Parliament, for a State legislature whose Bill stays in the State, and for a State legislature whose Bill does not. The first two answers are the same and are clean. The third is different, and the difference is not in any clause drafted to create it. It is in what the third provision leaves out.

## What the three articles provide

Article 111 deals with a Bill passed by the Houses of Parliament. It is presented to the President, "and the President shall declare either that he assents to the Bill, or that he withholds assent therefrom". Two declarations, and no third. The proviso then supplies the thinking-again mechanism: the President may, "as soon as possible after the presentation to him of a Bill for assent", return the Bill if it is not a Money Bill to the Houses with a message requesting that they reconsider it or specified provisions of it, and in particular consider the desirability of amendments he may recommend. The Houses reconsider. And then the proviso closes: "if the Bill is passed again by the Houses with or without amendment and presented to the President for assent, the President shall not withhold assent therefrom."

That is a complete answer. The President may refuse, and may ask for reconsideration, and once reconsideration has happened the refusal is spent. The legislature has the last word by passing the Bill twice.

Article 200 deals with a Bill passed by the Legislative Assembly of a State, or by both Houses where the State has a Legislative Council. It is presented to the Governor, who "shall declare either that he assents to the Bill or that he withholds assent therefrom or that he reserves the Bill for the consideration of the President". Three declarations, not two. The first proviso then reproduces Article 111's mechanism almost word for word, down to the "as soon as possible" and the recommended amendments, and closes the same way: if the Bill is passed again and presented, "the Governor shall not withhold assent therefrom". A second proviso makes one reservation compulsory, and is taken up further below.

To this point the two articles are the same arrangement with one extra option added at the State level. That extra option is the subject of everything that follows.

Article 201 deals with what happens when the extra option is taken. "When a Bill is reserved by a Governor for the consideration of the President, the President shall declare either that he assents to the Bill or that he withholds assent therefrom" — two declarations again, as under Article 111. Then the proviso, which is where the articles part company. Where the Bill is not a Money Bill, the President "may direct the Governor to return the Bill" to the House or Houses with such a message as is mentioned in the first proviso to Article 200. The House or Houses "shall reconsider it accordingly within a period of six months from the date of receipt of such message". And if it is again passed, with or without amendment, "it shall be presented again to the President for his consideration".

That is where Article 201 ends. There is no closing clause. The sentence that appears in Article 111 and again in the first proviso to Article 200 — that the authority shall not withhold assent from a Bill passed a second time — has no counterpart here.

## The second passage, and what it settles

It is worth being precise about what that missing clause does in the two places where it exists.

It does not oblige the head of State to agree with the Bill. It does not make the return mechanism pointless: the message, the recommended amendments and the reconsideration all still happen, and a House that is persuaded may amend the Bill, which is why both provisions say "with or without amendment". What the clause does is convert a discretion into a duty at a fixed moment. Before the second passage, assent is something the authority decides. After it, assent is something the authority performs.

This is the only device in the assent machinery that makes the legislature's will final, and the Constitution offers no substitute for it. There is no provision anywhere that a Bill becomes law if assent is not given within some period, and none that assent is deemed. The Constitution does not make a Bill law without the declaration; it makes the declaration compulsory. Everything therefore depends on the clause that compels the declaration, and on reaching the moment at which that clause bites.

Under Article 201 the moment does not arrive. The Bill is passed, reserved, returned, reconsidered and passed again, and at the end of it the text says only that it shall be presented again "for his consideration" — the same word the article uses for the first presentation, describing the same undertaking. The second passage returns the Bill to where it already was. On the words, the President may then declare either of the two things Article 201 permitted him to declare the first time.

## Reserving is not withholding

The natural objection is that this makes too much of an omission, and that a Governor's second-round obligation under Article 200 must carry through to the President. The structure of Article 200 makes that reading difficult, because the article itself keeps the two acts apart.

Article 200 lists three declarations and they do different work. Assent makes the Bill law. Withholding ends it. Reserving does neither: it moves the Bill to another authority for a decision not yet taken. Withholding and reserving are listed as alternatives, which means they are not the same act, and the Constitution attaches different consequences to each — nothing follows a withholding, whereas a reservation triggers Article 201.

That distinction cuts in both directions, and the second direction is the more striking. The first proviso to Article 200 bars one thing after a second passage: the Governor "shall not withhold assent therefrom". It does not say that he shall assent. It bars the second of his three declarations and is silent about the third. On the words alone, a Governor presented with a Bill his legislature has now passed twice is forbidden to end it and is not forbidden to reserve it — in which case the Bill proceeds to the President under Article 201, arriving as a Bill whose second passage has already occurred and whose conclusiveness has been spent against an authority who is no longer the one deciding.

Nothing in that paragraph is a statement of law. How the proviso is to be read is a question for a court, the answer is not supplied by the words, and this post takes no position on it. The point is narrower and is about drafting. A conclusiveness expressed as a bar on one named option is conclusive only against that option, and Article 200 is the single assent provision in the Constitution that offers more than one way to decline. The phrase "shall declare either" occurs five times in the Constitution — in Article 111, in Article 200, in Article 201, and twice in the Sixth Schedule. Article 200 is the only one of the five that follows it with three alternatives rather than two.

## The one period the articles state

The assent machinery contains exactly one stated period of time, and where the draftsman placed it is instructive.

Articles 111 and 200 both qualify the return option with "as soon as possible after the presentation". The phrase governs the proviso, not the main clause. It tells the President or the Governor to be prompt in exercising the power to send a Bill back. It says nothing about the time within which the declaration required by the main clause — assent, or withholding, or in a Governor's case reservation — must be made. The step that carries a temporal qualifier is the optional one.

Article 201 contains no such phrase at all. What it contains is a fixed period of six months, stated with precision and running from a definite event, the date of receipt of the message. That period applies to the House or Houses of the State legislature, which "shall reconsider it accordingly within a period of six months". It is a deadline on the body that has already passed the Bill once.

So the only authority in this machinery that the Constitution puts under a time limit is the legislature. The Governor, who may reserve, is under none. The President, who must then declare, is under none. The State legislature, which has done the thing the Constitution exists to let it do, is under one — and if it overruns, the proviso attaches no consequence to that either.

This is not the accident of a single article. It is what follows from securing the finality of legislation through a duty to assent rather than through a lapse of time. A duty needs a trigger, and the trigger is the second passage. Where the second passage carries no closing clause, nothing remains: no duty, and no clock to stand in for one.

## The Bills that get no second passage at all

There is one class of Bill for which the device does not merely stop short. It never starts.

All three return mechanisms exclude Money Bills, and they do it in identical terms. Article 111's proviso permits the President to "return the Bill if it is not a Money Bill". The first proviso to Article 200 permits the Governor to "return the Bill if it is not a Money Bill". Article 201's proviso operates only "where the Bill is not a Money Bill". For a Money Bill, therefore, there is no message, no reconsideration and no second passage — and because the duty to assent is triggered by the second passage, there is nothing to trigger it. The authority holds the Bill with the main clause's declarations available to it and no clause anywhere obliging it to choose one of them.

There is a respectable reason for the carve-out. A Money Bill is a Bill on which the Constitution has already given the popular chamber the decisive voice, and inviting the head of State to recommend amendments to it would cut across that. The reason explains the exclusion of the return power. It does not explain why the exclusion of the return power should also carry away the only clause that makes assent compulsory, because the two are joined in a single proviso and the draftsman removed both together. The result is that the class of Bills over which the legislature's control is most complete is the class on which the external assent is least constrained.

It is worth noting who makes the classification that produces this result. Under Article 199(3), where a State has a Legislative Council, "the decision of the Speaker of the Legislative Assembly of such State thereon shall be final". Under Article 199(4), the Speaker's certificate that a Bill is a Money Bill is endorsed on it when it is transmitted to the Council and again "when it is presented to the Governor for assent under article 200". So the certificate that withdraws the return mechanism, and with it the duty to assent, travels with the Bill and is signed by an officer of the legislature whose Bill it is. The legislature determines, conclusively, which of its own Bills will reach the Governor stripped of the device that would have made its will final.

## The subjects on which reservation is not optional

Article 200's second proviso makes one reservation mandatory. The Governor "shall not assent to, but shall reserve for the consideration of the President, any Bill which in the opinion of the Governor would, if it became law, so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position which that Court is by this Constitution designed to fill."

Two features deserve notice. The first is that the clause removes assent as an option, not merely withholding: the Governor is forbidden to assent even if he wishes to. The second is the subject matter. The one Bill the Constitution compels a Governor to send out of the State is a Bill about the High Court — an institution of the State in one sense and of the Union's judicial structure in another — and the route it compels delivers that Bill to the authority under whose consideration no second passage is conclusive.

Reservation is required in substance by several further provisions scattered across the Constitution, and in each the pattern is the same: the State's law has no effect, or forfeits a protection, unless it has been reserved and has received the President's assent. Under the first proviso to Article 31A, the saving that article gives to laws acquiring estates does not apply to a State law "unless such law, having been reserved for the consideration of the President, has received his assent". The proviso to Article 31C says the same of a State law claiming that article's protection. Under Article 254(2), a State law on a Concurrent List subject repugnant to an earlier Union law prevails in that State only "if it has been reserved for the consideration of the President and has received his assent" — subject to a proviso preserving Parliament's power to legislate again on the same matter, including by a law "adding to, amending, varying or repealing the law so made by the Legislature of the State". Under Article 288(2), where a State taxes the water or electricity of an inter-State authority, "no such law shall have any effect unless it has, after having been reserved for the consideration of the President, received his assent".

These are not the same species of provision as Article 200's second proviso. There the Governor's opinion triggers a duty to reserve. Here the Constitution states a condition on the law's operation, which the State must satisfy if it wants the result. But they end in the same place, and they are the provisions on which the asymmetry does its work. A State that wishes to legislate against an earlier Union law on a Concurrent subject has exactly one route, Article 254(2), and that route runs through Article 201.

Article 360(4)(a)(ii) supplies a further occasion. While a Proclamation of financial emergency is in operation, a direction to a State may include "a provision requiring all Money Bills or other Bills to which the provisions of article 207 apply to be reserved for the consideration of the President after they are passed by the Legislature of the State". The Bills that clause reaches are the State's financial Bills — and Article 201's proviso is available only "where the Bill is not a Money Bill". So in that condition reservation becomes compulsory for a class of Bills from which the return mechanism is itself withdrawn, leaving the President with the two declarations of the main clause and nothing in between them.

## What assent cures, and what it does not

The Constitution contains one provision treating a defect in this area as curable, and what it omits from its list is as informative as what it includes.

Article 255 is headed "Requirements as to recommendations and previous sanctions to be regarded as matters of procedure only". It provides that no Act of Parliament or of a State legislature, and no provision in such an Act, "shall be invalid by reason only that some recommendation or previous sanction required by this Constitution was not given", if assent to the Act was given — where the Governor's recommendation was required, by the Governor or by the President; where the President's recommendation or previous sanction was required, by the President.

The Constitution does require recommendations and previous sanctions in a number of places. Article 207(1) requires the Governor's recommendation before a State financial Bill is introduced or moved. The proviso to Article 304(b) requires that no Bill or amendment imposing restrictions on trade in the public interest "shall be introduced or moved in the Legislature of a State without the previous sanction of the President". Article 255 makes a failure in either a procedural defect, washed out by the assent that follows.

Reservation is not in Article 255's list, and could not sensibly be placed there. A recommendation or previous sanction is a step before the Bill's passage, and assent afterwards demonstrates that the authority whose permission was wanted had no objection. Reservation is not a preliminary to assent. It is the mechanism that determines whose assent the Bill needs. A Bill that should have been reserved and was not has not been assented to by the wrong authority in some curable way; it has been assented to by an authority whose assent the relevant provision does not accept.

The contrast is worth stating plainly, because it shows what the draftsman grasped. Where a requirement existed only to secure someone's prior agreement, the Constitution said that later agreement would do. Where the requirement existed to route the Bill to a particular decider, it said nothing of the kind. The routing is treated as substantive. And the authority to whom the Bill is routed is the one whose consideration no second passage concludes.

## The same sentence, in a schedule

The arrangement is reproduced below the State, which indicates a pattern followed rather than a clause overlooked.

Paragraph 3A of the Sixth Schedule, inserted in its application to the State of Assam by the Sixth Schedule to the Constitution (Amendment) Act, 1995, confers additional law-making powers on the North Cachar Hills Autonomous Council and the Karbi Anglong Autonomous Council. Laws so made, so far as they relate to Concurrent List matters, shall "be submitted forthwith to the Governor who shall reserve the same for the consideration of the President". Reservation here is not an option at all: the Governor has no declaration to make and no choice to exercise. Sub-paragraph (3) then provides that the President "shall declare either that he assents to the said law or that he withholds assent therefrom", with a proviso allowing him to direct the Governor to return the law to the Council, which shall consider it within six months, and if it is again passed "it shall be presented again to the President for his consideration". Paragraph 3B, inserted in the same manner by the Sixth Schedule to the Constitution (Amendment) Act, 2003, does the same for the Bodoland Territorial Council.

The wording tracks Article 201 closely — the six-month period on the reconsidering body, the final presentation for consideration, and the absence of any closing obligation. Three legislative bodies in two tiers reach the end of their second passage in the same place, and none of them reaches it with a duty to assent.

The Sixth Schedule is also where the alternative technique can be seen, which makes the choice in Article 201 easier to read. For ordinary laws and regulations the Schedule repeatedly uses a different formula: they shall be submitted forthwith to the Governor, or in some cases to the President, and "until assented to by him, shall have no effect". That formula states a condition on the law's operation and provides no mechanism whatever — no return, no reconsideration, no period, and no duty. It is the weaker of the two devices, and it is used for the weaker bodies. Article 201 uses the stronger device's machinery and stops short of its conclusion.

Article 213 shows the same structure from the other side. A Governor may not promulgate an Ordinance without instructions from the President in three cases that correspond to reservation: where a Bill containing the same provisions would have required the President's previous sanction for introduction; where the Governor "would have deemed it necessary to reserve a Bill containing the same provisions for the consideration of the President"; and where an Act containing the same provisions would have been invalid unless reserved and assented to. The Union's involvement therefore follows the subject matter rather than the instrument. What an Act must obtain after passage, an Ordinance must obtain before promulgation, and the subjects on which a State cannot legislate alone are the subjects on which it cannot ordain alone.

## Whose decision it is

An objection arrives at this point which is worth taking seriously, because it is the one most likely to be made. Neither the Governor nor the President decides anything personally. Both are heads of State in a parliamentary system, both have a Council of Ministers, and the gap identified above is therefore not a gap in anybody's unchecked discretion — it is the ordinary arrangement by which an executive acts.

The aid-and-advise articles are not drafted alike, which matters. Article 163(1) provides a Council of Ministers to aid and advise the Governor "except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions or any of them in his discretion", and clause (2) adds that where a question arises whether a matter is one for his discretion, "the decision of the Governor in his discretion shall be final". Article 74(1) contains no such exception: the Council of Ministers aids and advises the President, "who shall, in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice", subject to a proviso permitting him to require one reconsideration, after which "the President shall act in accordance with the advice tendered after such reconsideration."

Take the strongest version of the objection, then — that Article 74(1) binds the President absolutely, and that a Bill reserved under Article 200 is decided by the Union Council of Ministers. The objection succeeds on the question it addresses and does not touch the question here. Article 74(1) states whose will prevails. It states nothing about when. There is no period within which advice must be tendered, none within which it must be acted on, and the proviso's reconsideration carries no period either. So on the strongest reading the discretion is relocated from the President to the Union Council of Ministers and arrives there with no deadline attached, which leaves the State's Bill waiting on a different body for an unstated time.

Two further clauses close off the obvious check. Article 74(2) provides that the question what advice was tendered to the President "shall not be inquired into in any court", and Article 163(3) says the same of advice to a Governor. Whatever record would show when a decision became due, and whether one was taken, is therefore not available by that route. The aid-and-advise articles answer who decides. They were not drafted to answer when, and they do not.

## The limit the text does not contain

Nothing above depends on how any of these powers has in fact been exercised, and nothing above is a claim about what a court would hold. Both lie outside what the words can settle. What the words settle is the shape of the arrangement, and the shape has a gap in a locatable place.

A State Bill on a subject in which the Union has an interest reaches the President and waits. The article gives him two declarations and no deadline. If he returns it, the legislature is bound to reconsider within six months and does so, and the Bill comes back to him for consideration, which is where it began. The provision does not say he must then assent; does not require reasons for not assenting; does not deem assent after any period; and does not say he may not direct a second return. Each of those is a limit the Constitution imposed somewhere else — a duty to assent in Article 111 and in the first proviso to Article 200, a reason to be recorded in writing in the second proviso to Article 311(2), a fixed expiry of six weeks in Articles 123 and 213 — and none of them is imposed here.

That is the whole observation. Whatever constrains this power constrains it from outside the provision. The provision itself contains a period running against the legislature, an option the executive may exercise as soon as possible if it chooses to exercise it at all, and a final sentence that returns the Bill to the beginning.

## What the arrangement assumes

An assent provision has to assume something about the person assenting. The alternative is to draft a provision that works against a head of State determined to obstruct, and no constitution in this family has attempted it. Article 111 and the first proviso to Article 200 assume less than most: they assume only that the authority will perform a duty the text expressly imposes once the condition for it is satisfied. That is close to the minimum an arrangement of this kind can rest on.

Article 201 assumes more, and what it assumes is of a different type. It assumes that an authority under no duty to decide, no obligation to give reasons and no limit of time will nonetheless decide, and will decide within a period that leaves the State's legislation worth having. The assumption is not about good faith in the ordinary sense. It concerns how an office behaves when the text has withdrawn every mechanism by which behaviour is compelled.

The observation is narrower than a criticism. It is that the Constitution of India secured the finality of legislation by a single device — a duty to assent, triggered by a second passage — and wrote that device into every assent provision except the ones that route a Bill away from the legislature which passed it. At the Union, where there is nowhere further for a Bill to go, the second passage is conclusive. In a State it is conclusive against the Governor, who is the authority the second passage was drafted to bind. Where the Governor has instead sent the Bill out of the State, the second passage still happens, is recorded in the article, carries a deadline for the legislature, and concludes nothing.
