title: "Whether by Summoning Through Court or Otherwise: The Witness Order XVI Lets a Party Bring, and the Compulsion He Gives Up by Bringing Him"
excerpt: "Order XVI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 opens with a closed list and three short periods — fifteen days from the settlement of issues for the list, five days from the list for the summonses, seven days from the application for the expenses — and attaches a consequence to none of them. Rule 1A then lets a party dispense with the summons altogether and bring any witness to court. What the party gives up by doing so is everything coercive in the Order: the duty to attend under Rule 15, the duty to keep attending under Rule 16, the proclamation, the warrant, the attachment and the fine. The Order nowhere states the trade, and on the one default it does punish it now carries two ceilings ten times apart, because the 1999 amendment raised the figure in section 32 and left the figure in Rule 12 where it was."

A court that decides questions of fact needs people to come and speak to them. Almost none of those people are parties, almost none have any interest in coming, and the ones whose evidence matters most are often the ones with the strongest reason to stay away. So a procedural code has to confer two different things on a litigant, and it is worth separating them before reading the provisions that confer them. The first is permission — the court's leave to place a particular person's account on the record at all. The second is compulsion — the court's power to make that person turn up, stay, and answer.

These are not the same thing and they do not have the same natural owner. Permission is about the shape of the trial, which is the court's concern, because a party who could add witnesses at will could extend a suit indefinitely and ambush his opponent at the end of it. Compulsion is about force exercised against someone who is not a litigant at all, has done nothing wrong, and is being required to give up his time at a stranger's instance.

Order XVI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 does separate them, and it does so almost invisibly, because the separating provision is a single sentence inserted in 1976 that reads like a convenience. The consequence is that a party choosing how to bring a witness to court is choosing, without being told, how much of the court's power he will have over that witness once he gets there.

## The list, and the three periods it starts

Rule 1 was substituted in its entirety by the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1976, with effect from 1 February 1977, and it is the only place in the Order that imposes a timetable:

> "1. List of witnesses and summons to witnesses.—(1) On or before such date as the Court may appoint, and not later than fifteen days after the date on which the issues are settled, the parties shall present in Court a list of witnesses whom they propose to call either to give evidence or to produce documents and obtain summonses to such persons for their attendance in Court.
>
> (2) A party desirous of obtaining any summons for the attendance of any person shall file in Court an application stating therein the purpose for which the witness is proposed to be summoned.
>
> (3) The Court may, for reasons to be recorded, permit a party to call, whether by summoning through Court or otherwise, any witness, other than those whose names appear in the list referred to in sub-rule (1), if such party shows sufficient cause for the omission to mention the name of such witness in the said list.
>
> (4) Subject to the provisions of sub-rule (2), summonses referred to in this rule may be obtained by the parties on an application to the Court or to such officer as may be appointed by the Court in this behalf within five days of presenting the list of witnesses under sub-rule (1)."

The words at the end of sub-rule (4), which fix the five days, were themselves substituted by the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1999, with effect from 1 July 2002. A third period comes from the rule that follows, into which the 1999 Act inserted the bracketed words:

> "2. Expenses of witness to be paid into Court on applying for summons.—(1) The party applying for a summons shall, before the summons is granted and within a period to be fixed [which shall not be later than seven days from the date of making applications under sub-rule (4) of rule 1] pay into Court such a sum of money as appears to the Court to be sufficient to defray the travelling and other expenses of the person summoned in passing to and from the Court in which he is required to attend, and for one day's attendance."

So the machinery is meant to be set in motion on a measured schedule, and the schedule is additive. Fifteen days from the settlement of issues to file the list. Five days from the list to apply for the summonses. Seven days from that application to deposit the expenses. Twenty-seven days from the settlement of issues, and after that the Order expects every witness a party intends to call to have been named, applied for and paid for.

Three things about that timetable repay attention.

The first is that sub-rule (1) states two obligations in one sentence and only one of them is a filing. The parties shall "present in Court a list of witnesses" *and* "obtain summonses to such persons for their attendance in Court". The list and the summons are a single composite duty, and a party who files a list and stops there has done half of what the sub-rule directs.

The second is that the Order attaches nothing to the expiry of any of the three periods. It does not say what happens if the list is filed on the twentieth day, or the summons applied for on the tenth, or the expenses deposited on the fifteenth. Rule 2(1) does say the sum must be paid "before the summons is granted", which gives the deposit a practical sanction — the summons does not issue — but that sanction attaches to non-payment, not to late payment.

The third is that the consequence the Order does attach is attached to something else entirely. Sub-rule (3) is about names, not dates. It requires leave, sufficient cause and recorded reasons before a party may call "any witness, other than those whose names appear in the list referred to in sub-rule (1)". A party who files his list a month late has still filed a list, and the witnesses on it are not witnesses "other than those whose names appear in the list". A party who files on the fifteenth day and later wants a witness he did not name must satisfy sub-rule (3). The Order is exacting about the completeness of the list and silent about its punctuality, which is the opposite of the way the timetable reads.

## The sentence that lets a party out of the summons

Immediately after Rule 1 stands the provision that changes the character of the whole Order. It too was substituted by the 1976 Act:

> "1A. Production of witnesses without summons.—A Subject to the provisions of sub-rule (3) of rule 1, any party to the suit may, without applying for summons under rule 1, bring any witness to give evidence or to produce documents."

The stray "A" after the rule heading is a misprint carried in the official compilation itself rather than a transcription slip, and it should be read as though it were not there.

Read with Rule 1(3), the two provisions fit together exactly, and the fit is the key to the Order. Sub-rule (3) speaks of permitting a party "to call, whether by summoning through Court or otherwise, any witness, other than those whose names appear in the list". The word "otherwise" in that phrase is Rule 1A. So the textual position is this. For a witness whose name is on the list, Rule 1A applies without qualification: the party may bring him, and no summons, no application under Rule 1(2) and no deposit under Rule 2(1) is required. For a witness whose name is not on the list, the party needs leave under Rule 1(3) whichever route he takes, because sub-rule (3) covers the summons and the "otherwise" alike.

Rule 1A, then, creates no exception to the list. The list survives intact and leave is still needed to depart from it. What Rule 1A creates is an exception to the *summons* — and the summons, not the list, is what the rest of the Order is built on.

## What a summons buys, and what nothing else buys

Everything coercive in Order XVI is keyed to the fact that a person was summoned. Reading the rules in sequence makes the pattern hard to miss.

The duty to attend arises from the summons:

> "15. Duty of persons summoned to give evidence or produce document.—Subject as last aforesaid, whoever is summoned to appear and give evidence in a suit shall attend at the time and place named in the summons for that purpose, and whoever is summoned to produce a document shall either attend to produce it, or cause it to be produced, at such time and place."

The duty to keep attending arises from the summons, and so does the power to secure it:

> "16. When they may depart.—(1) A person so summoned and attending shall, unless the Court otherwise directs, attend at each hearing until the suit has been disposed of.
>
> (2) On the application of either party and the payment through the Court of all necessary expenses (if any), the Court may require any person so summoned and attending to furnish security to attend at the next or any other hearing or until the suit is disposed of and, in default of his furnishing such security, may order him to be detained in the civil prison."

The machinery against a defaulter opens with the summons. Rule 10(1), substituted in 1976, is engaged where a person "to whom a summons has been issued either to attend to give evidence or to produce a document, fails to attend or to produce the document in compliance with such summons", and it then directs an inquiry into service: the court *shall* examine the server on oath where the serving officer's certificate has not been verified by affidavit or where service was effected by a party or his agent, and *may* do so where the certificate has been verified. (The first limb is printed in the official compilation with a word missing — "if service of the summons has affected by a party or his agent" — and has to be read as "has been effected".) Where the court sees reason to believe that the evidence is material and the failure was without lawful excuse, sub-rule (2) permits a proclamation, and sub-rule (3) permits more:

> "(3) In lieu of or at the time of issuing such proclamation, or at any time afterwards, the Court may, in its discretion, issue a warrant, either with or without bail, for the arrest of such person, and may make an order for the attachment of his property to such amount as it thinks fit, not exceeding the amount of the costs of attachment and of any fine which may be imposed under rule 12:
>
> Provided that no Court of Small Causes shall make an order for the attachment of immovable property."

Rule 12 supplies the fine. Rule 13 supplies the method of attachment by borrowing the execution provisions, so that they apply "as if the person whose property is so attached were a judgment-debtor". Rule 17 extends rules 10 to 13 to a person who attends and then departs in contravention of Rule 16, and Rule 18 deals with a witness brought in under a warrant who cannot be examined, with bail or the civil prison as the alternatives.

Set against that apparatus, consider the position of a witness brought under Rule 1A. He was never summoned. Rule 15 does not impose a duty of attendance on him, because it speaks only of a person who "is summoned". Rule 16(1) does not require him to attend at each hearing until the suit is disposed of, because it speaks of "a person so summoned and attending", and Rule 16(2)'s security and civil prison are keyed to the same words. Rule 10 cannot be engaged against him, because there is no summons with which he failed to comply. Rules 12, 13, 17 and 18 all depend on Rule 10 or on Rule 16 and fall with them.

So a party who exercises the convenience in Rule 1A gets his witness into the courtroom without an application, without a deposit and without waiting for service, and acquires no power over him whatever. If he changes his mind on the way to court, the Order provides nothing. The party's position depends entirely on the witness's continued willingness, which is the condition the rest of the Order exists to dispense with.

Since 2002 that exposure has a particular shape, because the Code no longer takes a witness's evidence in one sitting. Order XVIII Rule 4 was substituted by the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 2002, with effect from 1 July 2002, and it splits the examination in two:

> "4. Recording of evidence.—(1) In every case, the examination-in-chief of a witness shall be on affidavit and copies thereof shall be supplied to the opposite party by the party who calls him for evidence:
>
> Provided that where documents are filed and the parties rely upon the documents, the proof and admissibility of such documents which are filed along with affidavit shall be subject to the orders of the Court."

and sub-rule (2) then provides for the other half:

> "(2) The evidence (cross-examination and re-examination) of the witness in attendance, whose evidence (examination-in-chief) by affidavit has been furnished to the Court, shall be taken either by the Court or by the Commissioner appointed by it:
>
> Provided that the Court may, while appointing a commission under this sub-rule, consider taking into account such relevant factors as it thinks fit."

(Further sub-rules were inserted into Rule 4 by Act 4 of 2016, with effect from 23 October 2015; they apply to commercial disputes of a specified value, govern the simultaneous filing and the withdrawal of affidavits of evidence, and leave the two-stage structure intact.)

The words "the witness in attendance" do the work. The scheme assumes the deponent of the affidavit will be in court afterwards, and the only machinery the Code has for ensuring that he is — Rules 15, 16, 10 and 12 of Order XVI — operates on a person who was summoned. A party who files his witness's affidavit of examination-in-chief under Order XVIII Rule 4(1), having brought that witness under Order XVI Rule 1A, has no provision in either Order to compel him if he then stops attending. Whether the affidavit can be read in those circumstances is a question of evidence on which the text of the two Orders is silent; what the text does settle is that the party who chose Rule 1A has no way of preventing the question from arising.

None of this is an oversight. The court's coercive power is exercised against a non-party, and the Order makes a party who wants it ask for it, name the person against whom it is to be used, state his purpose under Rule 1(2), and put up the money under Rule 2(1) before it issues. What the Order does not do is say so. Rule 1A is drafted as a permission and reads as a saving of trouble, and nothing in it or near it indicates that the trouble is the price of the compulsion.

## Two ceilings for one default

Order XVI is not the only place in the Code that deals with a summoned witness who does not come. Section 30, which sits in the body of the Code, contains the power to issue the summons:

> "30. Power to order discovery and the like.—Subject to such conditions and limitations as may be prescribed, the Court may, at any time, either of its own motion or on the application of any party,—
>
> (a) make such orders as may be necessary or reasonable in all matters relating to the delivery and answering of interrogatories, the admission of documents and facts, and the discovery, inspection, production, impounding and return of documents or other material objects producible as evidence;
>
> (b) issue summonses to persons whose attendance is required either to give evidence or to produce documents or such other objects as aforesaid;
>
> (c) order any fact to be proved by affidavit."

The opening words are the Code's standard device for sending the detail to the First Schedule: "prescribed" is defined by section 2(16) to mean "prescribed by rules", so the conditions and limitations on the section 30 power are the rules in Order XVI. The section confers the power; the Order regulates it. Two sections later the Code provides its own sanction:

> "32. Penalty for default.—The Court may compel the attendance of any person to whom a summons has been issued under section 30 and for that purpose may—
>
> (a) issue a warrant for his arrest;
>
> (b) attach and sell his property;
>
> (c) impose a fine upon him [not exceeding five thousand rupees];
>
> (d) order him to furnish security for his appearance and in default commit him to the civil prison."

The four clauses of section 32 are the four devices of Order XVI compressed: the warrant of Rule 10(3), the attachment and sale of Rules 10(3) and 13, the fine of Rule 12, and the security and civil prison of Rule 16(2). Both provisions address the same person, and both are in force.

The figures do not match. The bracketed words in clause (c) were substituted by section 4 of the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1999, with effect from 1 July 2002, and what they replaced was "not exceeding five hundred rupees". Rule 12(1), which that Act did not touch, still reads:

> "12. Procedure if witness fails to appear.—(1) The Court may, where such person does not appear, or appears but fails so to satisfy the Court, impose upon him such fine not exceeding five hundred rupees as it thinks fit, having regard to his condition in life and all the circumstances of the case..."

The sub-rule continues with the power to attach and sell the witness's property to satisfy the costs of attachment and the fine, and a proviso requiring release from attachment if he pays the costs and fine into court.

Until 1 July 2002 the section and the rule agreed: five hundred rupees in both places. The 1999 Act raised one of the two figures tenfold and left the other where the Code of 1908 had put it. This was not a case of the amending Act overlooking the Order. By its section 25 the same Act went into Order XVI and amended two of its rules, substituting the words that fix the five days in Rule 1(4) and inserting the words that fix the seven days in Rule 2(1). It reached the Order, amended the timetable, and did not alter the penalty.

The consequence is not academic. The same default, by the same witness, under the same summons, carries a ceiling of five thousand rupees if the court proceeds under section 32(c) and five hundred if it proceeds under Rule 12(1). The two provisions are keyed to different stages — section 32 to a summons "issued under section 30", Rule 12 to a person who has failed to satisfy the court under Rule 11 after the Rule 10 machinery has been set in motion — but the summons in an ordinary suit is issued in exercise of the section 30 power as regulated by Order XVI, so it answers both descriptions, and nothing in either provision excludes the other. The Code does not say which of the two ceilings governs, and it has not said so in the twenty-four years since the figures parted company.

A further asymmetry sits inside Rule 12 itself. Sub-rule (2), inserted in 1976, allows the court to impose the Rule 12(1) fine after a show-cause notice even where no proclamation, warrant or attachment has been ordered under Rule 10. So the smaller of the two ceilings is also the one with the shorter route to it.

## The two routes that never pass through the list

The list in Rule 1(1) binds the parties. It does not bind the court, and the Order contains two powers which are the court's own and which no list controls.

The first needs nothing but presence:

> "7. Power to require persons present in Court to give evidence or produce document.—Any person present in Court may be required by the Court to give evidence or to produce any document then and there in his possession or power."

The second needs nothing but the court's view of necessity, and the words in brackets were substituted in 1976:

> "14. Court may of its own accord summon as witnesses strangers to suit.—Subject to the provisions of this Code as to attendance and appearance and to any law for the time being in force, where the Court at any time thinks it necessary [to examine any person, including a party to the suit] and not called as a witness by a party to the suit, the Court may, of its own motion, cause such person to be summoned as a witness to give evidence, or to produce any document in his possession, on a day to be appointed, and may examine him as a witness or require him to produce such document."

Neither rule refers to the list, to sub-rule (3) or to the periods in Rule 1. Rule 14 is expressly for a person "not called as a witness by a party to the suit" — precisely the person a list would have omitted — and after the 1976 substitution it extends to a party. Rule 7 is wider in one respect and far narrower in another: it requires no summons, no notice and no expenses, but it reaches only a person who is in the room, and only documents "then and there in his possession or power".

One of the conditions Rule 7 escapes is geographical:

> "19. No witness to be ordered to attend in person unless resident within certain limits.—No one shall be ordered to attend in person to give evidence unless he resides—
>
> (a) within the local limits of the Court's ordinary jurisdiction, or
>
> (b) without such limits but at a place less then [one hundred] or (where there is railway or steamer communication or other established public conveyance for five-sixths of the distance between the place where he resides and the place where the Court is situate) less than [five hundred kilometres] distance from the court-house:
>
> [Provided that where transport by air is available between the two places mentioned in this rule and the witness is paid the fare by air, he may be ordered to attend in person.]"

The 1976 Act substituted "one hundred" for "fifty" and "five hundred kilometres" for "two hundred miles", and added the proviso. Two features of the rule decide its reach. It restrains an *order* that a person "attend in person" — whether the order is a summons applied for by a party under Rule 1, a proclamation under Rule 10(2), or a summons the court issues of its own motion under Rule 14 — and it therefore has nothing to say to a witness who comes voluntarily under Rule 1A, however far away he lives. And it restrains only an order to attend *in person*, which is why Rule 6 stands undisturbed by it:

> "6. Summons to produce document.—Any person may be summoned to produce a document, without being summoned to give evidence; and any person summoned merely to produce a document shall be deemed to have complied with the summons if he causes such document to be produced instead of attending personally to produce the same."

A distant custodian of a document is therefore reachable, because he need not come. A distant eyewitness cannot be ordered to attend at all once he is outside the Rule 19 limits, unless the air-travel proviso applies and his fare is paid.

What happens to that eyewitness is worth following, because the Code does for him exactly what it does not do for the Rule 1A witness. The 1976 Act, which set the Rule 19 distances, also inserted a proviso into Order XXVI Rule 4 — the rule listing the persons for whose examination a commission may issue, the first of whom is "any person resident beyond the local limits of its jurisdiction":

> "Provided that where, under rule 19 of Order XVI, a person cannot be ordered to attend a Court in person, a commission shall be issued for his examination if his evidence is considered necessary in the interests of justice."

That is an express cross-reference, and the verb is "shall". Where Rule 19 takes away the power to order attendance, the Code does not leave the party with the loss: it names the provision that has been exhausted and directs a substitute, subject only to the court's view that the evidence is necessary in the interests of justice. The geographical gap in Order XVI was identified and filled by the same amending Act in the same year.

Nothing of the kind was done for the gap Rule 1A creates. The two situations are not identical — Rule 19 is a limit the Code imposes, Rule 1A a choice the party makes — but the drafting contrast holds. Where a party loses the power to compel because of where the witness lives, the Code supplies another route to the evidence and makes it mandatory. Where he loses it because he took the convenient route to the witness box, the Code says nothing, and there is no second route.

## The party as a witness, and the two sanctions that apply to him

A party who gives evidence is in the witness box like anyone else, and the Order says so:

> "21. Rules as to witnesses to apply to parties summoned.—Where any party to a suit is required to give evidence or to produce a document, the provisions as to witnesses shall apply to him so far as they are applicable."

But the Order also contains a sanction that exists only for a party, and it is of a different order of magnitude from anything in Rule 12:

> "20. Consequence of refusal of party to give evidence when called on by Court.—Where any party to a suit present in Court refuses, without lawful excuse, when required by the Court, to give evidence or to produce any document then and there in his possession or power, the Court may pronounce judgment against him or make such order in relation to the suit as it thinks fit."

Rule 20 is the counterpart of Rule 7, and the correspondence of language is exact — "present in Court", "then and there in his possession or power". So the sequence is that the court requires a party who is in the room to speak or to produce, under Rule 7; and if he refuses without lawful excuse, Rule 20 permits the court to decide the suit against him.

Two sanctions are therefore available against a party over substantially the same failure. Under Rule 21 read with Rule 12 he may be fined up to five hundred rupees and have his property attached and sold. Under Rule 20 he may lose the suit. The Order does not say when the one is appropriate and the other is not, and the difference between their triggers is a matter of where the party is standing: Rule 20 requires him to be present in court and to refuse, while the Rule 12 route requires a summons, a failure to attend, and the Rule 10 and Rule 11 steps in between. A party who has been summoned and stays away is exposed to the smaller penalty; a party who comes and refuses is exposed to the loss of his case.

One further provision bears on the party who means to give evidence himself, and it sits in a different Order. Order XVI says nothing about the sequence in which the witnesses on a list are to be examined. Order XVIII Rule 3A, inserted by the 1976 Act, fixes one position in that sequence in advance, and it fixes the party's own: where a party wishes to appear as a witness he must do so before any other witness on his behalf has been examined, unless the Court, for reasons to be recorded, permits him to appear at a later stage.

## Where the deposit does not bite: the Uttar Pradesh amendment

The deposit under Rule 2(1) is the practical gate on the summons machinery, because the summons is not granted until the money is in. For courts in Uttar Pradesh the gate has an exception, inserted by section 8 of the Uttar Pradesh Act 57 of 1976, which amends Order XVI in its application to the State. It adds a proviso to Rule 2(1):

> "Provided, where Government is the party applying for a summons to a Government servant, it shall not be necessary for it to make any such payment into court."

and a proviso to Rule 4:

> "Provided that nothing in this rule shall apply to a case where the witness is a Government servant summoned at the instance of Government as a party."

It also omits the sub-rule (4) which the Allahabad High Court had itself inserted in Rule 2, and inserts a sub-rule (4-A) under which the travelling and daily allowances and the salary payable to a Government servant who attends to give evidence or to produce a document are, on the amount being certified by the witness, taxable as costs.

The two provisos do different work. The first removes the deposit, so where the State applies for a summons to one of its own servants the summons issues without money being paid in. The second removes Rule 4, which is the rule allowing the court, where the sum deposited proves insufficient, either to direct a further sum or to "discharge the person summoned without requiring him to give evidence". In that one configuration — Government as the applying party, a Government servant as the witness — the court's power to discharge the witness for want of expenses is therefore unavailable, and the expenses travel into the costs of the suit instead of being deposited in advance.

The State amendment changes who bears the cost and when. It changes nothing in the coercive chain, and it has no bearing on a party who proceeds under Rule 1A, because no summons is applied for and no deposit arises.

## The witness in custody

One class of witness is outside all of this, and the Code gives it an Order of its own. Order XVI-A, inserted by the 1976 Act, governs the attendance of witnesses confined or detained in prisons. Its central power is in Rule 2:

> "2. Power to require attendance of prisoners to give evidence.—Where it appears to a Court that the evidence of a person confined or detained in a prison within the State is material in a suit, the Court may make an order requiring the officer in charge of the prison to produce that person before the Court to give evidence:
>
> Provided that, if the distance from the prison to the Court-house is more than twenty-five kilometers, no such order shall be made unless the Court is satisfied that the examination of such person on commission will not be adequate."

The order then meets filters that are not the court's. Rule 3 requires the party at whose instance it issues to deposit the expenses of executing it, "including the travelling and other expenses of the escort provided for the witness". Rule 4 permits the State Government to direct that a person or class of persons shall not be removed from prison, having regard to the nature of the offence or grounds of detention, the likelihood of disturbance of public order and the public interest generally; while that direction is in force, no order under Rule 2 has effect. Rule 5 requires the officer in charge to abstain from carrying out the court's order, and to report his reasons, in four further cases. Rule 7 supplies the fallback of a commission for examination in the prison.

The arrangement inverts Order XVI's. For the ordinary witness the Code's premise is attendance and the commission is the exception; here the twenty-five kilometre proviso makes the commission presumptive and attendance the thing to be justified. Once again the Code names the substitute expressly. What it does not provide is the choice — Order XVI-A proceeds throughout on the footing that a party cannot bring this witness himself, and there is no Rule 1A in it.

## Three things that follow at the Bar

The first concerns the choice Rule 1A presents. It is a choice between convenience now and compulsion later, and it should be made witness by witness rather than for the case as a whole. For a witness who is the party's own employee, relative or professional adviser, Rule 1A costs nothing worth having. For a witness who is neutral, reluctant, or whose evidence the opposite party has reason to want suppressed, the summons is not a formality: it is the only thing that produces a duty to attend under Rule 15, a duty to keep attending under Rule 16(1), and access to Rule 10 and Rule 12 if the duty is broken. The question to ask before deciding is not whether the witness has agreed to come. It is what is to be done if he agrees and then does not come — and the answer, for a witness brought under Rule 1A, is that the Code offers nothing, not even the commission it makes mandatory for the witness who lives too far away.

The second concerns the list. Rule 1(3) requires leave, sufficient cause and recorded reasons for any witness "other than those whose names appear in the list", and it applies to the Rule 1A route as much as to a summons, because sub-rule (3) covers calling a witness "whether by summoning through Court or otherwise". A list drawn narrowly in the fifteen days after issues are settled is therefore not a tidy list but one that will have to be reopened on an application whose success is discretionary. The three periods pass without penalty; the completeness of the list is the one thing in this Order on which anything turns.

The third concerns what to ask for when a summoned witness does not attend. The Order's sequence is specific — Rule 10(1) requires the court, where service was effected by a party or his agent, to examine the server on oath; Rule 10(2) permits a proclamation only where the court sees reason to believe the evidence is material and the failure was without lawful excuse; Rule 10(3) permits the warrant and attachment; Rule 11 gives the witness his answer; and Rule 12 ends the matter with a fine. An application that asks for the warrant without laying the materiality is asking the court to skip the sub-rule that authorises it. And when the application does reach the penalty, the two ceilings in the Code — five hundred rupees under Rule 12(1), five thousand under section 32(c) since 1 July 2002 — have to be addressed rather than assumed, because the Code raised one and not the other, and said nothing about which of them governs a summons issued in an ordinary suit.
