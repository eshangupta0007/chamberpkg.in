A suit against the Government differs from a suit against a private defendant in one respect that has nothing to do with the merits. It cannot be filed when the plaintiff is ready to file it.

Section 80 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 is the provision that does this, and it is usually described as a privilege of the State — an immunity from being sued in a hurry, of a piece with the other advantages a government litigant enjoys. That description explains the inconvenience but not the design. The section does not shorten the plaintiff's right, does not add to what he must prove, and does not give the Government any defence it would not otherwise have. What it does is insert a fixed period between the grievance and the suit, require that the period begin with a document stating the claim, and require that the document reach a particular office. Everything else in the section, including the two sub-sections added in 1976, is an adjustment to that single mechanism.

Read closely, the mechanism turns out to be about one thing, and it is not delay. It is about making sure that before the State is sued it has been told, in writing, by someone identifiable, what is complained of and what is wanted — and that it has had an opportunity to answer. The 1976 amendment is the proof, because it removed the waiting period in urgent cases and kept the opportunity.

## What the section stops, and what it leaves alone

Sub-section (1) provides that, "Save as otherwise provided in sub-section (2), no suit shall be instituted against the Government ... or against a public officer in respect of any act purporting to be done by such public officer in his official capacity, until the expiration of two months next after notice in writing has been delivered to, or left at the office of" the officers the clauses then name.

Three things follow from the words chosen, and each of them matters more in practice than the two months itself.

The bar is on institution. It is not a bar on the cause of action, which arises when it arises; not a bar on the relief, which is whatever the substantive law allows; and not a period of limitation, which is governed by the Limitation Act, 1963. What the section prohibits is the act of filing, and it prohibits it only for a measured interval.

The interval runs from delivery, not from dispatch and not from receipt by any particular person. The notice must have been "delivered to, or left at the office of" the named officer, and the two months are counted "next after" that. A notice posted and not delivered has not started the clock. A notice left at the right office has, whether or not anyone there read it.

And the interval is deducted from limitation rather than borrowed from it. Section 15(2) of the Limitation Act provides that in computing the period of limitation for any suit "of which notice has been given ... in accordance with the requirements of any law for the time being in force, the period of such notice ... shall be excluded." So the plaintiff who waits does not pay for waiting. That is a necessary provision rather than a generous one: without it, a section that regulates when a suit may be filed would silently shorten every limitation period applicable to a suit against the State by two months — a substantive consequence Section 80 nowhere states.

## What the notice must say, and why the plaint must say it too

The section specifies the contents of the notice in one clause, and they fall under three heads: it must state "the cause of action, the name, description and place of residence of the plaintiff and the relief which he claims". Two of the three describe the claim. The third identifies the claimant.

Then it adds a requirement that belongs not to the notice but to the pleading: "and the plaint shall contain a statement that such notice has been so delivered or left."

That last requirement is a departure from the general rule of pleading, and the departure is deliberate. Order VI Rule 6 provides that "Any condition precedent, the performance or occurrence of which is intended to be contested, shall be distinctly specified in his pleading by the plaintiff or defendant ... and, subject thereto, an averment of the performance or occurrence of all conditions precedent necessary for the case of the plaintiff or defendant shall be implied in his pleading." The ordinary position, in other words, is that a plaintiff need not plead that he satisfied a condition precedent; the averment is implied, and it is for the defendant to specify the one he intends to contest.

Section 80 takes its own condition precedent out of that rule. The statement about the notice must appear in the plaint. The plaintiff does not get the benefit of the implication, and the Government does not have to raise the point before the plaintiff is required to address it.

Those three heads have a second function that the section does not spell out. The notice is the document in which the claim is first stated, and it is stated to a reader who is being invited to consider it rather than defend it. The cause of action and the relief claimed are therefore fixed, in outline, before the plaint exists. Sub-section (3), when it arrives, treats this as the substance of the requirement: what it preserves, through every defect it forgives, is that "the cause of action and the relief claimed by the plaintiff had been substantially indicated".

## The office, not the officer

The clauses that name the recipient are specific, and they are specific about offices.

For a suit against the Central Government, except where it relates to a railway, the notice goes to a Secretary to that Government. Where it relates to a railway, it goes to the General Manager of that railway. For a suit against a State Government, it goes to a Secretary to that Government or to the Collector of the district. In each case the notice may be "delivered to, or left at the office of" the officer named — so the officer need not be found, and the office will do.

For a public officer the rule is different, and the difference is easy to miss: the notice must be "delivered to him or left at his office". The category is wide. Section 2(17) defines "public officer" to mean "a person falling under any of the following descriptions", and the descriptions that follow are both closed and wide: among them every Judge, every member of an All-India Service, every commissioned or gazetted officer in the military, naval or air forces of the Union, certain officers of a Court of Justice, and every person who holds an office by virtue of which he is empowered to place or keep any person in confinement.

The asymmetry is sensible once the purpose is stated. Against the Government, the notice is addressed to the machinery that decides whether the State will contest a claim, and any Secretary will do because the decision is institutional. Against an officer sued personally for an official act, the person who must decide whether to contest is the officer, so the notice goes to him.

## "Purporting to be done"

The words that define the class of suits against a public officer are "in respect of any act purporting to be done by such public officer in his official capacity".

"Purporting" does work worth isolating. Almost every suit against an officer for an official act alleges that the act was unlawful — that the demand was unauthorised, the seizure without power, the order beyond jurisdiction. If illegality took the act outside "official capacity", the notice requirement would apply only to suits complaining of lawful conduct, which is to say almost none, and the provision would be self-defeating. The word fixes that by directing attention to what the act presented itself as, not to whether it was within authority.

The same conception appears in Order XXVII Rule 5A, inserted by the 1976 amendment: "Where a suit is instituted against a public officer for damages or other relief in respect of any act alleged to have been done by him in his official capacity, the Government shall be joined as a party to the suit." The act that attracts notice is the act that brings the Government in. The two provisions do not use the same word — Section 80 speaks of an act "purporting to be done" in an official capacity, Rule 5A of one "alleged to have been done" in that capacity — but both locate the officer's official character in the character of the act complained of, and neither makes it depend on a conclusion about the act's validity.

## Leave buys the institution and nothing else

Sub-section (2), inserted in 1976, is the route past the two months, and it is worth reading to the end before deciding what it gives.

"A suit to obtain an urgent or immediate relief against the Government ... or any public officer in respect of any act purporting to be done by such public officer in his official capacity, may be instituted, with the leave of the Court, without serving any notice as required by sub-section (1); but the Court shall not grant relief in the suit, whether interim or otherwise, except after giving to the Government or public officer, as the case may be, a reasonable opportunity of showing cause in respect of the relief prayed for in the suit".

The sentence has two halves and they point in different directions. The first half dispenses with the notice. The second withholds every form of relief until the opposite party has been heard. The words "whether interim or otherwise" leave nothing out.

What that means for a plaintiff is narrower than the sub-section first appears to promise. He may file at once. He may not obtain anything at once. The urgency that justified the leave does not justify an order, and the order must wait for the State to be given a reasonable opportunity to show cause on the very relief prayed for.

The contrast with an ordinary suit is instructive, because the Code elsewhere does allow relief before the defendant is heard. Order XXXIX Rule 3 provides that the Court "shall in all cases, except where it appears that the object of granting the injunction would be defeated by the delay, before granting an injunction, direct notice of the application for the same to be given to the opposite party", and its proviso then sets out the conditions on which an injunction may be granted without notice — recorded reasons, immediate service of the application, the affidavit and the plaint, and an affidavit of compliance. Rule 3A requires the Court to endeavour to dispose of such an application within thirty days.

So the Code contemplates the ex parte injunction, regulates it, and tries to shorten its life. In a suit instituted under sub-section (2), that route is closed by the statute itself. The sub-section admits no exception for the case where the object of the relief would be defeated by delay, prescribes no recorded-reasons route past the hearing, and does not distinguish between interim and final relief. The effect of leave is to replace a two-month wait with a short adjournment — a substantial concession, but not a licence to obtain an ex parte order.

The limit of that closure should be stated as precisely as the closure itself, because it defines the choice the plaintiff is actually making. The prohibition in sub-section (2) is directed at relief "in the suit" — the suit instituted with leave and without notice. It says nothing about a suit instituted in the ordinary way. A plaintiff who serves the notice and waits out the two months files a suit in which Order XXXIX Rule 3 applies as it applies to any other, proviso and all, and may ask for an injunction before the Government is heard.

So the two routes are not a slow one and a fast one. They are a trade. Sub-section (2) buys the earlier filing at the cost of the ex parte order; sub-section (1) preserves the possibility of the ex parte order at the cost of two months. Which is worth more depends on what the delay would do to the subject-matter, and that assessment belongs to the stage before the notice is drafted, not after.

## The plaint comes back to the same court

The proviso to sub-section (2) deals with the application for leave that fails: "Provided that the Court shall, if it is satisfied, after hearing the parties, that no urgent or immediate relief need be granted in the suit, return the plaint for presentation to it after complying with the requirements of sub-section (1)."

Four features of that sentence repay attention.

The plaint is returned, not rejected. The distinction is not formal. Section 2(2) provides that a decree "shall be deemed to include the rejection of a plaint"; it says nothing about the return of one. A rejection would be a decree and appealable as such.

It is returned "for presentation to it" — to the same court. Nothing in the proviso lets the refusal of leave become an occasion for choosing a different forum, and nothing requires the plaintiff to begin again elsewhere. He serves the notice, waits out the period, and brings the same plaint back.

The satisfaction is reached "after hearing the parties". This is the odd feature, and it is the one that confirms what the sub-section is for. The Government has had no notice — that is the premise of the application — and yet it is heard on whether urgent relief need be granted. Sub-section (2) will not let the Court grant relief without hearing the State, and the proviso will not let the Court refuse leave without hearing the parties. On both branches, the hearing survives.

And the Code provides no appeal against the refusal. Section 104(1) allows an appeal from the orders it lists "and save as otherwise expressly provided in the body of this Code or by any law for the time being in force, from no other orders". Order XLIII Rule 1(a) names "an order under rule 10 of Order VII returning a plaint to be presented to the proper Court" — which this return is not, since it is made under the proviso and the plaint returns to the same court. The list does not name it. That the omission is deliberate is suggested by what the same 1976 amendment did elsewhere: it inserted clause (ffa) into Section 104(1) to allow an appeal from "an order under section 91 or section 92 refusing leave to institute a suit of the nature referred to in section 91 or section 92". The Code knows how to make a refusal of leave appealable. It did so in 1976 for two other provisions and not for this one. What follows from that is only what it says: the Code's own appeal provisions supply no appeal. Whether such an order is open to challenge by any route outside those provisions is a separate question, and Section 80 does not address it.

## Two months on the other side

Order XXVII, which contains the procedure for suits by or against the Government, repeats the period from the other end.

Rule 5 provides that the Court, in fixing the day for the Government to answer the plaint, "shall allow a reasonable time for the necessary communication with the Government through the proper channel, and for the issue of instructions to the Government pleader to appear and answer on behalf of the Government and may extend the time at its discretion but the time so extended shall not exceed two months in the aggregate." Rule 7 adds that a public officer who considers it proper to refer the matter to Government before answering may apply for extension, and "upon such application the Court shall extend the time for so long as appears to it to be necessary."

Rule 5B then states the object in terms. In every suit or proceeding to which the Government or a public officer in his official capacity is a party, "it shall be the duty of the Court to make, in the first instance, every endeavour, where it is possible to do so consistently with the nature and circumstances of the case, to assist the parties in arriving at a settlement in respect of the subject-matter of the suit", and the Court may adjourn the proceeding to enable that attempt.

Put beside Section 80, these rules apply one assumption at three stages. Before the suit, the State has two months in which to consider the claim. After the summons, it must be allowed a reasonable time to take instructions through the proper channel, and any extension of that time is capped at two months in the aggregate. And at the first opportunity inside the suit, the Court is to attempt what the notice was supposed to achieve — a settlement that makes the litigation unnecessary.

## What sub-section (3) forgives

The third sub-section, also from 1976, is the one that keeps the section from becoming a trap:

"No suit instituted against the Government or against a public officer in respect of any act purporting to be done by such public officer in his official capacity shall be dismissed merely by reason of any error or defect in the notice referred to in sub-section (1), if in such notice — (a) the name, description and the residence of the plaintiff had been so given as to enable the appropriate authority or the public officer to identify the person serving the notice and such notice had been delivered or left at the office of the appropriate authority specified in sub-section (1), and (b) the cause of action and the relief claimed by the plaintiff had been substantially indicated."

What is forgiven is "any error or defect in the notice". The two conditions then restate the same three heads functionally. The particulars of the plaintiff need only be sufficient "to identify the person serving the notice" — and clause (a), asking for "the name, description and the residence of the plaintiff", does not repeat sub-section (1)'s "place of residence". The cause of action and the relief need only have been "substantially indicated".

What is not forgiven is as important, and it is visible in what the sub-section declines to describe as an error. The notice must still have been "delivered or left at the office of the appropriate authority specified in sub-section (1)" — that requirement sits inside clause (a), among the conditions of forgiveness, which means a notice delivered to the wrong office is not a defective notice but an unmet condition. Nor does sub-section (3) touch the absence of a notice, or the institution of a suit before the two months expire. It is addressed to the contents of a notice that was given, to the right office, in time.

The sub-section therefore shifts the inquiry from form to function, and states the function precisely: did the document reach the authority that could decide, and did it tell that authority enough to decide? A notice that did both has served the section, whatever is wrong with it. A notice that did neither has not, however correct its form.

## What the structure assumes

The premise of the whole arrangement is a prediction about what the State will do with two months, and the prediction is that it may reconsider. That is why the notice must state the claim rather than merely announce an intention to sue; why it must go to an office competent to decide rather than to the officer who caused the grievance; why the forgiveness clause in sub-section (3) asks only whether the right office learned enough; and why Rule 5B asks the Court to attempt, once the suit has begun, exactly what the notice was meant to make unnecessary.

Two consequences follow for anyone drafting.

The first document in a suit against the Government is not the plaint. It is the notice, and it settles more than the date of filing: the cause of action and the relief it states are the ones the plaint will have to be built on, because sub-section (3) protects a notice only to the extent that those two things were substantially indicated in it. A notice drafted as a formality, and a plaint that then claims something the notice did not foreshadow, creates a question that no amount of care with the plaint will answer.

The second is that the two months must be planned for and cannot be wished away. The urgency route under sub-section (2) is real, and where the two months are themselves the injury it is the only route; but what it secures is an earlier hearing, not an earlier order. A party who needs an order before the Government can be heard will not get there under sub-section (2) at all, and will have to buy that possibility with the notice and the wait.

There is one asymmetry left, and it is worth stating plainly because it is sometimes assumed away. Nothing in the section requires the Government to reply to the notice. It need not acknowledge it, consider it, or record a decision on it. Two months of silence is full compliance by the recipient and a complete answer to the plaintiff who complains that nothing happened. The section gives the State an opportunity and does not impose a duty — which is why, on the plaintiff's side, the notice is best drafted as the pleading it may turn out to be rather than as the formality it will usually be treated as.
