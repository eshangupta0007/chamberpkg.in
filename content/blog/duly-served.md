excerpt: "A court cannot decide against a person who has not been told, but a code that insisted on actual notice would hand every defendant a veto over the suit against him. So Order V builds four ways of treating a man as served when he may have received nothing — delivery to an adult member of his family, affixation on his door, substituted service ordered by the Court, and a declaration founded on a postal endorsement. The guards on the four are not graded by how far each one stands from real notice. The device that rests on the thinnest material is the only one on which the Code gives the Court no choice at all, and the proviso to it requires the declaration to be made even where nothing has come back."

A judgment binds a person who was given the chance to answer. That is not a rule of procedure; it is the condition on which the judgment is worth anything. But a code that required proof of actual notice before a suit could proceed would have given every defendant a simple method of never being sued. He would only have to stay away from his door.

So every procedural code builds devices that permit a Court to treat a man as told when he has not been told, and the real content of those devices is not in the physical act each one prescribes. Affixing a paper to a door and affixing a paper to a courthouse noticeboard are much the same act, and neither of them informs anybody. What distinguishes them is the question each code must answer before it allows the act to count: who decides that the fiction is safe, on what material, and with how much freedom to refuse.

The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 answers that question four separate times in Order V, and the four answers do not line up with the four devices in the order one would expect. The device that places the paper in a human hand is subject to no judicial check. The device that places it on a door is subject to the fullest one. The device that rests on an endorsement made by a stranger in a sorting office is the one on which the Code gives the Court no choice at all.

## What the Code asks for first

The baseline is unambiguous and it is personal service.

Section 27 provides that where a suit has been duly instituted, "a summons may be issued to the defendant to appear and answer the claim and may be served in manner prescribed on such day not beyond thirty days from date of the institution of the suit." The closing words were inserted by section 3 of the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1999 — Act 46 of 1999 — with effect from 1 July 2002. The section attaches no consequence to their breach, and nothing elsewhere in the Code supplies one.

Order V Rule 1(1), as substituted by section 6 of Act 22 of 2002, requires the summons to call on the defendant to appear and answer and to file his written statement within thirty days from the date of service on him. Rule 1(3) requires the summons to be signed by the Judge or an officer he appoints and sealed with the seal of the Court. Rule 2 requires a copy of the plaint to accompany it.

Then Rule 11: "Save as otherwise prescribed, where there are more defendants than one, service of the summons shall be made on each defendant." And Rule 12, which states the preference the rest of the Order is built around: "Wherever it is practicable, service shall be made on the defendant in person, unless he has an agent empowered to accept service, in which case service on such agent shall be sufficient."

Rule 10 says how that is done: by delivering or tendering a copy signed by the Judge or his appointed officer and sealed with the seal of the Court. Rule 16 says what the serving officer must then obtain: where he delivers or tenders the copy to the defendant personally, or to an agent or other person on his behalf, "he shall require the signature of the person to whom the copy is so delivered or tendered to an acknowledgment of service endorsed on the original summons."

That is the whole of the ordinary case, and it produces a document — a signed acknowledgment on the back of the original summons — which is as close to proof of notice as a procedural code can get without asking the defendant to admit it.

Everything that follows is a departure from it.

## The two substitutions the serving officer makes alone

The first departure substitutes a person. Rules 13 and 14 permit service on an agent who carries on the defendant's business within the local limits, and on an agent in charge of immovable property where the suit concerns that property. In both the defendant has, in a sense, chosen the recipient.

Rule 15 is different, because the recipient is chosen by nobody. It applies where the defendant is absent from his residence when service is sought there, where there is no likelihood of his being found at the residence within a reasonable time, and where he has no agent empowered to accept service. In that case "service may be made on any adult member of the family, whether male or female, who is residing with him." The rule carries an Explanation: "A servant is not a member of the family within the meaning of this rule." Rule 15 in its present form was substituted by section 55 of the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1976 — Act 104 of 1976 — with effect from 1 February 1977.

Three conditions, then. Two are facts about the defendant's movements and the third about his arrangements, and all three are found by the serving officer, at the door, without evidence and without a hearing.

The second departure dispenses with a recipient altogether. Rule 17 applies where the defendant or his agent or such other person refuses to sign the acknowledgment, or where the serving officer, "after using all due and reasonable diligence, cannot find the defendant, who is absent from his residence at the time when service is sought to be effected on him at his residence and there is no likelihood of his being found at the residence within a reasonable time", and there is no agent and no other person on whom service can be made. The words inside that second clause were inserted into Rule 17 by the same section 55 of the 1976 Act, which is why they reproduce the formula of Rule 15 exactly: so far as Rule 17 operates on the defendant's absence rather than on a refusal to sign, the two rules were made to turn on the same finding.

What the officer then does is affix a copy of the summons "on the outer door or some other conspicuous part of the house in which the defendant ordinarily resides or carries on business or personally works for gain", and return the original with a report stating that he has so affixed the copy, "the circumstances under which he did so, and the name and address of the person (if any) by whom the house was identified and in whose presence the copy was affixed."

That report is the most demanding thing Order V asks of a serving officer. It is not a form of words. It asks for the circumstances, which means the account of the diligence; and it asks for a named identifier with an address, which means a person who can be sent for.

## The one return the Court must take up

Rule 18 provides for the endorsement of the return, and the scope of the rule is easy to read past. "The serving officer shall, in all cases in which the summons has been served under rule 16, endorse or annex, or cause to be endorsed or annexed, on or to the original summons, a return stating the time when and the manner in which the summons was served, and the name and address of the person (if any) identifying the person served and witnessing the delivery or tender of the summons."

Rule 19 is narrower still, and its trigger is a single cross-reference: "Where a summons is returned under rule 17, the Court shall, if the return under that rule has not been verified by the affidavit of the serving officer, and may, if it has been so verified, examine the serving officer on oath, or cause him to be so examined by another Court, touching his proceedings, and may make such further enquiry in the matter as it thinks fit; and shall either declare that the summons has been duly served or order such service as it thinks fit."

Read the two rules together and the shape of the Code's scrutiny appears. Rule 19 is engaged by a return under Rule 17 and by nothing else. Service on an adult member of the family under Rule 15 is not a return under Rule 17; it is a delivery, the family member signs the acknowledgment under Rule 16, and the matter comes back to the Court as a Rule 18 endorsement. No examination on oath is required or contemplated. The officer's findings that the defendant was absent, that he was not likely to be found within a reasonable time, that the person who signed was an adult, that that person was a member of the family and not a servant, and that the person was residing with him — every one of them stands on the endorsement alone.

Rule 19 is also the only place in Order V where the Code gives a Court, in terms, an alternative to declaring that service has been made. The verb is "shall either declare that the summons has been duly served or order such service as it thinks fit." A Court reading an affixation report is given a choice, and the choice is between accepting the fiction and ordering a different attempt.

Notice, too, how Rule 19 grades its own compulsion. Where the return is not verified by the affidavit of the serving officer, the examination on oath is mandatory. Where it is verified, the examination is discretionary. An affidavit buys the officer a presumption of regularity; it does not buy him a conclusion, because the Court "may make such further enquiry in the matter as it thinks fit" either way.

## The substitution the Court orders in advance

Rule 20 is the fourth device and the only one that begins with the Court rather than with an officer in the field.

Sub-rule (1): "Where the Court is satisfied that there is reason to believe that the defendant is keeping out of the way for the purpose of avoiding service, or that for any other reason the summons cannot be served in the ordinary way, the Court shall order the summons to be served by affixing a copy thereof in some conspicuous place in the Court-house, and also upon some conspicuous part of the house (if any) in which the defendant is known to have last resided or carried on business or personally worked for gain, or in such other manner as the Court thinks fit."

The satisfaction is in two alternative limbs, and they are not of the same weight. The first is evasion, which is a finding about the defendant's conduct. The second — "that for any other reason the summons cannot be served in the ordinary way" — is a finding about the service machinery and carries no imputation at all. A defendant whose address is simply wrong falls within the second limb and not the first, and the order is available on either.

Sub-rule (1A), inserted by section 55 of the 1976 Act, regulates the commonest form of "such other manner": "Where the Court acting under sub-rule (1) orders service by an advertisement in a newspaper, the newspaper shall be a daily newspaper circulating in the locality in which the defendant is last known to have actually and voluntarily resided, carried on business or personally worked for gain." The requirement is of circulation in a particular place, not of a particular paper, and the place is defined by the defendant's last known voluntary residence rather than by the Court's convenience.

Sub-rule (2) then states the effect, under its own side-heading: "Service substituted by order of the Court shall be as effectual as if it had been made on the defendant personally." Sub-rule (3) requires the Court to fix such time for the defendant's appearance as the case may require.

Set Rule 20 beside Rule 17 and the physical acts almost coincide — a copy affixed to a conspicuous part of the house in both. What differs is the order of operations and what survives it. Under Rule 17 the officer acts and the Court then examines; the declaration is a judicial act performed on material, and the Court may refuse to make it. Under Rule 20 the Court is satisfied first, and once the ordered mode has been carried out the Code declares the result effectual as personal service in terms that leave nothing further to be decided.

That is a defensible arrangement. The judicial scrutiny has simply been moved to the front. But it means the thing a Court is deciding under Rule 20 is not whether the defendant has been told; it is whether the attempt to tell him may now stop.

## The declaration the Code does not leave to the Court

Rule 9 in its present form was substituted by section 6 of Act 22 of 2002 with effect from 1 July 2002, and it changed the status of the post in the Code. (The table of amending Acts prefixed to the official compilation lists Act 22 of 2002 under the year 2000, which is worth knowing before a citation is checked against it.)

The post had been in and out before. Rule 20A, "Service of summons by post", was inserted by Act 66 of 1956 and repealed by section 55 of the 1976 Act. Rule 19A, "Simultaneous issue of summons for service by post in addition to personal service", was omitted by section 15 of Act 46 of 1999 with effect from 1 July 2002 — the very day on which the new Rule 9 came into force. The Code therefore abolished postal service as a supplement and, on the same date, installed it as a principal mode.

Rule 9(1) directs that where the defendant resides within the Court's jurisdiction, or has an agent there empowered to accept service, the summons shall, unless the Court otherwise directs, be delivered or sent to the proper officer or "to such courier services as are approved by the Court". Rule 9(3) permits service "by delivering or transmitting a copy thereof by speed post with registration and proof of delivery addressed to the defendant or his agent empowered to accept the service or by such courier services as are approved by the High Court or by the Court referred to in sub-rule (1) or by any other means of transmission of documents (including fax message or electronic mail service) provided by the rules made by the High Court", at the plaintiff's expense. Rule 9(6) requires the High Court or the District Judge to prepare a panel of courier agencies.

Rule 9(5) is the operative provision, and it should be read against Rule 19.

"When an acknowledgment or any other receipt purporting to be signed by the defendant or his agent is received by the Court or postal article containing the summons is received back by the Court with an endorsement purporting to have been made by a postal employee or by any person authorised by the courier service to the effect that the defendant or his agent had refused to take delivery of the postal article containing the summons or had refused to accept the summons by any other means specified in sub-rule (3) when tendered or transmitted to him, the Court issuing the summons shall declare that the summons had been duly served on the defendant."

The comparison is in the verbs. Rule 19 says the Court "shall either declare ... or order such service as it thinks fit." Rule 9(5) says the Court "shall declare". There is no second limb. A Court that doubts a postal endorsement is not given the alternative that a Court doubting an affixation report is given, and it is not given the examination on oath either: the person who made the endorsement is a postal employee or a courier's nominee, not an officer of the Court, and no provision brings him before it.

Then the proviso, which goes further than the sub-rule: "Provided that where the summons was properly addressed, pre-paid and duly sent by speed post with registration and proof of delivery, the declaration referred to in this sub-rule shall be made notwithstanding the fact that the proof of delivery having been lost or mislaid, or for any other reason, has not been received by the Court within thirty days from the date of issue of summons."

That is a declaration of due service founded on dispatch alone. The sub-rule at least requires something to come back — a signature or an endorsement of refusal. The proviso operates where nothing has come back, requires the declaration to be made anyway, and sets a period of thirty days from the date of issue after which the absence of any return stops mattering. The facts the Court must find are that the article was properly addressed, that it was pre-paid, and that it was duly sent; all three are facts about the sender.

Put the four devices in a row and the scrutiny does not track the risk. Service on an adult member of the family puts the paper in a human hand inside the defendant's house, and attracts no judicial examination — which is the easiest of the four to justify, because somebody in the household has it. Affixation puts it on his door, and attracts the fullest examination the Order provides, with a power to refuse. Substituted service puts it on a courthouse wall, and attracts a prior judicial satisfaction and then conclusiveness. Postal service may put it nowhere at all, and attracts a declaration the Court is directed to make.

## The mode the plaintiff may not use

Rule 9A, inserted by the same 2002 substitution, lets the Court permit the plaintiff to serve the summons himself, in addition to service under Rule 9. The summons is delivered to the plaintiff, and under sub-rule (2) he effects service "by delivering or tendering to the defendant personally a copy thereof" signed and sealed, or by a mode referred to in Rule 9(3).

Sub-rule (3) then applies two rules to him and not the others: "The provisions of rules 16 and 18 shall apply to a summons personally served under this rule as if the person effecting service were a serving officer." Rule 16 is the signed acknowledgment; Rule 18 is the endorsement of time and manner. Rule 17 is not in the list.

The omission is deliberate, because sub-rule (4) supplies what Rule 17 would otherwise have supplied: "If such summons, when tendered, is refused or if the person served refuses to sign an acknowledgment of service or for any reason such summons cannot be served personally, the Court shall, on the application of the party, re-issue such summons to be served by the Court in the same manner as a summons to a defendant."

So the plaintiff may hand over a summons and take a signature, and he may not affix one. The moment the ordinary case fails, the process returns to the Court and to its officer. Whatever may be said about the guards on the other devices, the Code has at least declined to let the party with an interest in the outcome make the finding that the defendant could not be found.

## What the declaration buys

A declaration of due service is not an end in itself. It is a precondition, and the Code uses it in two places.

Order IX Rule 6(1)(a), as substituted by section 59 of the 1976 Act, provides that where the plaintiff appears and the defendant does not, then "if it is proved that the summons was duly served, the Court may make an order that the suit shall be heard ex parte". Clause (b) provides that if it is not proved, "the Court shall direct a second summons to be issued and served on the defendant". Clause (c) deals with the summons served but not in sufficient time, and requires the hearing to be postponed with notice of the new day to the defendant.

The structure is that due service is a gateway and not a command: the Court "may" order an ex parte hearing where service is proved, and "shall" issue a second summons where it is not.

At the other end, Order IX Rule 13 allows the defendant against whom an ex parte decree has been passed to have it set aside if he satisfies the Court "that the summons was not duly served, or that he was prevented by any sufficient cause from appearing when the suit was called on for hearing". To that the 1976 Act added a second proviso: "no Court shall set aside a decree passed ex parte merely on the ground that there has been an irregularity in the service of summons, if it is satisfied that the defendant had notice of the date of hearing and had sufficient time to appear and answer the plaintiff's claim."

That proviso is the Code's own account of what the modes in Order V are. They are not evidence of notice. If they were, proof that the defendant in fact had notice could not displace a finding that the mode was irregular — and the proviso says it does. The modes are substitutes for notice, and the second proviso is the Code admitting that the substitute may be dispensed with where the thing itself is present.

The admission runs one way only. Actual notice cures a defective mode. A good mode is not displaced by proof that there was no actual notice; a defendant who shows that a summons was affixed to a door he had not lived behind for a year, in strict compliance with Rule 17 and after a Rule 19 examination, is not within the opening words of Rule 13 at all, and must bring himself within "sufficient cause" instead. The two halves of Rule 13 are not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is the price the Code pays for having devices at all.

## The postal mode, changed in December 2025

One further thing has happened to Rule 9, and it is recent enough to be worth stating precisely.

The Repealing and Amending Act, 2025 — Act 37 of 2025 — by section 3 and Schedule II, with effect from 20 December 2025, changed the postal product the Code requires. The substitution was made term for term. Wherever the Code had said "registered post, acknowledgement due", it now says "speed post with registration and proof of delivery"; where it had said "registered post", it now says "speed post with registration".

Four places in the Code were touched. Section 148A(2), the caveator's duty to serve notice of the caveat on the person expected to make the application. Order V Rule 9(3) and the proviso to Rule 9(5) — the mode of postal service, and the proviso that declares service where the return has been lost. Order XXI Rule 1(2), the judgment-debtor's notice to the decree-holder of a payment made into Court. And clause (a) of the proviso to Order XXXIX Rule 3, the copies an applicant must send to the opposite party immediately after an injunction has been granted without notice.

A Repealing and Amending Act is the instrument by which the statute book is cleared of enactments that have become spent, and by which formal and verbal corrections are made across the rest of it. Whether this particular change altered anything of substance turns on whether the two descriptions name the same postal service in different words. The Code does not say so, and the amending Act's Schedule records the substitution and nothing else.

What can be said without resolving that is narrower and is enough for practice. The proviso to Rule 9(5) now requires a Court to declare due service, in the absence of any return at all, where the summons was properly addressed, pre-paid and "duly sent by speed post with registration and proof of delivery". The words "duly sent by" now carry the name of a different postal product from the one they carried before 20 December 2025, and the finding the Court makes under the proviso is a finding that the summons was sent by that product. The main limb of sub-rule (5) names no postal product at all, since it turns on what comes back; the proviso names one, and a despatch by some other product is outside the words it uses.

## Three things that follow at the Bar

The first concerns what is on the file when a declaration of due service is sought. For a Rule 17 affixation the Code has told the officer what his report must contain — the circumstances, and the name and address of the identifying person — and has told the Court, in Rule 19, that it may examine him and make such further enquiry as it thinks fit. A report that recites affixation and nothing else has not complied with Rule 17, and the Court's power under Rule 19 to order such service as it thinks fit is the answer to it. Where the opposing side's interest is the other way, an affidavit of the serving officer verifying the return converts a mandatory examination into a discretionary one, and is the cheapest step available.

The second concerns Rule 15 service. Because no examination follows it, the only occasion on which the findings Rule 15 requires can be tested is later — usually on an application under Order IX Rule 13 — and by then the endorsement is old and the signatory may be unavailable. Anybody relying on such service should want the serving officer's return to name the family member and state the relationship and the residence, because Rule 18 requires the name and address of the person identifying the person served, and the Explanation excluding a servant is not self-executing. Anybody attacking it must attack the endorsement on the material in it.

The third concerns the postal route. Rule 9(3) is permissive as to the mode and mandatory as to the expense, which falls on the plaintiff; Rule 9(5) is mandatory as to the declaration. The consequence is that a plaintiff choosing between the Court's officer and the post is not choosing merely between speed and cost. He is choosing between a return that a Court may examine and decline to act on, and an endorsement on which the Code directs the Court to declare service. Which of the two is preferable depends on which side of the suit one is on, and the choice is made at the time the summons is taken out, before anybody has had occasion to think about it.
