A federation has to decide something that a unitary State never has to think about. Its internal boundaries are real for the purpose of legislative power — each unit makes laws, levies taxes, and governs within its area — and the question is whether those boundaries are also real for the purpose of movement. May a province tax goods coming in from the next province? May it keep them out? May it give its own producers an advantage over producers elsewhere in the same country?

The question is not about trade policy. It is about what the federation is. A political union whose units may obstruct each other's commerce is a union in the sense that it has a common foreign policy and a common army, and in no sense that a person moving goods would recognise. A political union whose units may not obstruct each other's commerce has, by that fact, surrendered something that looks like an ordinary incident of self-government — the power of a legislature to decide the economic conditions prevailing in its own territory. Every federal constitution has to choose, and none of them chooses one extreme.

What makes the choice difficult is that the two things being separated are hard to keep apart. A tax on goods entering a State raises revenue and also deters their entry. A regulation on the sale of a commodity protects consumers and also suppresses competition from outside. A subsidy develops a backward region and also disadvantages producers in a neighbouring State. The same measure can be described honestly in two ways, and a constitution that forbids one description while permitting the other has set its courts a problem that no form of words fully solves.

The Constitution of India answers in Part XIII, which contains six operative articles and one that has been omitted. It is a short Part, and the shortness is deceptive: almost every clause in it either confers a power to restrict the freedom it has just declared, or saves something from that freedom, or redistributes the power to restrict between the Union and the States. The declaration occupies one sentence. The qualifications occupy the rest.

## What the Part provides

Part XIII is headed "Trade, Commerce and Intercourse within the Territory of India". Article 301 states the freedom:

> Subject to the other provisions of this Part, trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free.

Article 302 confers the first power to restrict it:

> Parliament may by law impose such restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce or intercourse between one State and another or within any part of the territory of India as may be required in the public interest.

Article 303 then limits both legislatures, and releases one of them. Clause (1) provides:

> Notwithstanding anything in article 302, neither Parliament nor the Legislature of a State shall have power to make any law giving, or authorising the giving of, any preference to one State over another, or making, or authorising the making of, any discrimination between one State and another, by virtue of any entry relating to trade and commerce in any of the Lists in the Seventh Schedule.

Clause (2) provides:

> Nothing in clause (1) shall prevent Parliament from making any law giving, or authorising the giving of, any preference or making, or authorising the making of, any discrimination if it is declared by such law that it is necessary to do so for the purpose of dealing with a situation arising from scarcity of goods in any part of the territory of India.

Article 304 confers the State's powers:

> Notwithstanding anything in article 301 or article 303, the Legislature of a State may by law—
>
> (a) impose on goods imported from other States or the Union territories any tax to which similar goods manufactured or produced in that State are subject, so, however, as not to discriminate between goods so imported and goods so manufactured or produced; and
>
> (b) impose such reasonable restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce or intercourse with or within that State as may be required in the public interest:

And then the proviso, which is the hinge of the Part:

> Provided that no Bill or amendment for the purposes of clause (b) shall be introduced or moved in the Legislature of a State without the previous sanction of the President.

Article 305, as substituted by the Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act, 1955 with effect from 27 April 1955, saves two classes of law:

> Nothing in articles 301 and 303 shall affect the provisions of any existing law except in so far as the President may by order otherwise direct; and nothing in article 301 shall affect the operation of any law made before the commencement of the Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act, 1955, in so far as it relates to, or prevent Parliament or the Legislature of a State from making any law relating to, any such matter as is referred to in sub-clause (ii) of clause (6) of article 19.

Article 306, which was headed "Power of certain States in Part B of the First Schedule to impose restrictions on trade and commerce", was omitted by the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956 with effect from 1 November 1956, when the classification of States into Parts A, B and C was abandoned.

Article 307 closes the Part:

> Parliament may by law appoint such authority as it considers appropriate for carrying out the purposes of articles 301, 302, 303 and 304, and confer on the authority so appointed such powers and such duties as it thinks necessary.

## A freedom with no provision behind it

The first thing to notice about Article 301 is where the qualification sits. The article does not declare a freedom and then provide, in a separate clause, for restrictions on it. It declares the freedom "[s]ubject to the other provisions of this Part", so that the qualification is inside the grant. Whatever Articles 302 to 305 permit is not an exception to Article 301; it is part of what Article 301 says.

That is a different construction from the one used for the rights in Part III, and the difference matters structurally rather than verbally. A Part III right is conferred in one clause and limited in another clause of the same article, and behind both stands Article 13(2), which provides that "The State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by this Part and any law made in contravention of this clause shall, to the extent of the contravention, be void." A law that exceeds the limitation clause is therefore void by force of a separate article addressed to the legislature.

Part XIII has no Article 13. There is no provision declaring that a law contravening Article 301 is void, and no provision addressed to the legislatures telling them not to make one. The invalidity of a law that restricts the freedom without the authority of Articles 302 to 305 follows from the ordinary proposition that a legislature cannot do what the Constitution has not empowered it to do, which is sufficient but is not the same thing as an express prohibition.

Nor is there an Article 32. The remedy in Article 32(1) is confined by its own terms: "The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the rights conferred by this Part is guaranteed." The rights conferred by Part XIII are not rights conferred by Part III, so Article 32 is not available for them. A person complaining that a State law obstructs inter-State trade proceeds under Article 226, or in a suit, and the difference is not merely one of forum. Article 32 is itself one of the rights in Part III, and stands on the footing of the rights it exists to enforce. Article 226 is a jurisdiction conferred on the High Courts, which Parliament's relationship to is of a different order from its relationship to Part III.

None of this weakens the freedom as a matter of substantive law, and courts have long described Article 301 as guaranteeing a freedom of trade. It does locate it. Whatever else Article 301 confers, it is not a fundamental right within the meaning of Part III, and it operates in the first place as a limit on legislative competence. The Part that contains it is drafted throughout as a distribution of power between the Union and the States rather than as a charter held by traders.

## Two powers to restrict, and three differences between them

The freedom may be restricted by Parliament under Article 302 and by a State under Article 304(b). Set the two clauses side by side and three differences appear, none of which is stated as a difference anywhere in the Part.

The first is the adjective. Article 302 permits "such restrictions ... as may be required in the public interest". Article 304(b) permits "such reasonable restrictions ... as may be required in the public interest". The word "reasonable" appears in the State's clause and not in Parliament's. On the face of the two provisions, a parliamentary restriction has to be required in the public interest and need not be reasonable; a State restriction has to be both.

The second is the territorial reach. Article 302 lets Parliament restrict trade "between one State and another or within any part of the territory of India" — which is to say, both across State boundaries and inside a single State. Article 304(b) lets a State restrict trade only "with or within that State".

That difference has to be stated carefully, because Article 302 is not a legislative entry. It lifts the bar in Article 301; it does not supply competence, which still has to be found in the Seventh Schedule. There, Entry 42 of List I is "Inter-State trade and commerce", while Entry 26 of List II gives a State "Trade and commerce within the State subject to the provisions of entry 33 of List III", and Entry 27 gives it "Production, supply and distribution of goods" on the same condition. Entry 33 of List III brings into the Concurrent List trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, the products of industries the Union has declared it expedient to control, together with foodstuffs, cattle fodder, raw cotton and raw jute. So the reach of Article 302 inside a State is real but is exercisable only over fields the Lists have already given the Union. What the words of Article 302 settle is that, where Parliament is competent, Part XIII will not stand in the way of its restricting intra-State trade — and a State enjoys no equivalent assurance about trade beyond its own borders.

The third is permission, and it is the substantial one. Parliament exercises its power under Article 302 by passing a law, and the Part requires nothing further. A State exercises its power under Article 304(b) only if the President has given previous sanction before the Bill was introduced or the amendment moved.

It is worth being precise about where that control operates, because it is unusual. The Constitution contains several provisions by which the Union participates in State legislation, and most of them operate at the end. Under Article 200 a Governor may reserve a Bill for the President's consideration, and under Article 201 the President then declares that he assents or that he withholds assent. Under Article 254(2) a State law repugnant to a law of Parliament on a Concurrent List subject prevails in that State if it was reserved for the President's consideration and received his assent. In each case the Bill has been passed by the State legislature before the Union's view is taken.

The proviso to Article 304(b) operates at the beginning. It does not provide that a Bill restricting trade shall be reserved, or that it requires the President's assent. It provides that no such Bill "shall be introduced or moved in the Legislature of a State without the previous sanction of the President". What the Union controls is not whether the measure becomes law. It is whether the State legislature may take the measure up at all.

That is a control over the agenda of an elected House, exercised by an authority outside the State and, through Article 74, on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers. The House is not being overruled on the merits of a decision it has taken; it is being prevented from deliberating. Among the Union's powers in relation to State legislation this is the only one of its kind in the Part, and the Constitution gives no reason for choosing that stage rather than the usual one.

## The bar both legislatures share, and the exit only one of them has

Article 303(1) is the one provision in the Part that limits Parliament and the States in the same words. Neither may give a preference to one State over another, or make a discrimination between one State and another, by virtue of a trade and commerce entry in the Seventh Schedule. The clause opens "[n]otwithstanding anything in article 302", so the bar prevails over the restricting power the preceding article confers on Parliament.

Then Article 303(2) releases Parliament, and the terms of the release are worth reading closely. Parliament may give a preference or make a discrimination "if it is declared by such law that it is necessary to do so for the purpose of dealing with a situation arising from scarcity of goods in any part of the territory of India". The condition is a declaration in the law itself. The clause does not require that a scarcity exist as a matter of fact found by anyone, does not require the declaration to state the material on which it rests, and does not provide for its review. Parliament certifies the occasion for its own departure from the bar, and the certificate is the thing the clause asks for.

There is no counterpart for a State. If a scarcity of goods arises in part of a State's territory, Article 303(2) gives the State legislature nothing; the bar in clause (1) continues to apply to it in full.

A State does, however, have Article 304, and the opening words of that article are wider than the discussion of its two clauses usually suggests. Article 304 begins: "Notwithstanding anything in article 301 or article 303". The non obstante names Article 303, not merely Article 301. On the words of the article, a State law falling within clause (a) or clause (b) operates notwithstanding the preference and discrimination bar in Article 303(1) — which would mean that the President's previous sanction under the proviso can unlock for a State exactly what Article 303(1) categorically denies to it, and that the Union executive holds a dispensing power over a bar that binds Parliament itself unless Parliament declares a scarcity.

Whether the article is to be read that way is not settled by anything in the Part, and the answers given by the Supreme Court in 2016, discussed below, do not address it. What can be said from the text alone is narrower and is enough for the present purpose. There are two stated routes out of the Article 303(1) bar. One belongs to Parliament and is exercised by Parliament's own declaration. The other, on the words of Article 304, requires the previous sanction of the President. Neither of them is the unaided judgment of a State legislature.

## The permission is cured by the person who should have given it

The proviso to Article 304(b) is the only Union control the Part imposes on a State's power to restrict trade, and it is weaker than it reads, because of a provision in another Part.

Article 255 is headed "Requirements as to recommendations and previous sanctions to be regarded as matters of procedure only". It provides that no Act of Parliament or of a State Legislature, and no provision in such an Act, "shall be invalid by reason only that some recommendation or previous sanction required by this Constitution was not given, if assent to that Act was given" — and, in clause (c), "where the recommendation or previous sanction required was that of the President, by the President".

The previous sanction required by the proviso to Article 304(b) is a previous sanction of the President. Article 255 therefore applies to it directly. A State Act imposing restrictions on trade in the public interest, introduced without the President's previous sanction, is not invalid on that ground alone if the President afterwards assents to it.

The logic of Article 255 is sound as far as it goes. A requirement of prior permission exists to secure the permitting authority's agreement, and agreement given afterwards shows that the authority had no objection; it would be a strange rule that struck down a law the President was content with because the formality had been taken in the wrong order. The same reasoning applies to the Governor's recommendation for a financial Bill under Article 207 and to the other requirements Article 255 covers.

What the combination produces in this Part, though, is a particular arrangement. The Union's control over a State's power to restrict trade is exercised at the stage of introduction, which is the stage at which it bites hardest on the legislature. And the failure to obtain it is cured by the assent of the same authority whose sanction was skipped. So the control is at once more intrusive than the Constitution's usual device — it reaches the House's agenda rather than the Bill's fate — and less binding, because its omission goes to procedure and not to competence. Both features follow from provisions that are individually unobjectionable, and neither is stated as a feature of Part XIII anywhere in Part XIII.

## Taking a trade over is easier than regulating it

Article 305 saves two classes of law from the freedom, and the second of them produces a result worth stating plainly.

The first limb saves existing law: "Nothing in articles 301 and 303 shall affect the provisions of any existing law except in so far as the President may by order otherwise direct." The term "existing law" is defined in Article 366(10) as a law, Ordinance, order, bye-law, rule or regulation "passed or made before the commencement of this Constitution" by a competent authority, so the class is closed and shrinking. The saving is from both the freedom and the preference bar, and it is defeasible — the President may, by order, direct otherwise, which gives the Union executive a power to withdraw the immunity of a pre-Constitution law without any legislation at all.

The second limb saves laws relating to State monopolies. Article 19(6)(ii) refers to "the carrying on by the State, or by a corporation owned or controlled by the State, of any trade, business, industry or service, whether to the exclusion, complete or partial, of citizens or otherwise." Article 305 provides that nothing in Article 301 shall affect the operation of a pre-Fourth Amendment law relating to such a matter, or "prevent Parliament or the Legislature of a State from making any law relating to" it.

Three things follow from the way that limb is drafted.

First, the power it preserves is prospective and unlimited in time. The immunity of *existing* monopoly laws is tied to laws made before the commencement of the Fourth Amendment on 27 April 1955. The power to *make* such laws carries no date at all. A State legislature may today enact a law by which the State takes a trade over to the complete exclusion of private citizens, and Article 301 will not affect it.

Second, the saving is from Article 301 only. The first limb of Article 305 saves existing law from "articles 301 and 303"; the second limb saves monopoly laws from "article 301" and says nothing about Article 303. A State monopoly law is therefore outside the freedom but, on the words of the article, still within the bar on preference and discrimination between States.

Third, and this is the structural point, the second limb requires no sanction of any kind. A State that wishes to impose a reasonable restriction on trade in the public interest must obtain the President's previous sanction before the Bill may be introduced. A State that wishes to exclude private trade from a field altogether, by carrying the business on itself, need obtain nothing. The greater interference with the freedom declared in Article 301 passes through a clause with no Union gate on it, while the lesser one passes through a clause with a gate at the earliest possible stage.

There is a historical explanation for the asymmetry, and it is not a justification of it. The second limb of Article 305 was put there by the Fourth Amendment in 1955, whose section 4 substituted Article 305 and whose section 3 substituted clause (1) of Article 31A and inserted three further clauses into it, among them the clause dealing with the taking over of the management of property by the State. The amendment belongs to a set of changes directed at insulating State acquisition and State undertaking of business from constitutional challenge. The clause was inserted to protect a programme, and it did so by exempting the programme from the freedom. The proviso to Article 304(b), by contrast, was part of the original Constitution, and was addressed to a different anxiety: that States which had recently maintained their own trade barriers would re-erect them. Each provision answers the question its drafters were asking. Read together, they rank the two interferences in the opposite order from the one the freedom would suggest.

## What 2016 settled, and which clause it left in charge

Part XIII has generated a long line of authority, and in 2016 a Bench of nine Judges reorganised it. In *Jindal Stainless Ltd. v. State of Haryana*, (2017) 12 SCC 1, decided on 11 November 2016, the Court answered a reference by majority. The order recorded the answers in ten numbered propositions, of which the first six are the ones that bear on the structure of the Part:

> 1. Taxes simpliciter are not within the contemplation of Part XIII of the Constitution of India. The word 'Free' used in Article 301 does not mean "free from taxation".
>
> 2. Only such taxes as are discriminatory in nature are prohibited by Article 304(a). It follows that levy of a non-discriminatory tax would not constitute an infraction of Article 301.
>
> 3. Clauses (a) and (b) of Article 304 have to be read disjunctively.
>
> 4. A levy that violates 304(a) cannot be saved even if the procedure under Article 304(b) or the proviso there under is satisfied.
>
> 5. The compensatory tax theory evolved in Automobile Transport case and subsequently modified in Jindal's case has no juristic basis and is therefore rejected.
>
> 6. Decisions of this Court in Atiabari, Automobile Transport and Jindal cases (supra) and all other judgments that follow these pronouncements are to the extent of such reliance over ruled.

The decisions so dealt with are *Atiabari Tea Co. Ltd. v. State of Assam*, AIR 1961 SC 232, *Automobile Transport (Rajasthan) Ltd. v. State of Rajasthan*, AIR 1962 SC 1406, and *Jindal Stainless Ltd. (2) v. State of Haryana*, (2006) 7 SCC 241, the last being the judgment of a Constitution Bench of five Judges delivered on 13 April 2006, which had laid down the tests for determining when a tax was compensatory. The further propositions in the 2016 order held that a tax on the entry of goods into a local area is permissible although similar goods are not produced in the taxing State; that Article 304(a) "frowns upon discrimination (of a hostile nature in the protectionist sense) and not on mere differentiation", so that time-limited incentives granted in a non-hostile fashion to develop economically backward areas do not violate it; and that States "are well within their right to design their fiscal legislations to ensure that the tax burden on goods imported from other States and goods produced within the State fall equally".

The effect on the architecture of the Part is considerable, and it runs in a direction the preceding sections of this post have been pointing at.

Before 2016, the question whether a tax offended Part XIII was asked under Article 301, through a judicially developed doctrine about which taxes were restrictions on the freedom and which were compensatory payments for facilities provided. After 2016, taxes are outside Article 301 altogether, and the question is asked under Article 304(a): is the tax on imported goods discriminatory as against goods produced in the State?

Article 304(a) is the one operative clause in Part XIII that requires no permission from anybody. It confers a power on a State legislature, states its own internal condition — the tax must be one "to which similar goods manufactured or produced in that State are subject", and must not "discriminate between goods so imported and goods so manufactured or produced" — and stops. The proviso requiring the President's previous sanction is expressly confined to clause (b). The Court's third answer, that clauses (a) and (b) are to be read disjunctively, and its fourth, that a levy violating clause (a) cannot be saved even if the proviso has been complied with, settle that the sanction has nothing to do with clause (a) in either direction. It is neither required for a tax nor capable of rescuing a discriminatory one.

So the clause that now carries the main weight of the Part is the clause with no Union gate and a test stated in the text, and the clause that carries the Union gate governs non-fiscal restrictions. That is a more coherent division than the one it replaced, because a condition written into the Constitution is a better instrument than a doctrine about the character of a levy, and because it puts the non-discrimination requirement where a federal common market's central requirement belongs — in a rule about equal treatment rather than in a rule about permission. It also means that the proviso to Article 304(b), the Part's most intrusive provision, now operates on a narrower field than it did.

## The authority the Part contemplates

Article 307 is the last provision of Part XIII, and it is the only machinery the Part contains for carrying its own purposes into effect. Parliament "may by law appoint such authority as it considers appropriate for carrying out the purposes of articles 301, 302, 303 and 304, and confer on the authority so appointed such powers and such duties as it thinks necessary."

Everything in that sentence is discretionary. Whether to appoint is for Parliament. Which authority is appropriate is for Parliament. What powers and duties it should have is for Parliament. The article names the four provisions the authority would exist to carry out, and attaches no consequence to its not being created, no period within which it should be, and no minimum content for the powers if it is.

The design question this raises is not whether such an authority ought to exist. It is which organ was given the decision. The purposes to be carried out are the purposes of a Part whose principal practical effect is to limit what legislatures — including Parliament — may do to inter-State trade. Article 302 gives Parliament a restricting power subject only to the public interest. Article 303(2) lets Parliament escape the non-discrimination bar on its own declaration. And Article 307 leaves the creation of the Part's enforcement machinery to that same Parliament, to be designed as it thinks necessary.

Whether and in what form an authority exists under Article 307 at any given moment is a question about the legislative record rather than about the Constitution. The constitutional point is prior to it: the freedom declared in Article 301 has no institutional guardian that the Constitution itself establishes. Its only guardians are the courts, exercising a jurisdiction conferred by Article 226 and by Article 136, on cases brought by whoever is willing to bring them.

It is worth noticing where the Constitution did eventually place an institution concerned with the internal market, because the location is instructive. The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016 inserted Article 279A, which required the President to constitute a Goods and Services Tax Council within sixty days of that Act's commencement. The Council consists of the Union Finance Minister as Chairperson, the Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance, and the Minister in charge of Finance or Taxation or another nominated Minister from each State Government. Its function under clause (4) is to "make recommendations to the Union and the States" on the taxes to be subsumed in the goods and services tax, the goods and services to be taxed or exempted, the model laws and the principles of levy, among other matters.

Article 279A sits in Part XII, among the finance articles, and not in Part XIII. It is a body of Union and State executives making recommendations, constituted by Presidential order rather than by law, and its subject is a tax rather than the freedom of trade. It is not an authority appointed under Article 307 and does not purport to be one. The one standing institution in which the Union and the States together consider the conditions of commerce across the country was created outside the Part that declares commerce to be free, and created as an organ of the executives rather than of the Constitution's limitation on them.

The same amendment then did something for that Council which the Constitution has never done for Part XIII. Section 15 of the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016 added Article 279A to the list in the proviso to Article 368(2), with effect from 16 September 2016, so that an amendment seeking to make any change in it must "also require to be ratified by the Legislatures of not less than one-half of the States". No clause of that proviso names Part XIII or any article in it. An amendment narrowing the freedom of trade, or deleting the proviso to Article 304(b), or repealing Article 307 outright, needs the special majority in each House of Parliament and nothing more; an amendment altering the composition or functions of the Goods and Services Tax Council needs the agreement of half the State legislatures as well. The Part that limits what the States may do to each other's commerce is amendable without consulting them. The body on which they sit is not.

## What the arrangement assumes

Read as a whole, Part XIII distributes the power to interfere with a single internal market in a pattern that follows no single principle.

Parliament may restrict the freedom for the public interest, anywhere in India, without any permission and without a requirement of reasonableness, and may make an otherwise forbidden discrimination between States by declaring a scarcity in its own law. A State may restrict the freedom within its own territory only if the restriction is reasonable, and only after the Union executive has permitted the Bill to be introduced — a permission whose absence the same authority's later assent repairs. A State may tax imported goods, without anybody's permission, on the condition of non-discrimination, and after 2016 that condition carries most of the Part's practical work. A State or Parliament may take a trade over completely, and Article 301 does not reach the law by which it does so. And the Part's own enforcement machinery is optional, in the gift of the legislature whose power it would be designed to check.

Three of those arrangements are defensible on their own terms. The Union's wider power is defensible because the Union is answerable to a legislature representing the whole country, and a national market needs a national regulator. The State's narrower power is defensible because a State legislature answers only to its own electorate and has an interest in favouring its own producers at the expense of people who did not elect it. The non-discrimination condition in Article 304(a) is defensible because it is the central rule of any common market, and it is in the text.

The arrangement that does not fit is the one in Article 305. The reason for subjecting a State's restriction on trade to Union permission — that a State's judgment about the interests of outsiders cannot be trusted — applies with at least as much force to a State's decision to exclude outsiders from a trade entirely. The Constitution reached the opposite result, not by deciding the question, but because a provision inserted in 1955 for a different purpose exempted State monopolies from the freedom while leaving the proviso to Article 304(b) where it was.

And underneath all of it is the assumption that makes the Part work at all. The freedom in Article 301 is not a right held against the State; it is a limit on legislative competence, with no Article 13 to declare contravening laws void, no Article 32 to enforce it, and no authority of its own. It depends on being litigated. For a trader obstructed at a State boundary, the constitutional guarantee that commerce throughout India shall be free is a proposition he must establish in a court, against a government, at his own cost, in a forum the Constitution did not reserve for him. That the Part has worked as well as it has is a fact about the courts rather than about its drafting.
