The distinction between primary and secondary evidence is not a rule about reliability. It is a rule that presupposes a particular fact about documents — that there is one of them. A deed exists as a single object; a copy of it is a second object made from the first, and the difference between the two is that one of them was there when the transaction happened and the other was made afterwards by someone whose fidelity is a question. Produce the first and the question does not arise. That is the whole of the best-evidence principle, and it works because the thing it governs has an original.

An electronic record does not. A file written to disk is not the original of the copy that is sent, cached, backed up or opened; all of them are the same sequence of bits, produced by a process that is lossless and leaves no mark of its own. There is no object in the series that was present at the event in a way the others were not, and no inspection of any one of them will disclose which came first. The category the law of documents is built on has no application to the material, and a code that has to deal with that material must do one of two things. It can decline to apply the category and give electronic records a channel of their own. Or it can apply the category by declaration, and say which of the indistinguishable instances the law will treat as primary.

The Indian Evidence Act, 1872 took the first course. The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 takes the second, in four new Explanations to section 57. What it does not do is attach a consequence to the declaration, and the provision that prevents it from doing so is three sections further on.

## The category the 1872 Act declined to extend

It is worth being exact about what the 1872 Act did, because the common description of it — that electronic records were secondary evidence requiring a certificate — is not what the Act said.

The Act defined "document" in section 3 as any matter expressed or described upon any substance by means of letters, figures or marks, intended to be used or which may be used for the purpose of recording that matter. The definition ended there. The Information Technology Act, 2000 did amend section 3 of the Evidence Act — by its section 92 and Second Schedule — but what it amended was the definition of "evidence", the second clause of which was substituted to read "all documents including electronic records produced for the inspection of the Court". The definition of "document" was left as it stood. So an electronic record was documentary evidence, and it was not, by the Act's own definition, a document.

Section 62 then defined primary evidence as "the document itself produced for the inspection of the Court", with two Explanations — the first covering documents executed in several parts and in counterpart, the second covering documents made by one uniform process such as printing, lithography or photography. Section 64 directed that documents "must be proved by primary evidence except in the cases hereinafter mentioned". Section 63 listed five kinds of secondary evidence, none of which is an electronic record.

What the 2000 amendment inserted instead was a separate channel. Section 65A provided: "The contents of electronic records may be proved in accordance with the provisions of section 65B." Section 65B then made a computer output "deemed to be also a document" if four conditions were satisfied, admissible "without further proof or production of the original", and supported by a certificate under sub-section (4).

That architecture has a logic to it. The primary/secondary distinction was left alone, and not applied; the words "without further proof or production of the original" in section 65B(1) are the Act's acknowledgment that there is no original to produce. Whether the certificate was mandatory, and what happened when the record was in the hands of the opposite party, were questions litigated for two decades, and this note does not depend on how they were answered. The structural point is narrower: under the 1872 Act, no provision said that an electronic record was primary evidence, so no argument could be built on the proposition that it was.

## What section 57 now declares

The Adhiniyam changes the definition first. Section 2(1)(d) defines "document" as before and then adds five words: it "includes electronic and digital records". The illustrations to the clause are new as well, and the sixth of them is a list:

> "An electronic record on emails, server logs, documents on computers, laptop or smartphone, messages, websites, locational evidence and voice mail messages stored on digital devices are documents"

Section 2(1)(e)(ii) carries the change into the definition of evidence, which now means "all documents including electronic or digital records produced for the inspection of the Court".

Section 57 then reproduces the 1872 definition of primary evidence word for word — "Primary evidence means the document itself produced for the inspection of the Court" — and carries over the old Explanations with a renumbering that is easy to misread. The 1872 Act's Explanation 1 ran to two paragraphs, one on documents executed in several parts and one on counterparts. The Adhiniyam splits those into its Explanations 1 and 2, which makes the 1872 Act's Explanation 2 (the uniform-process rule) the Adhiniyam's Explanation 3. Explanations 1 to 3 are therefore old material rearranged.

Explanations 4 to 7 are new, and they are the subject of this note:

> "Explanation 4.—Where an electronic or digital record is created or stored, and such storage occurs simultaneously or sequentially in multiple files, each such file is primary evidence.
>
> Explanation 5.—Where an electronic or digital record is produced from proper custody, such electronic and digital record is primary evidence unless it is disputed.
>
> Explanation 6.—Where a video recording is simultaneously stored in electronic form and transmitted or broadcast or transferred to another, each of the stored recordings is primary evidence.
>
> Explanation 7.—Where an electronic or digital record is stored in multiple storage spaces in a computer resource, each such automated storage, including temporary files, is primary evidence."

The device is the one the 1872 Act used for printed placards, extended. Rather than deny that an electronic record has an original, the Adhiniyam declares that it has many, and that each of them is primary evidence of the contents of the rest. On the material, that is the right answer. If every instance is bit-identical and none is prior in any respect an inspection could reveal, then either all of them are primary or none is, and treating all of them as primary is the only version of that choice which lets a party prove anything.

Two of the four Explanations go further than the symmetry requires. Explanation 6 is about a single class of record — video — and says of it what Explanation 4 already says of records generally, adding the case where the recording is transmitted or broadcast rather than merely stored twice. Explanation 7 names temporary files. A cache file, a swap file, an autosave, the copy a messaging application writes while a video is being forwarded: each is, on the words, primary evidence of the contents of the record it was made from. The reach of that is considerable and it runs in both directions. It assists the party who has only a cached copy, because he no longer has to explain the absence of something better. It exposes the party whose device holds temporary copies, because a file he never knowingly saved is now primary evidence against him of what the original contained.

## The clause that decides proof

Having declared the status, the Adhiniyam then puts the declaration beyond the reach of the question it would otherwise decide. The sequence occupies five sections and is best read in order.

Section 59 is the direction to use primary evidence, and it is not absolute:

> "Documents shall be proved by primary evidence except in the cases hereinafter mentioned."

Section 60 lists the cases in which secondary evidence may be given — original with the opponent, admitted in writing, destroyed or lost, not easily movable, a public document, a document of which a certified copy is permitted, and numerous accounts. Then comes section 61:

> "Nothing in this Adhiniyam shall apply to deny the admissibility of an electronic or digital record in the evidence on the ground that it is an electronic or digital record and such record shall, subject to section 63, have the same legal effect, validity and enforceability as other document."

The first half of that sentence has no counterpart in the 1872 Act and is a rule of non-discrimination: admissibility may not be refused on the ground of the medium. The second half is the one that matters here. The record has the same legal effect as any other document — *subject to section 63*.

Section 62 is section 65A of the old Act, unchanged in substance:

> "The contents of electronic records may be proved in accordance with the provisions of section 63."

And section 63(1) opens as section 65B(1) opened:

> "Notwithstanding anything contained in this Adhiniyam, any information contained in an electronic record which is printed on paper, stored, recorded or copied in optical or magnetic media or semiconductor memory which is produced by a computer or any communication device or otherwise stored, recorded or copied in any electronic form (hereinafter referred to as the computer output) shall be deemed to be also a document, if the conditions mentioned in this section are satisfied..."

Put the five together. Section 59 directs proof by primary evidence except in the cases hereinafter mentioned. Sections 61 to 63 are hereinafter. Section 61 makes the record's legal effect subject to section 63. Section 62 channels proof of the contents of electronic records to section 63. And section 63 begins by overriding anything contained in the Adhiniyam — which includes section 57 and the four Explanations the Adhiniyam has just added to it.

So the Explanations settle what an electronic record is in the taxonomy of documents. They do not settle how its contents are proved, because the provision that settles that is drafted to displace them. A party who produces a WhatsApp message from the handset on which it was received can say, correctly, that the stored file is primary evidence under Explanation 4 and that the temporary copies on the same device are primary evidence under Explanation 7. What he cannot say on the text is that this dispenses with section 63, because section 62 routes the proof of contents to section 63 and section 63 is non obstante.

That is, in my view, the better reading, and it should be stated with its weakness showing. The 1872 Act gave no foothold for the contrary argument at all, since it never called an electronic record primary evidence. The Adhiniyam supplies the foothold and then relies on a non obstante clause to remove it. It would have taken one line in either section to say so expressly — that nothing in section 57 dispenses with the certificate under section 63(4), or conversely that section 63 does not apply where the record is primary evidence under section 57. Neither line was written, and the question is therefore left to be settled by the relationship between a declaration and an overriding clause, which is the least informative way a statute can settle anything.

## The Explanation that depends on the other side

Explanation 5 is unlike the other six in the section, and the difference is in its last four words.

> "Where an electronic or digital record is produced from proper custody, such electronic and digital record is primary evidence unless it is disputed."

Every other Explanation to section 57, old and new, states a conclusion that follows from the facts it describes. A document executed in parts: each part is primary evidence. Placards printed from one original: each is primary evidence of the contents of the rest. Storage in multiple files: each file is primary evidence. None of them asks what the opposite party says.

Explanation 5 does. A record produced from proper custody is primary evidence *unless it is disputed*, which means that the status is defeated by the other side's objection rather than by anything about the record. Whatever that is, it is not a rule of the law of documents. A category that a party can exit by asserting that he disputes it is a presumption, and a presumption needs two things the Explanation does not supply: a statement of what must be disputed, and a statement of what happens next.

On the first, the Explanation does not say whether the dispute must be as to the custody, the authenticity of the record, or the truth of its contents. The three are different objections with different consequences, and the first of them — a dispute about whether the custody was proper — would at least be an objection to the premise of the Explanation. A dispute about contents is not, and on the words it is enough. On the second, the Explanation does not say what the record becomes. It does not become secondary evidence, because section 58 lists eight kinds of secondary evidence and a disputed electronic record is not among them. It presumably reverts to being proved under section 63, which is where section 62 sent it in any event.

The practical effect is that the one new Explanation which tracks the ordinary practice of a courtroom — a document produced from the custody in which it would be expected to be found — is the only one an opponent can switch off by saying a word in his objection; while the three that describe technical facts about storage, including the Explanation about temporary files, are unconditional and cannot be switched off at all.

## The certificate, and the two additions to it

Section 63 largely restates section 65B, with changes that widen it. Sub-section (1) adds semiconductor memory to the media, adds "any communication device" to the computer, and adds the catch-all "or otherwise stored, recorded or copied in any electronic form". Sub-section (2) adds "create" to the storing and processing in clause (a), and adds communication devices throughout. Sub-section (3) replaces the old reference to computers used in combination with five lettered cases — standalone mode, a computer system, a computer network, a computer resource, and "through an intermediary" — all of which are to be treated as a single computer.

Sub-section (4) is where the two substantive additions are. The old section spoke of "a certificate doing any of the following things" which, when signed as the sub-section required, "shall be evidence of any matter stated in the certificate" — it said nothing about when the certificate was to be filed, or how often. The new sub-section says: the certificate "shall be submitted along with the electronic record at each instance where it is being submitted for admission". The words "at each instance" are new. A certificate is no longer a document filed once in the proceeding; it accompanies each submission of the record for admission.

The second addition is in the signature. Under section 65B(4) the certificate was to be signed by "a person occupying a responsible official position in relation to the operation of the relevant device or the management of the relevant activities (whichever is appropriate)". Section 63(4) requires that it purport to be signed by

> "a person in charge of the computer or communication device or the management of the relevant activities (whichever is appropriate) and an expert"

An expert is now a necessary signatory, and the Schedule reflects this in its structure: Part A is "To be filled by the Party" and Part B "To be filled by the Expert", each asking for the make, model, serial number and IMEI or equivalent identifier of the device, and each asking for the hash value of the record and the algorithm used — SHA1, SHA256, MD5 or another legally acceptable standard — with the hash report to be enclosed.

The sub-section also tells the reader where the certificate is to be found, but it tells him in an odd place. The reference to the Schedule arrives at the very end of the sub-section, attached to the clause about knowledge and belief: it is sufficient for a matter "to be stated to the best of the knowledge and belief of the person stating it in the certificate specified in the Schedule". Read literally, the Schedule is identified as a qualification on the standard of affirmation rather than as the prescribed form, and the requirement to use that form has to be gathered from the Schedule's own heading, which cites section 63(4)(c). Nothing turns on this — the form is plainly prescribed — but a statute that makes a form compulsory usually says so in the sub-section that requires it.

Who the expert is, the section does not say. Section 39(2) makes relevant the opinion of the Examiner of Electronic Evidence referred to in section 79A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 where the Court has to form an opinion on information stored in any computer resource, but section 63(4) does not use that term, and nothing in it requires the expert to be a notified Examiner. Which is as well, given how many certificates the sub-section now requires and how often an expert must sign one; but it leaves the qualification of a necessary signatory unstated.

## The hash value, and the three places the Act puts the same file

The Statement of Objects and Reasons appended to the Adhiniyam describes what the Bill was to do, and its fourth paragraph includes this, among the provisions relating to secondary evidence:

> "it seeks to expand the scope of secondary evidence to include copies made from original by mechanical processes, copies made from or compared with the original, counterparts of documents as against the parties who did not execute them and oral accounts of the contents of a document given by some person who has himself seen it and giving matching hash value of original record will be admissible as proof of evidence in the form of secondary evidence"

The first part of that is an accurate description of section 58, which does expand the 1872 list from five clauses to eight. The last part is not, and the gap is worth noting precisely because the Statement of Objects and Reasons cannot control the enacted text and nothing turns on it as a matter of construction. Section 58 lists eight kinds of secondary evidence, and a matching hash value is not one of them. The hash appears in the Adhiniyam in exactly one place: the Schedule, which is headed "[See section 63(4)(c)]" and is the certificate accompanying a computer output admitted under section 63.

So the same file is characterised three ways in one statute. The Statement of Objects and Reasons treats a hash match as a route to secondary evidence. Section 57 declares the file, and its temporary copies, to be primary evidence. And sections 62 and 63 make the hash a particular to be affirmed in a certificate so that the output may be deemed to be also a document. Only the second and third of those are enacted, and the third overrides the second. But the Statement of Objects and Reasons is the Act's own account of its purpose, and the account does not match the text on the one point where the text is doing something new.

## Which records are governed by which regime

Section 170 repeals the 1872 Act and saves pending matters:

> "Notwithstanding such repeal, if, immediately before the date on which this Adhiniyam comes into force, there is any application, trial, inquiry, investigation, proceeding or appeal pending, then, such application, trial, inquiry, investigation, proceeding or appeal shall be dealt with under the provisions of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (1 of 1872), as in force immediately before such commencement, as if this Adhiniyam had not come into force."

The Adhiniyam is Act 47 of 2023, assented to on 25 December 2023, and was brought into force on 1 July 2024 by notification S.O. 849(E) dated 23 February 2024.

The saving is keyed to the pendency of the proceeding, not to the date of the record or to the date of the conduct it evidences. That is the ordinary and correct way to write a savings clause for an adjective law, because the law of evidence governs the conduct of a trial rather than the rights it adjudicates, and a trial should not change its rules of proof halfway. But the consequence is that for as long as proceedings instituted before 1 July 2024 remain pending — which, in the trial courts, will be a long time — the same file will carry two different characterisations in the same building. Tendered in a matter pending on that date, it is a computer output under section 65B and is not primary evidence of anything, because the 1872 Act never said it was. Tendered in a matter instituted afterwards, it is primary evidence under section 57 and a computer output under section 63 at once.

## What the Explanations do

The Adhiniyam has done something the 1872 Act, through the 2000 amendment, did not do. It has brought electronic records inside the definition of "document" and inside the primary/secondary taxonomy, and it has resolved the problem that kept them out — the absence of an original — in the only way the material permits, by declaring that each instance is primary.

Having done that, it has left the declaration without work in the one place it would have mattered. Section 59 would have made a file that is primary evidence provable by production. Section 62 does not let it, and section 63 begins by saying that nothing in the Adhiniyam is to the contrary. The effect is that section 57's four new Explanations tell a court what an electronic record is and tell it nothing about what to do with one.

A declaration of status with no consequence attached is not a harmless surplus. It is an argument, available to be made in every matter in which a certificate is missing or defective, resting on words Parliament enacted and on a section that directs proof by primary evidence. The answer to it is good, but it is an answer assembled from a "subject to" in section 61, a channelling provision in section 62 and a non obstante clause in section 63 — and it has to be assembled afresh each time, because the Adhiniyam nowhere states the relationship between sections 57 and 63 in terms. The drafting required to settle it was one sentence. A statute that was willing to write seven Explanations to section 57 and a two-part Schedule to section 63 had the room.
