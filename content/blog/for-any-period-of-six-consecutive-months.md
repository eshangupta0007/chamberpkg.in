A parliamentary system is usually explained by the location of its executive. The people who exercise executive power are not separately elected; they are drawn from the legislature, sit in it, answer questions in it, and lose office when it withdraws confidence. The justification for giving unelected office enormous power is that the office is not unelected — the holder sits in the chamber as a member, and the chamber can remove him twice over, once from the ministry and once from the House.

The Constitution of India does not quite adopt that account. It permits a person who is in neither House to be appointed a Minister, and then provides for what happens if he stays out. Article 75(5) reads: "A Minister who for any period of six consecutive months is not a member of either House of Parliament shall at the expiration of that period cease to be a Minister." Article 164(4) says the same for a State, with "the Legislature of the State" in place of "either House of Parliament."

The clause is drafted as a termination, not as a permission. Nowhere does the Constitution say that a non-member may be appointed. What it says is when a non-member stops being a Minister, and the permission is inferred backwards from the limit. That inference is unavoidable — a provision stating when a non-member Minister ceases to hold office would be meaningless if a non-member could not hold it — but it is worth noticing that the only textual acknowledgment of the possibility is a clause written to end it.

This piece sets out what the two clauses do and do not settle: what the six months are measured from, which Houses count, what a non-member Minister may do inside the chamber, what qualifications the Constitution requires of a Minister as distinct from a member, and why the office-of-profit clauses had to say expressly that being a Minister is not an office of profit.

## The clause that works by expiry

Both clauses operate by lapse of time rather than by any act of any authority. No one declares the Minister's seat vacant. No one moves a motion. The Speaker makes no ruling, the Governor issues no order, and the President passes no notification. At the end of the sixth month of continuous non-membership, the person "shall at the expiration of that period cease to be a Minister."

This distinguishes the clause from almost every other way ministerial office ends. Under Article 75(2) the Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President; under Article 164(1) during the pleasure of the Governor. Pleasure requires a decision by someone. Resignation requires an act by the Minister. A vote of no confidence requires the House to act, and the consequence — the resignation of the Council of Ministers — follows from the collective responsibility stated in Article 75(3) and Article 164(2) rather than from any express clause about what a lost vote does.

Articles 75(5) and 164(4) require nothing of anybody. Other offices in the Constitution also end with time, but they end on a term: the President holds office for five years from the date on which he enters upon it, under Article 56(1); the House of the People continues for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting and no longer, under Article 83(2). A term is fixed when the office is taken and runs to a date that is known then. The six months are not a term. They are the duration of a condition, they begin when the condition begins, and they never begin at all if the condition does not arise.

That construction has a consequence for enforcement. Where a clause operates automatically, nothing in the text identifies who is to notice that it has operated. The Minister's own view that he remains in office is not made conclusive, and no authority is nominated to decide otherwise. By contrast, the Constitution is careful about this elsewhere: Article 103 refers a question about a sitting member's disqualification to the President for decision, and Article 192 does the same for a State, with the Governor. For the cessation of a Minister under Article 75(5), there is no referee named at all. The clause assumes that cessation by efflux of time needs no adjudication because the fact — membership or not, six months or not — is not the kind of fact that can be in dispute.

## What the six months measure

The clause does not say "within six months of his appointment." It says "for any period of six consecutive months is not a member". What is measured is a state of affairs, not the interval since an event.

For the ordinary case the two readings coincide. A person outside both Houses is appointed a Minister; from the date of appointment he is a Minister who is not a member; six consecutive months later he ceases. Nothing turns on the distinction.

The distinction appears in the other case — where a person who is already a Minister stops being a member. That happens in several ways. A Minister in the Council of States reaches the end of his term and is not re-elected. A Minister resigns his seat. A Minister's election is set aside. And an Assembly is dissolved, which ends the membership of everyone in it. The Constitution attaches no consequence to that so far as ministerial office is concerned: Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the Governor, and nothing in Article 164 provides that the office ends when the Assembly does.

On the words of Article 164(4), the trigger is non-membership for six consecutive months, however the non-membership comes about. The clause does not distinguish between a person who was never a member and a person who has ceased to be one, and it does not supply a different starting point for the second case. It is tempting to answer the point from Article 174(1), which requires that six months shall not intervene between the last sitting of a House in one session and the date appointed for its first sitting in the next. But that clause measures the gap between sessions of a House that exists. It says nothing about the interval between the dissolution of one Assembly and the first sitting of the next, which is the interval during which an outgoing Council of Ministers consists of people who are not members of anything.

So the text does not resolve the question; it does not address it. The reason it does not is visible in the drafting. The clause was written with the appointed outsider in view, and was expressed as a condition — non-membership for six consecutive months — wide enough to catch a situation it was not aimed at.

## The two routes back in, and the State that has only one

The two clauses are not identical in the exit they leave open. Article 75(5) measures membership of "either House of Parliament." A Union Minister appointed from outside may therefore be returned either by a general constituency to the House of the People or by the elected members of a State legislature to the Council of States. The second route does not require a popular election at all.

Article 164(4) measures membership of "the Legislature of the State." Under Article 168 the Legislature of a State consists of the Governor and either one or two Houses. In the States that have a Legislative Council, a Minister appointed from outside may be brought in through the Council — again, without facing a general electorate. In the States that have only a Legislative Assembly, the single route is a by-election to the Assembly.

The consequence is that the same constitutional provision, identically worded, is much easier to satisfy in some States than in others, and the difference does not follow anything about the States. It follows whether the State had a second chamber under Article 168 as enacted, or has since acquired or lost one under Article 169 — which turns on a resolution of its own Assembly and a law of Parliament. A clause whose evident function is to require a non-member Minister to obtain a seat within six months therefore requires seats of quite different kinds, and the difference tracks a feature of State structure that has nothing to do with ministerial accountability.

For Delhi, the Constitution's own provision is silent. Article 239AA(4) constitutes a Council of Ministers of not more than ten per cent of the total number of members in the Legislative Assembly, and Article 239AA(5) provides for appointment by the President on the advice of the Chief Minister and for tenure during the President's pleasure. There is no counterpart to Article 75(5) or Article 164(4) in the article. What the article does instead is give Parliament power, under clause (7)(a), to make provisions for giving effect to or supplementing the foregoing clauses. The six-month rule, which the Constitution writes out twice for the Union and the States, is for the National Capital Territory a matter the Constitution leaves to legislation.

## The seat in the chamber that is not a seat

A non-member Minister is not excluded from the House. Article 88 provides that every Minister, and the Attorney-General of India, "shall have the right to speak in, and otherwise to take part in the proceedings of, either House, any joint sitting of the Houses, and any committee of Parliament of which he may be named a member, but shall not by virtue of this article be entitled to vote." Article 177 does the same in a State for every Minister and the Advocate-General.

Three features of that provision are worth separating.

First, the right is conferred on Ministers as such, not on member-Ministers. A Minister who is in neither House has exactly the same right to speak in either House as a Minister who sits in one of them. He may move a Bill, reply to a debate on it, answer questions on his portfolio and appear before a committee of which he is named a member. The whole visible work of a Minister inside the legislature is available to him.

Second, the vote is withheld in a particular way. The article does not say that a Minister may not vote; it says he shall not "by virtue of this article" be entitled to vote. The qualification is necessary because most Ministers do vote — not under Article 88, but as members of the House in which they sit. Article 88 is the source of the right to be present and to speak in the House of which one is not a member; voting rights come from membership alone. The clause therefore separates the two things that membership ordinarily bundles together, and gives a Minister the first without the second.

Third, the same right is given to the Attorney-General and the Advocate-General — law officers who are not Ministers, are not members, and are not collectively responsible to anyone. Their presence in the article tells us what kind of right it is. It is not an incident of political accountability. It is a right of access granted to the people the executive needs to have heard in the chamber.

The penalty clauses confirm the division. Article 104 makes a person liable to five hundred rupees a day if he "sits or votes as a member of either House of Parliament" before taking the oath under Article 99, or when he knows he is not qualified or is disqualified. Article 193 says the same for a State. A non-member Minister speaking in the House under Article 88 is not sitting as a member, has taken no member's oath, and is not within the penalty. He has taken the oaths of office and of secrecy under Article 75(4) or Article 164(3), in the forms set out in the Third Schedule, which are the oaths of a Minister and not of a member.

## Responsible to a House one need not sit in

Article 75(3) provides that the Council of Ministers shall be collectively responsible to the House of the People. Article 164(2) provides that it shall be collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly of the State.

Both clauses name one House. At the Union, a Minister who sits in the Council of States is collectively responsible to the House of the People — a chamber he is not a member of, cannot vote in, and can address only by virtue of Article 88. In a State with a Legislative Council the same is true of a Minister who sits in the Council. And a Minister who sits in neither is collectively responsible to an Assembly or House in which he has no seat at all.

This is not an oversight. Responsibility in the clause is collective and it is the Council of Ministers that bears it, not the individual Minister; what the clause secures is that the Government as a body falls when the popular chamber withdraws its confidence. A Minister's own membership is beside the point for that purpose, which is precisely why the Constitution could afford to be relaxed about it in Article 75(5) and Article 164(4). The device that makes the executive answerable is the confidence of one named chamber in the Council as a whole. Individual membership is a secondary matter, and the six-month clause treats it as one.

It is worth being exact about what follows. The non-member Minister is not unaccountable. He holds office during pleasure, he belongs to a body that must retain the confidence of the popular House, and he can be questioned on the floor of that House under Article 88. What he lacks is the thing a member has and he does not: a constituency that chose him and can refuse him next time. The six-month clause exists to supply that lack within a fixed period, and it is the only provision in the Constitution that addresses it.

## What the Constitution requires of a Minister

Article 84 states the qualifications for membership of Parliament: citizenship of India together with an oath or affirmation before a person authorised by the Election Commission; a minimum age of thirty for the Council of States and twenty-five for the House of the People; and such other qualifications as Parliament may prescribe. Article 173 states the corresponding qualifications for a State legislature.

Neither article is extended to Ministers. Article 75 does not require that a Minister satisfy Article 84, and Article 164 does not require that he satisfy Article 173. Articles 102 and 191, which set out the disqualifications for membership, are addressed to a person "being chosen as, and for being, a member" — not to a person being appointed a Minister. Nothing in Part V or Part VI imports those conditions into the appointment.

On the text, therefore, there is no condition of eligibility for ministerial office beyond appointment under Article 75(1) or Article 164(1) and the taking of the oaths. Everything else that we associate with eligibility for executive office in a parliamentary system is attached by the Constitution to membership, and reaches a Minister only through the requirement that he become a member within six months.

This is a real gap rather than a clever reading, and it has been recognised as an open one. In *S.R. Chaudhuri v. State of Punjab*, (2001) 7 SCC 126, decided on 17 August 2001, the Supreme Court considered a non-legislator who had been appointed a Minister, had not been elected within the six months, had resigned, and had then been appointed again without having been elected. The Court held that Article 164(4) is not a source of power or an enabling provision for the appointment of a non-legislator as a Minister, but is in the nature of a restriction on such a person continuing in office without getting elected; and that re-appointment of the same individual, without election, during the term of the same Legislative Assembly is not permissible. Having so held, the Court expressly declined to decide whether a person who is under a constitutional or statutory disqualification to contest an election may be appointed a Minister at all, recording that the question was not directly involved in the case before it.

The question was left where the text leaves it. Article 75(5) is worded identically to Article 164(4), so the reasoning about what the clause is — a restriction rather than a source of power — applies at the Union by parity of language. But the point the Court set aside is the one the text is silent on: the Constitution regulates how long a non-member may remain a Minister and says nothing about who may be appointed one.

## The one disqualification the text does add

There is a single exception, and its shape is instructive. Article 75(1B), inserted by the Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003, provides that a member of either House of Parliament belonging to a political party who is disqualified for being a member under paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule "shall also be disqualified to be appointed as a Minister under clause (1)" for the period from the date of his disqualification until the date on which his term as a member would have expired, or, if he contests an election to either House before then, until the date on which he is declared elected. Article 164(1B) does the same for a State.

The clause is confined in two ways. It applies only to the Tenth Schedule disqualification for defection, and it applies only to a person who was a member and was disqualified as one. A person who was never a member, and who therefore could never be disqualified under paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule, falls outside it entirely.

The reason for the confinement is apparent from the problem the amendment was addressing. The mischief was the use of ministerial office as the consideration for a defection: a member crosses the floor, is disqualified, and is appointed a Minister by the government he has crossed to. To close that, it was enough to disqualify the defector from appointment for the balance of his term. The amendment did not need to say anything about the qualifications of appointees generally, and it did not.

The result is that the Constitution now contains an express bar on appointing one specific class of person as Minister — a disqualified defector — and no general requirement that an appointee be eligible for membership at all. The only disqualification for ministerial office in the text is the one that was politically urgent in 2003.

## Why the office-of-profit clause had to make an exception

Articles 102(1)(a) and 191(1)(a) disqualify a person from being chosen as, and from being, a member if he holds any office of profit under the Government of India or the Government of a State, other than an office declared not to disqualify its holder — by Parliament in the first case, by the State legislature in the second. The purpose is familiar: a legislature that is to scrutinise the executive should not be populated by the executive's own appointees and salary-earners.

A Minister holds an office under the Government, and it is an office of profit: Article 75(6) and Article 164(5) provide for the salaries and allowances of Ministers, to be determined by the legislature concerned. On the face of the disqualification clause, every Minister who is a member would be disqualified from the membership that the parliamentary system requires him to have.

The Constitution deals with this by express exception. An Explanation to Article 102(1) provides that a person shall not be deemed to hold an office of profit under the Government of India or of any State "by reason only that he is a Minister either for the Union or for such State." The Explanation to Article 191(1) is in the same terms.

The exception is as old as the text. In the Constitution as enacted it was not an Explanation at all but clause (2) of Article 102; the Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985 relabelled it as an Explanation to clause (1) so that clause (2) could carry the new disqualification under the Tenth Schedule. The words themselves were not altered. The framers saw the difficulty at the outset and dealt with it by exempting the ministry by name.

The need for that Explanation is a useful reminder of what the two sets of clauses are doing. The purity rule and the parliamentary executive are in direct tension: one keeps the executive's paid officers out of the legislature, the other requires the executive's principal officers to be in it. The Constitution does not reconcile them. It carves the ministry out of the rule by name, and the carve-out is drafted as a deeming provision rather than as a statement of principle, because there is no principle available that would distinguish a Minister from any other salaried holder of office under the Government. He is distinguished because the system cannot work unless he is.

## The ceiling of 2003, and the floor that outranks it

The Ninety-first Amendment also capped the size of ministries. Article 75(1A) provides that the total number of Ministers, including the Prime Minister, shall not exceed fifteen per cent of the total number of members of the House of the People. Article 164(1A) provides the same for a State, by reference to the total number of members of the Legislative Assembly.

Article 164(1A) then adds a proviso: "Provided that the number of Ministers, including the Chief Minister in a State shall not be less than twelve". A second proviso gave States whose ministries exceeded the new limits six months from a date to be appointed by the President by public notification to come into conformity; the date appointed was 7 January 2004.

The first proviso is drafted as a floor and it is set in absolute numbers, while the limit it qualifies is a percentage. Wherever the percentage of a particular Assembly works out to fewer than twelve, the floor governs, and the limit enacted to cap ministries becomes, for that State, a number larger than the cap.

The arithmetic is not marginal. Article 170(1) provides that the Legislative Assembly of each State shall consist of not more than five hundred and not less than sixty members chosen by direct election. In an Assembly at that constitutional minimum, fifteen per cent is nine, and the floor of twelve is twenty per cent of the House. The Constitution permits smaller Assemblies still: Article 371F(a) provides, notwithstanding anything in the Constitution, that the Legislative Assembly of the State of Sikkim shall consist of not less than thirty members. In an Assembly of thirty, fifteen per cent is four and a half, and twelve is forty per cent.

So the cap binds only in the larger Assemblies. In the smaller ones the operative rule is the floor, and the floor permits between a third again and nearly three times the number of Ministers the stated limit would allow — twelve against nine in an Assembly of sixty, twelve against four and a half in an Assembly of thirty. The percentage and the number were not reconciled. Both were written down, with the number given priority wherever the two meet.

The two provisions were not drafted for the same purpose, which explains without excusing the result. The percentage addresses the inflation of ministries in large Houses, where office can be distributed to hold a majority together. The floor addresses the opposite difficulty — that a very small Assembly cannot staff a working government if it is held to a percentage. Both concerns are real. What the text does not do is state a rule that serves both, and the drafting resolves the conflict in favour of the floor in every case where the two meet.

## The one portfolio the Constitution insists on

The Constitution names a ministerial portfolio exactly once. The proviso to Article 164(1) provides that in the States of Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha "there shall be a Minister in charge of tribal welfare who may in addition be in charge of the welfare of the Scheduled Castes and backward classes or any other work."

Three things about the provision are worth noting.

It is mandatory where it applies: "there shall be a Minister". Everything else about the composition of a ministry — its size below the cap, its distribution of subjects, whether a particular department exists at all — is left to the Chief Minister's advice and the Governor's appointment. This one portfolio is constitutionally required to exist.

It applies to four States and not to the rest. The obligation is not stated as a general one, applicable wherever the relevant population is substantial. It is stated as a list.

And the list has been edited. It originally named Bihar, Madhya Pradesh and Orissa. The words "Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand" were substituted for "Bihar" by the Constitution (Ninety-fourth Amendment) Act, 2006, with effect from 12 June 2006, and "Odisha" was substituted for "Orissa" by the Orissa (Alteration of Name) Act, 2011. When Bihar and Madhya Pradesh were reorganised in 2000, the districts with the largest Scheduled Tribe populations in Bihar went to the new State of Jharkhand, and a substantial part of those in Madhya Pradesh went to Chhattisgarh. Six years later the Constitution was amended to place the obligation on the two new States and to lift it from Bihar, while leaving it on Madhya Pradesh.

A duty written as a list of named States has to be amended whenever the States change. A duty written as a general condition would not. The proviso is a small illustration of the cost of drafting an obligation by enumeration, and of what has to happen afterwards to keep the enumeration true.

## What the arrangement rests on

Collect the provisions and the design is consistent, though not the one usually described.

The Constitution fixes with precision the things that can be fixed by rule: the maximum size of a ministry as a percentage, a minimum in absolute numbers, a specific portfolio in four named States, the oaths, the authority that fixes salaries, the single disqualification for defectors, and the six months after which a non-member ceases to hold office. On the question that matters most — who may be appointed to exercise executive power — it says only that the President appoints the Prime Minister, and appoints the other Ministers on the Prime Minister's advice.

The gap is filled not by the qualification articles, which the text does not extend to Ministers, but by the six-month clause, which converts an unqualified appointment into a mandate requirement enforced by lapse of time. The Supreme Court has read that clause for what it says it is — a restriction on continuance, not an authority to appoint — and has held that it cannot be run twice over for the same person in the same Assembly. What it has not had to decide, and what the text does not answer, is whether a person who could not be elected at all may be appointed for the six months.

The arrangement therefore depends on something outside itself. It assumes that appointments will be made from among people who can realistically be returned to one House or the other within six months, because the appointing authority has no interest in installing a Minister who must cease to hold office at the end of that period. That assumption is about political cost, not about constitutional text. It is doing the work that Articles 84 and 173 do for members, and it is doing it without being written down anywhere.
