A special appeal is the name the Allahabad High Court gives to an intra-court appeal: an appeal from the judgment of one Judge of the Court to a Bench of two Judges of the same Court. The Rules of the Court define it in exactly those terms. Chapter I Rule 3 says that "Special Appeal" means "an appeal from the Judgment of one Judge". Other High Courts call the same remedy a Letters Patent appeal or a writ appeal.

It is unlike every other appeal a practitioner files, in three ways.

The first is its source. It does not come from the Code of Civil Procedure, and it does not come from an ordinary appeal statute. It began in a royal charter, the Letters Patent of 1866. It passed through the United Provinces High Courts (Amalgamation) Order, 1948 and Article 225 of the Constitution, and it now rests on Chapter VIII Rule 5 of the Rules of the Court, 1952, as cut down by the U.P. High Court (Abolition of Letters Patent Appeals) Act, 1962. The Full Bench in *Rajit Ram Yadav v. State of U.P.*, 2024:AHC:109996-FB, put it in one sentence: special appeals are now governed by the 1962 Act as amended and by Rule 5 (para 26).

The second is that it is defined by exclusion. Rule 5 grants an appeal in one line and then removes six classes of judgment from it. Almost all the case law is about what was removed.

The third is its procedure. Because the right was born outside the Code, the Code does not govern it of its own force. The Rules import a few provisions of the Code by name, and the rest is available, if at all, only as principle.

This note follows the right from 1866 to the present, quoting the operative text at each stage, and then sets out what is excluded, what remains, how the Code bears on it, and what has to be on the paper when one is drafted.

## The charter: Clause 10 as granted in 1866

The Indian High Courts Act, 1861 allowed the Crown, by Letters Patent, to establish a High Court for any territory not within the local jurisdiction of another High Court. Under that power the Letters Patent of 17 March 1866 established the High Court of Judicature for the North-Western Provinces. Clause 10 gave it an appeal from its own Judges. As reproduced by the Full Bench in *Rajit Ram Yadav* (para 7), the clause read:

> "10. And We do further ordain that an appeal shall lie to the said High Court of Judicature for the North-Western Provinces from the judgement (not being a sentence or order passed or made in any criminal trial) of one judge of the said High Court or of one judge of any Division Court, pursuant to Section 13 of the said recited Act, and that an appeal shall also lie to the said High Court from the judgment (not being a sentence or order as aforesaid) of two or more Judges of the said High Court, or of such Division Court, wherever such Judges are equally divided in opinion, and do not amount in number to a majority of the whole of the Judges of the said High Court at the time being; but that the right of appeal from other judgments… shall be to Us… in Our or their Privy Council…"

Four features of that text shaped everything that followed.

It was nearly universal. The only exclusion was a sentence or order "in any criminal trial". A Single Judge's judgment in a second appeal, or in a revision, went to the full court like any other.

It was an appeal from the court to itself. It lay "to the said High Court" from one of its own Judges. It was never an appeal under the Code, which regulates appeals from a subordinate court to a superior one.

It also covered an equally divided Bench that did not form a majority of the whole Court. That limb has no counterpart in today's Rule 5.

And it did not define "judgment". That gap produced a century of litigation, which the Supreme Court finally settled in 1981.

The charter was also subject to legislation from the start. The consolidated text of the Letters Patent published by the High Court (Clause 35, as it stood after 1915) makes every provision "subject to the legislative powers of the Governor General in Legislative Council", and allows them to be "in all respects amended and altered thereby". Every later curtailment rests on that root.

### The first collision with the Code

The Code soon ran into Clause 10. The question was whether the Code's list of appealable orders also limited a Letters Patent appeal. The Privy Council answered no: in *Hurrish Chunder Chowdry v. Kali Sundari Debia* (1883) it held that s.588 of the 1877 Code did not restrict appeals from one Judge to the full court, and in *Mt. Sabitri Thakurain v. Savi* (1921) it held that the Code regulates the procedure in a Letters Patent appeal but does not cut down the right. Both decisions are read in that sense by the Supreme Court in *Shah Babulal Khimji v. Jayaben D. Kania*, (1981) 4 SCC 8.

Allahabad took the other view. Its Full Bench in *Muhammad Naim-ul-lah Khan v. Ihsan-ul-lah Khan* (ILR 14 All 226) tied Letters Patent appeals to the orders appealable under the Code. The Constitution Bench in *P.S. Sathappan v. Andhra Bank Ltd.*, (2004) 11 SCC 672 records the sequel: the Allahabad High Court "held that a Letters Patent Appeal was barred under Section 588 C.P.C.", and in view of the conflict the legislature "introduced Section 4 and also introduced Section 104" in the Code of 1908.

That history still matters. Allahabad is the court that historically read the Code into Clause 10, and each time it was corrected from above.

### 1919, 1928 and 1929

The Supplementary Letters Patent of 11 March 1919 renamed the court the High Court of Judicature at Allahabad (*Rajit Ram Yadav*, para 8). The name changed; the jurisdiction did not.

The first real cut came on 28 January 1928. The amended clause, as the Full Bench reproduces it (para 9), no longer gave an appeal from a judgment:

> "not being a judgment passed in the exercise of appellate jurisdiction in respect of a decree or order made in the exercise of appellate jurisdiction by a Court subject to the superintendence… and not being an order made in the exercise of revisional jurisdiction, and not being a sentence or order passed or made in the exercise of the power of superintendence under… Section 107 of the Government of India Act, or in the exercise of criminal jurisdiction"

The words matter. What was excluded was a judgment in appellate jurisdiction on a decree or order *itself made in appellate jurisdiction* — that is, a second appeal. Even a second-appeal judgment remained appealable where the Judge declared the case fit for appeal. A Single Judge's judgment in a first appeal from a subordinate court stayed appealable as before, and remained so until 1962.

| Kind of Single Judge decision | Appealable after 1928? |
| --- | --- |
| First appeal from a subordinate court's original decree or order | Yes |
| Second appeal | Only if the Judge declared the case fit for appeal |
| Revision | No |
| Superintendence under s.107, Government of India Act 1915 | No |
| Any order in the exercise of criminal jurisdiction | No |

The criminal exclusion also widened. It moved from a sentence or order "in any criminal trial" to any order "in the exercise of criminal jurisdiction". The new words are not confined to a trial, which is why the modern debate asks whether a writ petition can be "criminal jurisdiction" at all.

The amendment of 26 January 1929 applied the certificate route to judgments made "on or after the first day of February One thousand nine hundred and twenty-nine" (paras 10-11).

One caution about the texts. The consolidated Clause 10 on the High Court's website reads "not being a judgment passed in the exercise of appellate jurisdiction by a Court subject to the superintendence", without the words "in respect of a decree or order made in the exercise of appellate jurisdiction". Read literally, that version would exclude every appellate judgment, which the clause never did. The Full Bench's text, and the Gazette, are the ones to quote.

## Amalgamation, the Constitution and the Rules of 1952

The Governor-General made the United Provinces High Courts (Amalgamation) Order, 1948 under s.229 of the Government of India Act, 1935. Its appointed day was 26 July 1948. Article 3 amalgamated the High Court in Allahabad and the Chief Court in Oudh into "the High Court of Judicature at Allahabad". Four Articles carried the appeal forward:

- **Article 7(1)** gave the new court "all such original, appellate and other jurisdiction as, under the law in force immediately before the appointed day, is exercisable" by either existing court. The Clause 10 appeal was part of that jurisdiction.
- **Article 9** continued the existing law on practice and procedure "with the necessary modifications", and gave the new court "all such powers to make rules and orders with respect to practice and procedure" as the Allahabad court had.
- **Article 17(a)** provided that the Letters Patent of 17 March 1866 "shall cease to have effect except for the purpose of construing, or giving effect to, the provisions of this order".
- **Article 18** provided that the Order "shall have effect subject to any provisions that may be made on or after the appointed day with respect to the new High Court by any Legislature or authority having power to make such provision".

After 26 July 1948, then, Clause 10 no longer operated of its own force. The right survived as jurisdiction carried forward by the Order, and Clause 10 remained the key to construing it (*Rajit Ram Yadav*, paras 12-13). Article 18 left the State legislature free to cut it down, and in 1962 it did.

Article 225 of the Constitution then preserved the jurisdiction of every existing High Court and its power to make rules of court, subject to the Constitution and to laws of the appropriate legislature (*Rajit Ram Yadav*, para 14). Article 226 created a jurisdiction no charter had imagined, and the question at once arose whether a Single Judge's decision under it was appealable. In *Aidal Singh v. Karan Singh*, AIR 1957 All 414, a Full Bench answered yes, by a majority of two to one. Kidwai and Beg JJ. held that the writ power and the power of superintendence are "two separate and distinct powers" (paras 12, 63), so a writ judgment was not excluded as superintendence. Agarwala J., dissenting, treated Article 226 against courts and tribunals as superintendence in substance (para 38). Orders under Article 227 were, and remain, excluded.

The Rules of the Court, 1952 put the appeal into Chapter VIII Rule 5. The original Rule re-enacted the 1928-29 Clause 10, certificate route included (*Rajit Ram Yadav*, para 16). Two features of that design carry forward. Because the Rule borrowed Clause 10's words, it bears the meaning Clause 10 had acquired, which is why "judgment" in Rule 5 is read as the Supreme Court read it under the Letters Patent. And because the Rule is made under Article 225 and the 1948 Order, both expressly subject to legislation, it cannot create an appeal that a statute takes away. A Letters Patent appeal survives unless the legislature excludes it — the proposition the Supreme Court stated in *South Asia Industries v. S.B. Sarup Singh*, [1965] 2 SCR 756, as later read in *Umaji Keshao Meshram v. Radhikabai*, 1986 Supp SCC 401, and in *Rajit Ram Yadav* (para 27).

## The legislative cut-back, 1962 to 1983

### Section 3 of the 1962 Act

U.P. Act 14 of 1962 came into force on 13 November 1962. Section 3, as the Full Bench reproduces it (para 18), provides:

> "3. (1) No appeal, arising from a suit or proceeding instituted or commenced, whether prior or subsequent to the enforcement of this Act, shall be to the High Court from a judgment or order of one Judge of the High Court, made in the exercise of appellate jurisdiction, in respect of a decree or order made by a Court, subject to the Superintendence of the High Court, anything to the contrary contained in Clause 10… read with Clause 17… notwithstanding."

Sub-section (2) saved appeals already pending.

The bar now reached every appellate judgment of a Single Judge on a subordinate court's decree or order — first appeals, second appeals and appeals from orders alike. The *non obstante* clause names Clause 10 and the Amalgamation Order expressly, which is the express exclusion *South Asia Industries* requires.

The Rules followed on 6 November 1963 (Notification 345/VIII-C-150, U.P. Gazette 5.12.1964). Rule 5 was substituted, and Rule 6, which had governed the declaration of fitness, was deleted (*Rajit Ram Yadav*, para 19).

### Sections 4 and 5

The first bar on appeals from writ judgments came on 18 August 1972, when U.P. Act 33 of 1972 inserted s.4. It covered Article 226 and 227 judgments on orders of the Board of Revenue or the Director of Consolidation under the land revenue, tenancy, zamindari abolition and consolidation statutes it lists (*Rajit Ram Yadav*, paras 20-21). An amendment of 1975 inserted a first s.5, barring appeals from writ judgments on appellate or revisional orders of District Judges, Additional District Judges, Civil Judges and Additional Civil Judges under U.P. Acts (para 22).

On 12 May 1981, U.P. Act 12 of 1981 substituted the present s.5. It bars appeals from Article 226 and 227 judgments on any order of a tribunal, court or statutory arbitrator, and on appellate or revisional orders of the Government or of any officer or authority, made under a U.P. Act or under a Central Act on a State List or Concurrent List subject (paras 23-24). Its Statement of Objects and Reasons refers to the earlier amendments and to mounting arrears (para 23), and respondents rely on that purpose to argue for a wide reading.

Rule 5 was amended to its present text on 27 July 1983 (Notification 436/VIII-C-2, Gazette 13.8.1983) to match (para 25).

### Validity

The bar has been upheld three times. A Full Bench upheld the 1962 Act and the 1972 amendment in *Hakim Singh v. Shiv Sagar*, AIR 1973 All 596. A Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court held neither the 1962 Act nor Act 33 of 1972 unconstitutional in *Hasinuddin Khan v. Deputy Director of Consolidation*, (1980) 3 SCC 285. And in *Firdosh Fatima v. Firdosh Begum*, (1996) 2 SCC 563, the Supreme Court upheld s.4 as within the State List entries on the jurisdiction of courts.

The 1981 s.5 has not been struck down. In *Vajara Yojna Seed Farm v. Presiding Officer, Labour Court-II*, (2003) 1 UPLBEC 496, a Division Bench declined to entertain an unpleaded challenge to it and held that the ratio of *Firdosh Fatima* and *Hasinuddin Khan* was "fully attracted" (para 56).

The logic is simple. No Article of the Constitution guarantees an intra-court appeal. It is a charter or statutory right, and what a competent legislature gave it may take away, provided it does so in clear terms. Article 226 itself is untouched: a litigant shut out of a special appeal still has Article 136.

## Rule 5 as it now reads

The present text, as reproduced by a Division Bench in *Brij Mohan Lal Saxena v. State of U.P.* (26.9.2024, para 20):

> "5. Special Appeal: - An appeal shall lie to the Court from a judgment (not being a judgment passed in the exercise of appellate jurisdiction) in respect of a decree or order made by a Court subject to the superintendence of the Court and not being an order made in the exercise of revisional jurisdiction or in the exercise of its power of superintendence or in the exercise of criminal jurisdiction or in the exercise of the jurisdiction conferred by Article 226 or Article 227 of the Constitution in respect of any judgment, order or award - (a) of a tribunal, Court or statutory arbitrator made or purported to be made in the exercise or purported exercise of jurisdiction under any Uttar Pradesh Act or under any Central Act, with respect to any of the matters enumerated in the State List or the Concurrent List in the Seventh Schedule to the Constitution, or (b) of the Government or any officer or authority, made or purported to be made in the exercise or purported exercise of appellate or revisional jurisdiction under any such Act of one Judge."

The Rule is one sentence in three parts: the operative words ("An appeal shall lie to the Court from a judgment"), a parenthesis of exclusions, and the closing words ("of one Judge"). Read without the parenthesis, it grants an appeal from any judgment of one Judge, in any jurisdiction. The parenthesis then carves out the exceptions.

That structure allocates the burden. The appellant must show a "judgment" and that it is "of one Judge". A respondent who invokes a bar must bring the case within an exclusion. An exception to a remedial grant is not read so as to swallow the grant, and the Supreme Court has recently said in terms that Rule 5 is to be read to advance access to justice (*Abhishek Gupta v. Dinesh Kumar*, 2025 INSC 1406, para 15).

A handful of phrases decide most cases.

| Phrase | Meaning settled so far |
| --- | --- |
| "a judgment" | The *Shah Babulal Khimji* sense, not the definition in s.2(9) CPC |
| "appellate jurisdiction in respect of a decree or order made by a Court subject to the superintendence" | A Single Judge hearing a first appeal, second appeal or appeal from order from a subordinate court |
| "revisional jurisdiction" | Any revision, whatever the statute |
| "power of superintendence" | Article 227, and any other superintendence |
| "criminal jurisdiction" | Read by the nature of the proceeding, not its label (*Ram Kishan Fauji*) |
| "made or purported to be made in the exercise or purported exercise" | Catches orders attacked as void; voidness goes to merits, not maintainability (*Vajara Yojna*, para 50) |
| "under any Uttar Pradesh Act or under any Central Act" | Includes rules, regulations and statutory orders made under the Act (*Sheet Gupta*; *Vajara Yojna*, para 56) |
| "with respect to any of the matters enumerated in the State List or the Concurrent List" | Looks to the legislative source of the parent Act, not the subject of the dispute (*Rajit Ram Yadav*) |
| "appellate or revisional jurisdiction" in limb (b) | Only appellate and revisional orders; original orders of officers and authorities are outside (*Vajara Yojna*, para 53) |

## The threshold: what is a "judgment"

If the order is not a judgment, the exclusions are never reached.

The governing authority is *Shah Babulal Khimji v. Jayaben D. Kania*, (1981) 4 SCC 8, decided by three Judges on 10 August 1981. The Court rejected both the narrow Calcutta test and the wide Madras test, and identified three kinds of judgment: a final judgment, which decides the suit; a preliminary judgment, which decides an objection such as jurisdiction or limitation; and an intermediary or interlocutory judgment, which includes orders under Order XLIII Rule 1 and other orders that have the characteristics and trappings of finality and directly affect a valuable right. A routine procedural order does not qualify. The Court also held that there is no inconsistency between s.104 read with Order XLIII Rule 1 and the Letters Patent appeal: the Code adds a forum of appeal for the orders it lists, without cutting down the appeal the Letters Patent give.

Earlier, in *Asrumati Debi v. Kumar Rupendra Deb Raikot*, [1953] SCR 1159, the Court had held that an order transferring a suit is not a judgment, as it "neither affects the merits of the controversy between the parties in the suit itself nor terminates or disposes of the suit on any ground" (as summarised in *Shanti Kumar R. Canji v. Home Insurance Co.*, 24.7.1974).

The Allahabad test today is the Full Bench's in *Ashutosh Shrotriya v. Vice-Chancellor, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar University*, AIR 2015 All 187 (4.9.2015). An order calling for counter and rejoinder affidavits is "merely a procedural order in aid of the progression of the case", has no trappings of finality, and is not appealable. But whether a particular order decides matters of moment, or affects the "vital and valuable rights of the parties", is for the Division Bench to decide on the facts. And a party who needs *ad interim* protection must press for it before the Single Judge, who should deal with it, however briefly.

Transfer orders under s.24 CPC have their own Allahabad line. *Amit Khanna v. Smt. Suchi Khanna*, 2009 (1) AWC 929, held that no special appeal lies against a Single Judge's order on a transfer application: s.104 does not make it appealable and s.105 impliedly excludes an appeal. In *Mahendra Pratap Bhatt v. Smt. Saroj Mahana* (Special Appeal 153 of 2016, decided 25.4.2016) a Division Bench nonetheless entertained an appeal where the transfer had been made without territorial jurisdiction, on the concession of counsel, holding that "a jurisdiction cannot be assumed on the concession of the counsel for the parties". In *Vinay Mohan v. Smt. Nidhi Singh* (Special Appeal Defective 387 of 2025) a Division Bench of Rajan Roy and Prashant Kumar JJ. again held a s.24 order not to be a judgment.

The sound basis for that line is *Asrumati Debi*'s: a transfer order is not a judgment. The supporting reasoning from ss.104 and 105 is weaker, because *Shah Babulal* holds that s.104 adds to the Letters Patent appeal rather than cutting it down, and *Sathappan* holds that s.104(2) does not bar it. A respondent relying on this line is safer on the "not a judgment" ground.

| Single Judge's order (in an otherwise appealable writ) | Judgment? |
| --- | --- |
| Final order allowing or dismissing the writ | Yes |
| Dismissal *in limine* on maintainability, delay or alternative remedy | Yes: a preliminary judgment |
| Refusal of interim relief where the subject matter would be lost | Arguably yes, on the facts (*Ashutosh Shrotriya*) |
| Order calling for counter and rejoinder affidavits | No (*Ashutosh Shrotriya*) |
| Adjournment, or a direction to list with a connected case | No |
| Transfer of a case under s.24 CPC | No (*Asrumati Debi*; *Vinay Mohan*) |

One point is often missed. If the writ itself falls in an excluded class, its interim orders are barred whatever their finality, because the exclusions turn on the jurisdiction exercised, not on the type of order.

## The six exclusions

The Full Bench in *Sheet Gupta v. State of U.P.*, AIR 2010 All 46 (11.12.2009) set out the six excluded classes, and in *Abhishek Gupta* (2025, para 14) the Supreme Court said that *Sheet Gupta* reads Rule 5 correctly. The examples given under each class below are illustrations, not reported cases.

### 1. Appellate jurisdiction over a subordinate court

Since s.3 of the 1962 Act, no special appeal lies from a Single Judge's judgment in a first appeal, second appeal or appeal from order arising from a court subject to the High Court's superintendence. Section 100A of the Code is an independent bar on the same ground, and wider (below).

*Illustrations.* A Single Judge dismisses a first appeal under s.96 CPC from a Civil Judge's decree, or allows an appeal from order against the refusal of a temporary injunction. No special appeal lies in either case; the remedy is Article 136.

### 2. Revisional jurisdiction

Any revision decided by a Single Judge is excluded, whatever the statute (*Sheet Gupta*).

*Illustrations.* A civil revision under s.115 CPC; a revision under s.25 of the Provincial Small Cause Courts Act. A criminal revision is barred twice over, as revisional and as criminal.

### 3. Superintendence

Article 227 orders are excluded as superintendence, and have been since *Aidal Singh*. The 226/227 distinction that case drew survives, although a Lucknow Division Bench has held that *Aidal Singh* no longer binds for appeals barred by the 1981-83 amendments (*Abhishek Sahu v. Civil Judge*, Special Appeal 713 of 2014, 28.11.2014).

*Illustration.* A petition labelled "under Article 226/227" against a Civil Judge's order refusing amendment of a plaint is, in substance, superintendence over a civil court. No special appeal lies.

### 4. Criminal jurisdiction

In *Ram Kishan Fauji v. State of Haryana*, (2017) 5 SCC 533, three Judges held that the "litmus test" is "the nature of the proceeding" (para 65), and that "the nomenclature of a writ petition is not the governing factor" (para 64). A writ to avoid a criminal investigation was held to be criminal jurisdiction (para 65).

On 14 October 2026, in *Sanjay Prasad v. Madhusudan Dwivedi*, 2026 INSC 1128, a two-Judge Bench arising from a Lucknow Bench contempt doubted *Ram Kishan Fauji* for conflating writ jurisdiction with ordinary criminal jurisdiction (paras 49-51) and referred its correctness to a larger Bench (para 52). Until the larger Bench rules, *Ram Kishan Fauji* binds.

*Illustrations.* A writ to quash an FIR or to stay arrest is criminal jurisdiction. So is habeas corpus against detention by the police or preventive detention. A writ seeking a fair or transferred investigation is criminal by nature under *Ram Kishan Fauji*. Habeas corpus for the custody of a child between parents is, by the same test, civil.

### 5. A writ against any order of a tribunal, court or statutory arbitrator

This limb catches any order of such a body, original or appellate, "made or purported to be made" under a U.P. Act or a Central Act on a State or Concurrent List subject. In *Vajara Yojna* (21.11.2002) the Division Bench held a Labour Court under the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 to be a tribunal and the Act a Concurrent List law, so that no appeal lay (paras 40, 51-52); an argument that the award was void went to merits, not maintainability (para 50); and an Election Tribunal under the U.P. Panchayat Raj Act had the trappings of a court (paras 59-61).

At Allahabad this limb bars an Article 226 judgment as well as an Article 227 one. The argument, common in other High Courts, that a petition against a tribunal was correctly brought under Article 226 and is therefore appealable, helps an appellant here only where this limb does not reach: where the parent law is a Union List Act, or the body is not a tribunal, court or arbitrator.

*Illustrations.* A writ against a Labour Court award. A writ against an order of a Revenue Court under the U.P. Revenue Code, 2006. A writ against an Election Tribunal under the Panchayat Raj Act. All barred.

### 6. A writ against an appellate or revisional order of the Government, an officer or an authority

In *Sheet Gupta* the Full Bench held that a Commissioner's appellate order under the U.P. Scheduled Commodities Distribution Order, 2004 was made "under" the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, a Concurrent List law, through the Public Distribution System (Control) Order, 2001, and that no appeal lay. It also held that *Ram Dhyan Singh* (DB, 2004), which had allowed an appeal because the appellate power came from a Government Order, did not lay down the correct law. In *State of U.P. v. Madhav Prasad Sharma*, (2011) 2 SCC 212, the Supreme Court held that where the writ was against a DIG's appellate order under the U.P. Police (Punishment and Appeal) Rules, 1991, "no further appeal by way of special appeal before the Division Bench would lie" (para 8).

Two points follow. The limb catches only appellate and revisional orders: an original order of an officer or authority is outside it unless the body is a tribunal within limb 5 (*Vajara Yojna*, para 53). And where the writ attacks both the original and the appellate order, the bar applies — that was *Sheet Gupta*'s own case.

*Illustrations.* A writ against a Commissioner's appellate order upholding the cancellation of a fair-price-shop agreement: barred. A writ against an appellate order in police disciplinary proceedings: barred. A writ against a District Magistrate's original order cancelling a licence under a U.P. Act, with no appeal taken: not barred by this limb.

### The bars outside Rule 5

Rule 5 is not the only gatekeeper. A special appeal also fails where another statute excludes it: s.100A of the Code; s.19 of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971, for orders punishing contempt; and a special Act that is a self-contained code, such as the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. In each case the general grant in Rule 5 yields to the special exclusion — *generalia specialibus non derogant*.

## Where the appeal still lies

Rule 5 is a grant, so everything a Single Judge decides as a judgment is appealable unless an exclusion bites. In practice the appeal is alive in the following areas.

**Writs against original orders of the State, its officers and authorities.** Limb 6 catches only appellate and revisional orders, and limb 5 only tribunals, courts and arbitrators. A writ against an original order of dismissal from service, with no departmental appeal taken, or against a tender decision, a recruitment policy or an advertisement condition, or for mandamus on an unanswered representation, is appealable.

**Orders under Union List legislation.** In *Rajit Ram Yadav* (8.7.2024) the Full Bench of Arun Bhansali CJ, Manoj Kumar Gupta and Vikas Budhwar JJ. held that service regulations made under the Road Transport Corporations Act, 1950, a Union List law, are not "with respect to" a State or Concurrent List matter. A special appeal against a writ judgment on an appellate order under those regulations is therefore maintainable (para 67). The Division Bench decision in *Abhai Raj Singh*, which had held otherwise, was overruled to that extent (para 62). The same reasoning should open the appeal for writs on orders made under other Union List statutes.

**Appellate orders under instruments that are not "any such Act".** The Full Bench in *Rajit Ram Yadav* (para 63) noted a Division Bench view that appellate orders under rules made under the proviso to Article 309, rather than under an Act, fall outside the bar. *Sheet Gupta*, however, treats delegated legislation under an Act as "under" the Act. The appellate power has to be traced to its source in every case.

**Original-side judgments of a Single Judge.** In *Shri Colonizers and Developers Pvt. Ltd. v. Abha Gupta* (Special Appeal 394 of 2025, decided 16.12.2025) a Lucknow Division Bench entertained a special appeal against an order of a Single Judge sitting as Commercial Division in the execution of an arbitral award. The Single Judge had exercised original jurisdiction, not appellate, revisional, supervisory or Article 226/227 jurisdiction, so the exclusions did not apply (paras 5-6), and counsel were agreed that neither the 1996 Act nor the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 provided another remedy (para 4). The appeal failed on merits. Where a special Act carries its own exhaustive appeal scheme, that scheme should be checked first.

**PIL judgments, and interim orders with real finality** in otherwise appealable writs.

**Two gateways into barred classes**: the merits part of a contempt order, and an appeal with leave by an affected non-party.

| Writ against… | Special appeal? |
| --- | --- |
| Original order of the State, an officer or an authority (not a tribunal) | Yes |
| Any order under a Union List Act | Yes (*Rajit Ram Yadav*) |
| Appellate order under Article 309 rules alone | Arguable |
| Single Judge's original-side order, where no special Act excludes appeal | Yes (*Shri Colonizers*) |
| Interim order with trappings of finality, in an appealable writ | Yes |
| Contempt order deciding the merits | Yes, as to that part |
| A judgment that prejudices a non-party | Yes, with leave |

## Contempt and non-parties

### Contempt

Two remedies run on two routes. Section 19 of the Contempt of Courts Act gives an appeal only against an order punishing for contempt. Rule 5 gives an intra-court appeal against the part of a contempt order that decides the merits of the underlying dispute.

Applying para 11 of *Midnapore Peoples' Co-op. Bank v. Chunilal Nanda*, (2006) 5 SCC 399, the Supreme Court held in *Deepak Kumar v. Devina Tewari*, 2024 INSC 925 (26.11.2024) that an Allahabad special appeal against the dismissal of a contempt application was not maintainable, and that the remedy was a petition for special leave, with the benefit of s.14 of the Limitation Act (paras 6-9). In *Sanjay Prasad* the Court then held that civil contempt is not "criminal jurisdiction" under Rule 5, and that Rule 5 does not bar every order passed before punishment (paras 36-38). It listed four kinds of contempt order open to a special appeal (para 33): directions to a person not bound by the parent order; orders requiring fresh compliance after rejecting a compliance report; orders that travel beyond the parent order; and orders "deciding disputed questions of fact/law having the effect of finally determining rights of parties". The earlier three-Judge decision in *Ajay Kumar Bhalla v. Prakash Kumar Dixit*, (2024) 12 SCC 159, on a contempt order that also decided entitlement to promotion, is explained there to the same effect (paras 30-32).

*Illustrations.* A contempt Bench directs the grant of seniority from a date the parent order never decided: the appeal lies. A contempt Bench closes the proceedings recording compliance: no appeal lies under Rule 5 or s.19, and the remedy is Article 136. A contempt Bench punishes the officer: the appeal is under s.19, not Rule 5.

### Non-parties

In *Abhishek Gupta v. Dinesh Kumar*, 2025 INSC 1406 (3.12.2025), a fair-price-shop allottee who had not been impleaded in the writ was displaced by it, and the Division Bench dismissed his special appeal as barred. The Supreme Court held that *Sheet Gupta* reads Rule 5 correctly but did not consider an affected non-party (para 14). Rule 5 must be read to advance access to justice and yields to natural justice (paras 15-16). A non-party shown to be prejudiced, adversely affected or bound by the judgment may appeal with leave (para 17), and a writ order passed without an affected party is "liable to be invalidated on that ground alone" (para 16). Where non-joinder is made out, the Division Bench should remand or decide the matter on merits (para 19).

A Division Bench has since gone further. In *Sanjay Agrawal v. State of U.P.* (Special Appeal 400 of 2026, decided 1.7.2026), the writ had challenged a Commissioner's appellate order under the Societies Registration Act, 1860, and the appeal was barred on a plain reading (para 9). The Bench held it maintainable nonetheless, because the Single Judge's order was unreasoned and *Abhishek Gupta* holds that Rule 5 yields to natural justice (paras 10-14).

*Abhishek Gupta* concerned a non-party who was never heard. *Sanjay Agrawal* applies the same idea to a party who was heard but received an unreasoned order. That is a wider proposition, and a Division Bench cannot dilute a Full Bench that the Supreme Court has itself approved. Until a larger Bench speaks, the gateway is available but exposed.

A non-party should file an application for leave with the memorandum, pleading *locus standi* in the terms of para 17: prejudice, adverse effect, or being bound.

## The special appeal and the Code of Civil Procedure

Two questions are often run together. Do the Code's statutory appeals create, replace or bar the special appeal? And does the Code's procedure apply to a special appeal *stricto sensu*?

### Two different kinds of appeal

An appeal under ss.96, 100 or 104 of the Code runs from a subordinate court's decree or order to a superior court. A special appeal runs from one Judge of the High Court to the same Court; it was born in a charter and lives in the Rules. The Code regulates the first and never created the second.

Section 4 of the Code provides that, in the absence of a specific provision to the contrary, nothing in it limits or affects any special or local law, special jurisdiction or power, or special form of procedure. Section 104(1) gives appeals from the listed orders and saves appeals provided "by any law for the time being in force". In *P.S. Sathappan* (7.10.2004) the majority of the Constitution Bench (Variava J., for himself and B.P. Singh and H.K. Sema JJ.; Santosh Hegde and S.B. Sinha JJ. dissenting) held that the legislature "simultaneously saves, in Section 104(1), appeals under 'any law for the time being in force'", and that "These would include Letters Patent Appeals". Sub-section (2) of s.104 "does not provide for any express exclusion". A Letters Patent appeal cannot be ousted by implication; it can be taken away by an express provision, and when the legislature wanted to exclude one, it did so specifically.

### Section 100A: the express exclusion

Section 100A, as substituted in 2002, provides:

> "100A. No further appeal in certain cases.—Notwithstanding anything contained in any Letters Patent for any High Court or in any other instrument having the force of law or in any other law for the time being in force, where any appeal from an original or appellate decree or order is heard and decided by a Single Judge of a High Court, no further appeal shall lie from the judgment and decree of such Single Judge."

As *Sathappan* put it, "now by virtue of Section 100A no Letters Patent Appeal would be maintainable" in such a case. At Allahabad the section overlaps the first exclusion in Rule 5, but it reaches further in two ways. It catches a Single Judge's decision in an appeal from an *original* order of any forum, not only from a court subject to superintendence. And its override is central legislation, which a Rule of Court cannot dilute.

### Special Acts with their own appeal scheme

In *Fuerst Day Lawson Ltd. v. Jindal Exports Ltd.*, (2011) 8 SCC 333, the Supreme Court held that no Letters Patent appeal lies against an order that is not appealable under s.50 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996: where a special Act sets out a self-contained code, the applicability of the general law procedure is impliedly excluded. *Sathappan*'s rule against ouster by implication and *Fuerst Day Lawson*'s implied exclusion by a self-contained code are reconciled by treating the arbitration statutes as self-contained codes. At Allahabad, a special appeal against an arbitration order that the Act does not make appealable is therefore unlikely to be maintainable.

### Does the Code apply *stricto sensu*?

It does not apply of its own force to proceedings under Article 226. The Explanation to s.141, inserted in 1976, provides that the expression "proceedings" in that section "includes proceedings under Order IX, but does not include any proceeding under article 226 of the Constitution". In *Puran Singh v. State of Punjab*, (1996) 2 SCC 205 (18.1.1996), the Supreme Court, drawing on its earlier decision in *Babubhai Muljibhai Patel v. Nandlal Khodidas Barot*, held that "it cannot be held that the provisions contained in Order 22 of the Code are applicable per se to writ proceedings" (para 7). It added that even so, a petitioner cannot ignore the death of a respondent where the right to pursue the remedy survives.

The Lucknow Bench has said the same thing more than once. In *Smt. Kamala Devi v. State of U.P.* (Writ-C 7195 of 2022, decided 23.2.2024, Rajan Roy and Manish Mathur JJ.), the Court observed that the provisions of the Code "do not apply to writ proceedings stricto sensu" in view of the Explanation to s.141, although their principles can be, and very often are, applied (para 23). In *State of U.P. v. Deepak Singh* (23.8.2024, Rajan Roy and Jaspreet Singh JJ.), an appeal arising from an order on a review application, it said that "the provision of CPC does not apply stricto sensuo to the proceedings in a writ petition", but that "the principles contained therein can always be applied" (para 15).

A special appeal from a writ judgment is heard by a Bench of the same Court in the same Article 226 proceeding, and its source is the Rules, not the Code. On that reasoning the Code does not govern it of its own force either.

### What the Rules themselves import

This is the point most often missed. The Rules of the Court import specific provisions of the Code, by name. Rule 38A, which stands at the end of Chapter VIII, reads:

> "38A. Adjustment of appeals, petitions and references etc.—The provisions contained in Rules 1 to 6 and 9 of Order XXII and in Order XXXII of the Code of Civil Procedure shall, so far as may be and with necessary modifications and adaptations, apply to special appeals, writ petitions under Article 226 of the Constitution and Tax Acts references and revisions falling under Chapter IX, XXII and XXVII of these rules."

Chapter X Rule 7 adds that Rules 1 to 6 of that Chapter "shall, with necessary modifications and adaptations, also apply to special Appeals and applications for review and revision".

Two consequences follow. The first is *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*: by selecting Order XXII Rules 1 to 6 and 9, and Order XXXII, for special appeals, the rule-maker indicated that the rest of the Code, Order XLI included, is not applied of its own force. If the whole Code already applied, Rule 38A would be redundant, and a rule is not to be read as surplusage. The second is that, where the Rules do import a provision, it binds, "so far as may be and with necessary modifications". At Allahabad, substitution on the death of a party and the rules for minors therefore apply in a special appeal by force of the Rules, whatever the position in a writ court elsewhere.

| Question in a special appeal | Governing source | Position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Death of a party; substitution | Rule 38A (Order XXII Rules 1-6, 9) | Applies, with adaptations |
| Minor or person of unsound mind as a party | Rule 38A (Order XXXII) | Applies, with adaptations |
| Limitation and condonation of delay | Limitation Act, Article 117 and s.5; Chapter IX Rule 10 | Applies by its own force: the Limitation Act is not the Code |
| Additional evidence (Order XLI Rule 27) | No importing rule | Principle only; a matter of the Court's discretion |
| Stay of the judgment under appeal | Chapter IX Rule 10; inherent power | Principle only |
| Cross-objection (Order XLI Rule 22) | No importing rule | Not available as of right; a party aggrieved should file its own appeal |
| Review of the Division Bench's judgment | Order XLVII principles | Principles applied (*Deepak Singh*, para 15) |
| Underlying proceeding was itself under the Code (e.g. execution of an award under s.36 of the 1996 Act read with Order XXI) | The Code governs that proceeding (*Shri Colonizers*, paras 9-10) | The *stricto sensu* principle concerns writ-derived appeals and should not be over-extended |

The doctrine cuts both ways. A respondent may say that, since the Code does not apply, an appellant cannot claim Order XLI Rule 27 or Rule 22 as of right; that is correct as far as it goes, and the answer is that the Court applies the principles *ex debito justitiae*. An appellant may say that, since the Code does not apply, abatement cannot defeat the appeal; Rule 38A is the answer to that.

## Limitation and the Rules of procedure

Article 117 of the Limitation Act, 1963 provides the period for an appeal "From a decree or order of any High Court to the same Court": thirty days from the date of the decree or order.

In *State of U.P. v. Vijay Prakash Bajpai* (15.12.2006) a Division Bench held that a special appeal may be filed within thirty days under Chapter IX Rule 10, excluding the time taken to obtain the copy (para 9), and that appeals filed with an application for condonation of delay are listed as "Special Appeal Defective" (para 1). In *State of U.P. v. Ram Nayan Yadav* (Special Appeal Defective 345 of 2025, decided 14.10.2025) a Division Bench read Chapter IX Rule 10 with Article 117 (para 3) and held that s.5 applies, but that governmental litigants, no less than private parties, must show sufficient cause, and that a strong case on merits is no ground for condonation (paras 7-9). Delays of 533 and 348 days were not condoned.

The Rules of the Court contain the procedure:

| Rule | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Chapter I Rule 3 | "Special Appeal" means an appeal from the Judgment of one Judge |
| Chapter II Rule 1(iv-a)(b) | Special appeals are laid before the Court within one week of presentation |
| Chapter V Rule 8 | Two Judges hear cases, including writ petitions in which special appeals are not barred |
| Chapter VIII Rule 5 | The appeal |
| Chapter VIII Rule 38A | Order XXII Rules 1-6 and 9 and Order XXXII apply to special appeals |
| Chapter IX Rule 7(2) | "In special appeals, the appellant must furnish two sets of the entire pleadings, which was there before the learned Single Judge" |
| Chapter IX Rule 10 | Presentation, limitation, service on counsel, listing and interim orders in special appeals |
| Chapter X Rule 7 | Chapter X Rules 1-6 apply to special appeals |
| Chapter XIV Rule 13 | Contents of the paper-book in a special appeal from a judgment in an original trial or proceeding |

Chapter IX Rule 10 is the working rule, and it repays a careful reading before every filing. Among other things, it provides for an affidavit explaining any delay; an affidavit of service of the memorandum on the counsel who appeared before the Single Judge, with registered post to respondents who were unrepresented; service of an interim application on opposing counsel before interim relief is sought; and listing after six weeks unless the Bench directs otherwise, with defective appeals listed for orders within a week.

## Drafting the appeal

**Triage before drafting.** Read the writ petition, its prayer and the impugned judgment together; the jurisdiction exercised is read from the whole petition, not its label (*Ram Kishan Fauji*, para 64). Then ask the three questions in order: is it a judgment; what jurisdiction did the Single Judge exercise; and, if it was a writ, what order was under challenge, under which Act, on which List. Identify the parent Act and its Seventh Schedule entry and write the entry number down, because that single fact decides most objections. Check the bars outside Rule 5: s.100A, s.19 of the Contempt Act, and any self-contained special Act. Diarise the thirtieth day the same day the judgment is pronounced.

**The title.** Describe the appeal as a "Special Appeal" under Chapter VIII Rule 5, identify the judgment by date and writ number, and keep the array of parties as it stood in the writ. A non-party appellant should describe himself as not a party to the writ petition and file the application for leave with the memorandum.

**The maintainability paragraph.** Put it first, before the facts. The registry and the Bench will look for it. A form that can be adapted:

> "That the present special appeal is maintainable under Chapter VIII Rule 5 of the Rules of the Court, 1952. The judgment dated ___ is a final judgment of the learned Single Judge in exercise of jurisdiction under Article 226 of the Constitution of India. The order under challenge in the writ petition, namely the order dated ___ passed by the ___, is an original order and not one passed in exercise of appellate or revisional jurisdiction; the said authority is not a tribunal, Court or statutory arbitrator; and the proceeding is not criminal in nature. None of the exclusions in Rule 5, or in Section 5 of the U.P. High Court (Abolition of Letters Patent Appeals) Act, 1962, is attracted."

Where the parent law is a Union List Act, say so and cite *Rajit Ram Yadav*. Where the appeal is against a contempt order, identify which of the four categories in para 33 of *Sanjay Prasad* it falls in. Where the appellant was not a party, plead prejudice in the terms of para 17 of *Abhishek Gupta*.

**Facts.** A special appeal is decided on the writ record. Plead what was before the Single Judge, file both sets of the writ pleadings that Chapter IX Rule 7(2) requires, and set out any later development separately, on affidavit.

**Grounds.** Lead with the ground that shows a demonstrable error: a jurisdiction exercised that did not exist, or declined when it did; a finding resting on no evidence or on a misreading of a document on the record, identified by annexure and line; a binding judgment of the Supreme Court or a Full Bench not considered; a failure of natural justice, including non-joinder of an affected party; or a wrong test applied. A ground that merely re-argues the writ, or says that another view was possible, invites dismissal.

**Relief.** Ask for the judgment to be set aside and the writ petition allowed or, in the alternative, remitted to the Single Judge. Ask for a stay of the operation and effect of the judgment, pleading a *prima facie* case, the balance of convenience and irreparable injury briefly, and serve the application on opposing counsel as Chapter IX Rule 10 requires.

**Supporting papers.** As the case requires: an application for condonation of delay, with an affidavit accounting for the delay; an application for leave to appeal by a non-party; an application for substitution under Rule 38A where a party has died; the impugned judgment; the writ pleadings in two sets; and the affidavit of service on counsel below.

**Language.** Plead in plain English. Latin earns its place only where it is the term of art — *in limine*, *ex parte*, *locus standi*, *res judicata*. The Bench reads for the jurisdictional fact, not the maxim.

## How far the Division Bench interferes

A special appeal is an appeal on fact and law, but it is an appeal within the same Court from a Judge of co-ordinate standing, and the Division Bench is slow to substitute its own view for a reasoned decision of the writ court merely because another view is possible. Its options follow the defect found.

| Finding on appeal | Usual course |
| --- | --- |
| Appeal barred under Rule 5 or a statute | Dismissed as not maintainable, without comment on merits |
| Order not a "judgment" | Dismissed; the point can be pressed before the Single Judge |
| Writ dismissed *in limine* on a wrong ground (delay, alternative remedy, maintainability) | Set aside and remitted for decision on merits |
| Necessary party not joined | Remitted, or decided on merits (*Abhishek Gupta*, para 19) |
| Findings shown to be erroneous on the record | Reversed on merits |

*Madhav Prasad Sharma* illustrates the first line. Having held the special appeal not maintainable, the Supreme Court went on to the merits under Article 136, because it had issued notice and heard both sides (paras 8-12). A Division Bench that finds the appeal barred cannot do the same: its jurisdiction ends with the finding that none exists.

## Questions still open

**The *Ram Kishan Fauji* reference.** If the larger Bench holds that Article 226 is a single original jurisdiction, writs with a criminal-law origin would leave the "criminal jurisdiction" exclusion. Many would still be caught by limbs 5 and 6, because the orders challenged are usually made under State or Concurrent List laws.

**The width of the *Abhishek Gupta* gateway.** *Sanjay Agrawal* has extended it to a party who was heard but received an unreasoned order. A reference is likely if Division Benches diverge.

**Transfer orders.** The "not a judgment" basis of the s.24 line is secure; the reasoning from ss.104 and 105 sits uneasily with *Shah Babulal* and *Sathappan*.

**Special Acts on the original side.** *Shri Colonizers* entertained an appeal from a Commercial Division execution order on the parties' agreement that no other remedy existed. Whether the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 or the self-contained-code principle of *Fuerst Day Lawson* closes that door has not yet been argued.

**Article 309 instruments.** Whether appellate orders under rules made under the proviso to Article 309 fall outside "any such Act" rests on Division Bench authority, not a Full Bench holding.

## Conclusion

The special appeal began in 1866 as a near-universal right to take a Single Judge's judgment to the full court. It was narrowed in five steps — 1928-29, 1962-63, 1972, 1975 and 1981-83 — and every step was legislative or rule-based, and has been upheld. What remains is a grant with six exclusions, three statutory bars outside the Rule, and two gateways back into the barred classes. Procedurally it is *sui generis*. The Code does not apply to it of its own force; the Rules import Order XXII and Order XXXII by name; the Limitation Act governs time through Article 117; and everything else in the Code is available only as principle.

For the practitioner the discipline is the same at every stage: identify the jurisdictional fact first, put it in the first paragraph of the memorandum, and frame the grounds around a demonstrable error. *Ubi jus ibi remedium* is the premise of every appeal, but at Allahabad this remedy exists only where the Rules and the 1962 Act have left it.
