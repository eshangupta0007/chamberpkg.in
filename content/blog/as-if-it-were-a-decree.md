A decree settles what the parties owe each other. It does not deliver anything. Between the decree and the thing decreed there is a second proceeding, conducted by a Court that did not necessarily try the suit, against a party who has already lost once and against property that may turn out not to be his. Execution is where a judgment meets facts the judgment never found.

That produces disputes of two kinds, and they are structurally different. The first arises between the people the decree bound: whether it has been satisfied, whether it is capable of execution, whether the person sought to be proceeded against is a representative of one who was. The second arises with somebody who was never before the Court at all: the person whose property has been attached for another man's debt, or who is in possession of land that a decree-holder has come to take.

A code has to decide how much process each of these gets, and in particular which of them yields a decision that can be appealed. The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 answers that question in opposite directions for the two cases, and the answers were not always the ones it now gives. Both were changed by a single amending Act in a single year, by provisions placed sixty-nine sections apart, and the effect of the pair is that the fuller remedy belongs to the person who was never in the suit.

## What section 47 requires, and what it stopped being

Section 47(1) states the rule of exclusivity: "All questions arising between the parties to the suit in which the decree was passed, or their representatives, and relating to the execution, discharge or satisfaction of the decree, shall be determined by the Court executing the decree and not by a separate suit."

Sub-section (3) adds that where a question arises as to whether any person is or is not the representative of a party, that question "shall, for the purposes of this section, be determined by the Court." Sub-section (2) is no longer there; it was omitted by section 20 of the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1976 — Act 104 of 1976 — with effect from 1 February 1977.

Two Explanations follow, substituted by the same section 20 in place of the single Explanation that had stood before it. Explanation I provides that a plaintiff whose suit has been dismissed and a defendant against whom a suit has been dismissed are parties to the suit. Explanation II is in two limbs: "(a) For the purposes of this section, a purchaser of property at a sale in execution of a decree shall be deemed to be a party to the suit in which the decree is passed; and (b) all questions relating to the delivery of possession of such property to such purchaser or his representative shall be deemed to be questions relating to the execution, discharge or satisfaction of the decree within the meaning of this section."

So the section does two things at once. It compels a class of disputes into the execution proceeding, and it widens the class by deeming into it a person — the auction-purchaser — who was not a party to the suit in any ordinary sense, along with every question about getting him his possession.

The thing to notice is what section 47 does not say. It says who decides and where. It says nothing about what the decision is, or what may be done about it afterwards. For that, the section used to depend on the definition in section 2(2), and the definition no longer carries it.

Section 2(2) defines a decree as "the formal expression of an adjudication which, so far as regards the Court expressing it, conclusively determines the rights of the parties with regard to all or any of the matters in controversy in the suit and may be either preliminary or final", and then extends it: "It shall be deemed to include the rejection of a plaint and the determination of any question within ... section 144". Where the ellipsis stands, the official compilation prints an omission marker carrying a footnote, and the footnote reads: "The words and figures 'section 47 or' omitted by Act 104 of 1976, s. 3. (w.e.f. 1-2-1977)."

Before that date, a determination of a question within section 47 was a decree by force of the definition, and section 96(1) provides that, save where otherwise expressly provided, "an appeal shall lie from every decree passed by any Court exercising original jurisdiction to the Court authorized to hear appeals from the decisions of such Court." After that date the determination is an order, because section 2(14) defines an order as "the formal expression of any decision of a Civil Court which is not a decree", and nothing else in the Code brings it back within section 96.

What survives of the extension is worth reading beside what was deleted. Section 2(2) still deems a decree to include the rejection of a plaint, and still deems it to include the determination of any question within section 144 — the restitution section, which applies where a decree or order "is varied or reversed in any appeal, revision or other proceeding or is set aside or modified in any suit instituted for the purpose". Section 144 is itself a post-decree proceeding, and it is exclusive in the same way as section 47: sub-section (2) provides: "No suit shall be instituted for the purpose of obtaining any restitution or other relief which could be obtained by application under sub-section (1)." Two neighbouring provisions, both compelling a post-decree dispute between the same parties into an application rather than a suit. A determination under one of them is a decree; a determination under the other is not. The 1976 Act went into the single sentence that had covered both and removed one.

## What is left of the appeal

Nothing else in the Code supplies the appeal that section 2(2) withdrew, and the way the Code is built makes that easy to demonstrate rather than assume.

Section 104(1) opens: "An appeal shall lie from the following orders, and save as otherwise expressly provided in the body of this Code or by any law for the time being in force, from no other orders". The list that follows runs from clause (ff) to clause (i) — clauses (a) to (f) having been omitted by section 49 and Schedule III of Act 10 of 1940 — and contains no reference to section 47. Clause (i) carries in "any order made under rules from which an appeal is expressly allowed by rules", which sends the enquiry to Order XLIII.

Order XLIII Rule 1 is the list of appealable orders. It contains three entries referable to Order XXI: clause (i), for an order under Rule 34 on an objection to the draft of a document or an endorsement; clause (j), for an order under Rule 72 or Rule 92 "setting aside or refusing to set aside a sale"; and clause (ja), inserted by section 89 of Act 104 of 1976, for "an order rejecting an application made under sub-rule (1) of rule 106 of Order XXI, provided that an order on the original application, that is to say, the application referred to in sub-rule (1) of rule 105 of that Order is appealable". There is no entry for a determination under section 47.

Rule 1A(1) of Order XLIII, inserted by the same section 89, provides that where an order is made under the Code against a party "and thereupon any judgment is pronounced against such party and a decree is drawn up, such party may, in an appeal against the decree, contend that such order should not have been made and the judgment should not have been pronounced." That rule is the Code's general answer to a non-appealable order, and it is unavailable here for a structural reason: it operates through the appeal against the decree that follows the order, and in an execution proceeding no decree follows. The decree is what the proceeding started from.

What is left is section 115. The High Court may call for the record of a case decided by a subordinate Court "in which no appeal lies thereto", on the three jurisdictional grounds in clauses (a) to (c). But the proviso, substituted by section 12 of Act 46 of 1999 with effect from 1 July 2002, confines the power: the High Court shall not "vary or reverse any order made, or any order deciding an issue, in the course of a suit or other proceeding, except where the order, if it had been made in favour of the party applying for revision would have finally disposed of the suit or other proceedings."

So the party's remedy against a section 47 determination is a discretionary jurisdiction, exercisable only on jurisdictional error, and only where the order, decided the other way, would have finished the proceeding. Whether a particular execution order answers that description is a question for the case; the point here is simply that the Code has moved the party from an appeal as of right to a jurisdiction of that kind, and has done it by deleting a cross-reference from a definition.

## Investigation, and then adjudication

Now take the other half of the same Act.

Before 1 February 1977 the sub-heading above Rule 58 of Order XXI read "Investigation of claims and objections", and rules 58 to 63 under it set out what a Court did when a stranger said that attached property was his. The compilation no longer prints their text, but it preserves their marginal headings in brackets, and the headings are enough to show the shape of the old scheme. Rule 60 was "Release of property from attachment", Rule 61 "Disallowance of claim to property attached", Rule 62 "Continuance of attachment subject to claim of incumbrancer" — and the last of the group, Rule 63, was "Saving of suits to establish right to attached property". A group of rules that ends by saving suits is a group whose own orders were not the end of the matter.

Section 72 of Act 104 of 1976 substituted the sub-heading and rules 58 to 63 together, with effect from 1 February 1977. The new sub-heading is "Adjudication of claims and objections". Rules 60 to 63 were omitted by the same section, and the saving of suits went with them. What stands in their place is Rule 58, and it is built the other way round.

Rule 58(1) requires the Court to adjudicate: "Where any claim is preferred to, or any objection is made to the attachment of, any property attached in execution of a decree on the ground that such property is not liable to such attachment, the Court shall proceed to adjudicate upon the claim or objection in accordance with the provisions herein contained". The proviso then names the only two cases in which the Court does not: where, before the claim or objection, the property has already been sold; and "where the Court considers that the claim or objection was designedly or unnecessarily delayed."

Rule 58(2) fixes the scope, and the words in the bracket are the whole of its significance: "All questions (including questions relating to right, title or interest in the property attached) arising between the parties to a proceeding or their representatives under this rule and relevant to the adjudication of the claim or objection, shall be determined by the Court dealing with the claim or objection and not by a separate suit."

That is the formula of section 47(1), extended to a person who is not a party to the suit, and enlarged to cover title. An executing Court is not ordinarily a Court that tries title. Under this rule it must.

Rule 58(3) sets out what the Court may then do: allow the claim and release the property wholly or in part; disallow it; continue the attachment subject to any mortgage, charge or other interest in favour of any person; or pass such order as in the circumstances it deems fit.

Rule 58(4) then states the consequence, in the words that give this scheme its character. Where a claim or objection has been adjudicated upon under the rule, the order made thereon "shall have the same force and be subject to the same conditions as to appeal or otherwise as if it were a decree."

Rule 58(5) completes the design by showing where the old suit survives: only where the Court, under the proviso to sub-rule (1), refuses to entertain the claim at all. In that case the party against whom the refusal is made "may institute a suit to establish the right which he claims to the property in dispute", and the order refusing to entertain is otherwise conclusive. A suit is what the stranger gets when he is not adjudicated upon; when he is, he gets a decree.

## The person in possession

The same substitution was made at the other end of Order XXI, by the same section 72, for the case where the dispute is not about attachment but about possession.

The starting point is Rule 35(1): where a decree is for the delivery of immovable property, possession is delivered to the party to whom it has been adjudged, "and, if necessary, by removing any person bound by the decree who refuses to vacate the property." The warrant reaches persons bound by the decree. Rule 95, for an auction-purchaser who holds a certificate under Rule 94, reaches a little further — the judgment-debtor, a person holding on his behalf, and "some person claiming under a title created by the judgment-debtor subsequently to the attachment of such property" — and there too removal is of a person within a defined class.

Everybody else who is on the land is outside those words. For them, Rule 97(1) provides the route: "Where the holder of a decree for the possession of immovable property or the purchaser of any such property sold in execution of a decree is resisted or obstructed by any person in obtaining possession of the property, he may make an application to the Court complaining of such resistance or obstruction." Sub-rule (2), as substituted in 1976, removes the Court's choice: "Where any application is made under sub-rule (1), the Court shall proceed to adjudicate upon the application in accordance with the provisions herein contained."

Rule 99 gives the mirror-image application to the person already dispossessed: any person other than the judgment-debtor who is dispossessed by the decree-holder, or by the purchaser, may apply complaining of the dispossession, and the Court "shall proceed to adjudicate upon the application".

Rule 101 then does for these applications what Rule 58(2) does for attachment, and goes one step further: "All questions (including questions relating to right, title or interest in the property) arising between the parties to a proceeding on an application under rule 97 or rule 99 or their representatives, and relevant to the adjudication of the application, shall be determined by the Court dealing with the application and not by a separate suit and for this purpose, the Court shall, notwithstanding anything to the contrary contained in any other law for the time being in force, be deemed to have jurisdiction to decide such questions."

The non obstante clause and the deemed jurisdiction are there because the question may be one the executing Court would otherwise have no power to try. The Code confers the power rather than sending the parties away.

Rules 98 and 100 state the orders to be made upon that determination — allowing the application and directing possession, dismissing it, or such other order as the Court deems fit. Rule 98(2) adds a specific power where the Court is satisfied that the resistance was occasioned without any just cause by the judgment-debtor, or by someone at his instigation or on his behalf, or by a transferee pendente lite: the Court shall direct that the applicant be put into possession, and where he is still resisted, may order the judgment-debtor or the person acting for him "to be detained in the civil prison for a term which may extend to thirty days."

Rule 102 takes the transferee pendente lite outside Rules 98 and 100 altogether: nothing in those rules applies to resistance by a person to whom the judgment-debtor has transferred the property after the institution of the suit, or to the dispossession of such a person, and the Explanation adds that "transfer" includes a transfer by operation of law.

And Rule 103 supplies the same sentence as Rule 58(4): where an application has been adjudicated upon under Rule 98 or Rule 100, "the order made thereon shall have the same force and be subject to the same conditions as to an appeal or otherwise as if it were a decree."

Two further rules complete the group, and both are drafted on the assumption that these orders matter. Rule 104 makes every order under Rule 101 or Rule 103 subject to the result of any suit pending on the date the proceeding commenced, where in that suit the party against whom the order went has sought to establish a right claimed to the present possession of the property. (The rule as printed in the compilation drops a word from its own operative clause, so it is better paraphrased than quoted.) Rule 105 allows the application to be dismissed for the applicant's non-appearance and heard ex parte against an absent opposite party, and its Explanation carries the claim or objection under Rule 58 into the same machinery. Rule 106 lets the party against whom such an order was made apply within thirty days to have it set aside on sufficient cause for non-appearance. And Order XLIII Rule 1(ja), quoted above, gives an appeal from the rejection of that application — but only where an appeal would have lain against the order on the original application. The appeal from the setting-aside remedy is made to follow the appealability of the thing it restores.

## One question, two labels

Set Explanation II to section 47 beside Rule 97(1) and the two halves of the Act meet.

Explanation II(a) deems the purchaser at an execution sale to be a party to the suit. Explanation II(b) deems all questions relating to the delivery of possession of the property to him to be questions relating to the execution, discharge or satisfaction of the decree "within the meaning of this section". A question of that description is a section 47 question: it must be determined by the executing Court, it may not go to a separate suit, and since 1 February 1977 the determination of it is not a decree.

Rule 97(1) names the same purchaser as a person who may apply when resisted in obtaining possession of the same property. If he applies, the Court must adjudicate under Rule 97(2), must determine all questions including title under Rule 101, and the order made under Rule 98 has the force of a decree under Rule 103.

The same dispute — the purchaser, the property, delivery of possession — therefore answers to two descriptions in the Code at once, and the appeal consequence is opposite in each. The Code does not say which characterisation governs, and neither provision is drafted as subject to the other: Explanation II says "for the purposes of this section", and Rule 103 says "as if it were a decree", and neither carries a saving for the other. How the overlap falls to be resolved is not something the two texts settle between themselves, and it is a point on which the authorities have to be consulted in any given case. What can be said from the Code alone is that the question belongs at the outset of such a proceeding rather than at the end of it, because what turns on it is not the forum but the existence of an appeal.

The Explanation's local history is worth knowing in this State for a separate reason. The compilation records, under section 47, a State amendment: "In section 47 of the principal Act, Explanation II inserted by the U. P. Civil Laws (Reforms and Amendment) Act, 1954, shall be omitted", vide Uttar Pradesh Act 57 of 1976, section 3. What that provision in terms omits is an Explanation II inserted by a State Act of 1954 — not the Explanation II now printed in the section, which section 20 of Act 104 of 1976 substituted with effect from 1 February 1977. The two are different enactments that happen to carry the same number, and a submission on the point has to begin by identifying which of them is being spoken of.

## The gradient, and where it points

Put the two halves of Act 104 of 1976 side by side and the direction is unmistakable.

Section 3 of that Act removed "section 47 or" from the definition of decree. Section 20 omitted section 47(2) and substituted the Explanations. Section 72 replaced an investigation of a stranger's claim with an adjudication, abolished the suit that used to follow it, conferred jurisdiction to decide title, and declared the resulting order to have the force of a decree. Section 89 pruned seven clauses from the Order XLIII list and added the nested appeal in clause (ja).

The result is that the Code's fuller remedy now belongs to the person who was never a party. A stranger whose property is attached, or who is put out of possession under a decree that did not bind him, gets a compulsory adjudication, a determination of title, and an order appealable as a decree. The judgment-debtor, who was served, who appeared, who lost, and who now says that the decree has been satisfied or is not executable against him, gets a determination that the Code declines to call a decree and leaves to the revisional jurisdiction.

There is a coherent reason for part of that. The stranger's dispute has never been tried, and the 1976 scheme buys finality by giving him a real hearing in the execution proceeding instead of a later suit — it withdrew the suit and paid for it with the appeal. The party's dispute, by contrast, follows a trial, and curtailing the appeal is a way of stopping a decree-holder from being litigated into a second round. Neither half is unreasoned.

What is hard to defend is the pairing. The same Act, on the same day, made the stranger's post-decree order appealable as a decree and the party's post-decree order not appealable at all. The two were not opposite answers to one question; they were answers to two questions, and the Act did not place them beside each other. The result is a Code in which the right to a first appeal in execution turns on whether the person complaining was in the array of parties when the suit was tried — and in which an auction-purchaser, who is a stranger made a party by a deeming provision, can fall on either side of that line depending on which rule his application is brought under.

## Three things that follow at the Bar

The first concerns characterisation at the threshold. Whether an objection in execution is one under section 47 or one under Rule 58 or Rules 97 to 101 is not a matter of nomenclature. It decides whether the order that follows is appealable as a decree, and the choice is largely made by the applicant when he settles his application. An objection that an executing Court cannot try title is answered, for an application under Rule 97 or Rule 99, by the closing words of Rule 101.

The second concerns the proviso to Rule 58(1). The two grounds on which a Court may decline to entertain a claim are that the property has already been sold, and that the claim was "designedly or unnecessarily delayed". Both are findings, and the consequence of either is the loss of the adjudication and its deemed decree — Rule 58(5) leaves the claimant a suit instead. Delay in preferring a claim to attachment is therefore not a matter of indulgence; it converts one remedy into another.

The third concerns the record in a proceeding under Rules 97 to 101. Because Rule 101 requires the Court to determine questions of right, title and interest, and Rule 103 makes the order appealable as a decree, the adjudication is the trial of those questions and not a preliminary skirmish before one. Rule 104 preserves the effect of a suit already pending when the proceeding began, and Rule 102 excludes the transferee pendente lite from Rules 98 and 100 — so the date of the transfer and the date of the suit belong in the application. Evidence that would be led at a trial of title belongs there too, because the Code does not contemplate a later occasion for it.
