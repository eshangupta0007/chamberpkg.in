A civil suit produces two documents, and the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 keeps them apart with some care. One is the judgment, which is what the Court pronounces. The other is the decree, which is what the Court draws up afterwards and what the losing party appeals from. Nothing in the Code allows the two to be collapsed into one, and section 33 states the sequence in a single sentence: "The Court, after the case has been heard, shall pronounce judgment, and on such judgment a decree shall follow."

Between the pronouncement and the following there is an interval. The Code addresses that interval in a single rule, Order XX Rule 6A, and the way it addresses it is worth reading closely, because the obligation it creates is expressed in one register and the consequence of missing it is governed by a statute that uses another.

## The two documents, as the Code defines them

Section 2(2) defines the decree by what it does:

> "'decree' means the formal expression of an adjudication which, so far as regards the Court expressing it, conclusively determines the rights of the parties with regard to all or any of the matters in controversy in the suit and may be either preliminary or final."

Section 2(9) defines the judgment by what it contains:

> "'judgment' means the statement given by the Judge of the grounds of a decree or order"

The two definitions are not symmetrical, and the dependence runs the opposite way to the chronology. The decree is the formal expression of the adjudication; the judgment is the statement of the grounds of the decree. On the definitions, therefore, the judgment is described by reference to the decree — it states the grounds of a document that does not yet exist at the moment the judgment is pronounced. The decree is the operative instrument. It is the decree that conclusively determines rights, the decree that is executed, and the decree from which an appeal is preferred.

Order XX Rule 6(1) then fixes the relationship between them:

> "The decree shall agree with the judgment; it shall contain the number of the suit, the names and descriptions of the parties, their registered addresses, and particulars of the claim, and shall specify clearly the relief granted or other determination of the suit."

"Shall agree with the judgment" is the whole of the drafting instruction. The decree adds nothing and decides nothing. It is a transcription of the operative part of the judgment into the form in which the Code requires relief to be expressed. That is why the interval between the two is, in principle, administrative: no adjudication happens inside it.

## What Rule 6A asks for, and in what language

Order XX Rule 6A(1) reads:

> "Every endeavour shall be made to ensure that the decree is drawn up as expeditiously as possible and, in any case, within fifteen days from the date on which the judgment is pronounced."

Two things about that sentence. First, the obligation is addressed to nobody in particular. The rule does not say that the Court shall draw up the decree, or that the officer in charge of the record shall do so; it says that every endeavour shall be made. Second, the obligation is to endeavour, and the fifteen-day figure is governed by that verb. A decree drawn on the fortieth day is not drawn in breach of a prohibition. It is drawn in circumstances where, on the face of the record, the endeavour did not succeed.

This is not an accident of drafting peculiar to Rule 6A. The same construction governs the pronouncement of the judgment itself. The proviso to Order XX Rule 1(1) reads:

> "Provided that where the judgment is not pronounced at once, every endeavour shall be made by the Court to pronounce the judgment within thirty days from the date on which the hearing of the case was concluded but, where it is not practicable so to do on the ground of the exceptional and extraordinary circumstances of the case, the Court shall fix a future day for the pronouncement of the judgment, and such day shall not ordinarily be a day beyond sixty days from the date on which the hearing of the case was concluded, and due notice of the day so fixed shall be given to the parties or their pleaders."

Thirty days, extendable to a day that "shall not ordinarily be" beyond sixty, on grounds of "exceptional and extraordinary circumstances" of which the Court is the judge. Again there is a number, and again the number sits inside a formula that supplies its own release.

The contrast within the same rule is instructive. Order XX Rule 1 also carries a sub-rule applicable, by substitution, to commercial disputes of a specified value:

> "The Commercial Court, Commercial Division, or Commercial Appellate Division, as the case may be, shall, within ninety days of the conclusion of arguments, pronounce judgment and copies thereof shall be issued to all the parties to the dispute through electronic mail or otherwise."

Ninety days is a longer period than thirty. But it is stated as a direction — "shall, within ninety days" — and not as an endeavour, and it carries with it an obligation to issue copies. Where the legislature wanted a timetable in that form, it wrote one. In Rule 6A it did not.

## The date the decree bears

The practical consequence of the interval is settled not by Rule 6A but by Rule 7:

> "The decree shall bear the day on which the judgment was pronounced, and, when the judge has satisfied himself that the decree has been drawn up in accordance with the judgment, he shall sign the decree."

So the decree is back-dated to the judgment, by operation of the rule, however long the drawing takes. A decree signed on the ninetieth day bears the date of pronouncement. Rule 8 confirms how little the signing is supposed to add: where a Judge has vacated office after pronouncing judgment but without signing the decree, "a decree drawn up in accordance with such judgment may be signed by his successor or, if the Court has ceased to exist, by the Judge of any Court to which such Court was subordinate." A successor who never heard the suit can sign it, because the signature certifies only agreement with the judgment, not the adjudication.

The judgment, by contrast, closes on the day it is pronounced. Order XX Rule 3:

> "The judgment shall be dated and signed by the Judge in open Court at the time of pronouncing it and, when once signed, shall not afterwards be altered or added to, save as provided by section 152 or on review."

Section 152 permits correction of "[c]lerical or arithmetical mistakes in judgments, decrees or orders or errors arising therein from any accidental slip or omission" at any time, on the Court's own motion or on an application. That is the only route by which a drawn decree can be reconciled with a judgment it has failed to transcribe correctly, short of review or appeal. It is a narrow route: the mistake must be clerical, arithmetical, or an accidental slip or omission. A decree that gets the relief wrong because the judgment was ambiguous is not obviously within it.

## The clock

The period for an appeal is not in the Code. It is in the Schedule to the Limitation Act, 1963. Article 116 provides for appeals under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 — ninety days for an appeal to a High Court from any decree or order, and thirty days for an appeal to any other court — and in both cases the period begins to run from what the third column of the Schedule states as "The date of the decree or order". Article 117 provides thirty days for an appeal from a decree or order of any High Court to the same Court, running from the same point.

Section 3(1) states the consequence of overshooting, and states it without reference to the parties' conduct:

> "Subject to the provisions contained in sections 4 to 24 (inclusive), every suit instituted, appeal preferred, and application made after the prescribed period shall be dismissed, although limitation has not been set up as a defence."

Put Article 116 beside Order XX Rule 7 and the structure becomes visible. Limitation for the appeal runs from the date of the decree; the date of the decree is the date on which the judgment was pronounced; and the decree may not come into existence for weeks after that date. The ninety days, or the thirty, are therefore not ninety or thirty days in which to decide whether to appeal and to prepare the memorandum. They are ninety or thirty days *inclusive of* the time the court takes to produce the document appealed from.

## What section 12 excludes, and what its Explanation does not

Section 12 of the Limitation Act is the provision that redistributes some of that interval. Sub-section (2):

> "In computing the period of limitation for an appeal or an application for leave to appeal or for revision or for review of a judgment, the day on which the judgment complained of was pronounced and the time requisite for obtaining a copy of the decree, sentence or order appealed from or sought to be revised or reviewed shall be excluded."

Sub-section (3):

> "Where a decree or order is appealed from or sought to be revised or reviewed, or where an application is made for leave to appeal from a decree or order, the time requisite for obtaining a copy of the judgment shall also be excluded."

Both exclusions are measured by the same phrase — "the time requisite for obtaining a copy" — and the Explanation tells us where that time starts:

> "In computing under this section the time requisite for obtaining a copy of a decree or an order, any time taken by the court to prepare the decree or order before an application for a copy thereof is made shall not be excluded."

Two things about that Explanation. It is expressed in terms of "a copy of a decree or an order" and not of a copy of the judgment, so on its own words it bars the exclusion of the court's pre-application delay in preparing a decree or an order and says nothing about the judgment — a distinction of limited practical consequence, since Rule 6B requires the judgment copy to be available immediately in any event. And it is the operative sentence for anyone keeping a limitation diary. The court's delay in drawing the decree is excluded from the computation only from the date on which the party applies for a copy. Whatever part of that delay precedes the application is the party's to absorb. A litigant who waits for intimation that the decree is ready before applying for a certified copy converts the court's delay into his own, and the conversion is complete: section 12 does not restore it, because the Explanation says in terms that it "shall not be excluded".

What section 5 offers after that is of a different character. It permits an appeal to be admitted after the prescribed period "if the appellant or the applicant satisfies the court that he had sufficient cause for not preferring the appeal or making the application within such period", and the Explanation to that section treats being misled by an order, practice or judgment of the High Court as capable of amounting to sufficient cause. That is a discretion to be invoked, pleaded and satisfied, and under Order XLI Rule 3A an appeal presented after the period must be accompanied by an application supported by affidavit setting out the facts relied on. It is not a right, and it is not a substitute for the exclusion that section 12 would have given automatically had the copy been applied for at once.

## The appeal that travels on the judgment alone

Order XX Rule 6A(2) addresses the case where the decree is not ready and the appeal cannot wait:

> "An appeal may be preferred against the decree without filing a copy of the decree and in such a case the copy made available to the party by the court shall for the purposes of rule 1 of Order XLI be treated as the decree. But as soon as the decree is drawn, the judgment shall cease to have the effect of a decree for the purposes of execution or for any other purpose."

The copy referred to is the one Rule 6B requires: "Where the judgment is pronounced, copies of the judgment shall be made available to the parties immediately after the pronouncement of the judgment for preferring an appeal on payment of such charges as may be specified in the rule made by the High Court." Immediately, on payment, and the stated purpose is the preferring of an appeal.

Order XLI Rule 1(1) now asks for no more than that:

> "Every appeal shall be preferred in the form of a memorandum signed by the appellant or his pleader and presented to the Court or to such officer as it appoints in this behalf. The memorandum shall be accompanied by a copy of the Judgment."

So the appeal is competent on the judgment. The appellant does not have to wait for the decree, and the ninety days under Article 116 can be used for the appeal rather than for the waiting.

Two features of this arrangement are worth noting, because they are features of the text and not of practice.

The first is a redundancy. Rule 6A(2) deems the court-supplied copy to be "the decree" for the purposes of Order XLI Rule 1 — but Order XLI Rule 1, as it now stands, does not require a copy of the decree. It requires a copy of the judgment, which is the very document the deeming clause operates on. The deeming provision is answering a requirement that the same statutory amendment removed. The overlap is harmless where the appellate court asks for nothing beyond the judgment. It is not idle, because the deeming clause is what answers a requirement for a decree copy wherever one survives — in a rule framed by a High Court, or in a form prescribed for a particular class of appeal.

The second is a presupposition. The second sentence of Rule 6A(2) provides that as soon as the decree is drawn, "the judgment shall cease to have the effect of a decree for the purposes of execution or for any other purpose." A provision that withdraws an effect on a stated date presupposes that the effect existed before it. On the plain words, therefore, between pronouncement and the drawing of the decree the judgment has the effect of a decree — for execution, and for every other purpose. The Code does not say so anywhere else, and no sub-rule sets out the conditions on which that effect operates or what a decree-holder is to produce in order to rely on it. The entire content of the proposition is carried by a sentence whose grammatical function is to end it.

## One amending Act, three provisions

The footnotes to the official compilations locate all of this in the same legislative moment. Rules 6A and 6B of Order XX in their present form were substituted by Act 46 of 1999, section 28, with effect from 1 July 2002. The words now in Order XLI Rule 1(1) were substituted by section 31 of the same Act, with effect from the same date. And the words "on which the decree or order is founded", which once qualified the copy of the judgment in section 12(3) of the Limitation Act, 1963, were omitted by section 33 of that Act, again with effect from 1 July 2002.

Read together, those three amendments do one thing. Before them, the appeal was built on the decree and the judgment came along as the document explaining it; section 12(3) said as much by describing the judgment as the one "on which the decree or order is founded". After them, the appeal is preferred on a copy of the judgment, the copy is to be furnished immediately on pronouncement, and the Limitation Act refers to the judgment without tying it to a decree. The decree remains the instrument that conclusively determines rights and the instrument that is executed. But it is no longer the document the appeal has to wait for.

What the same amendments did not do is attach a consequence to the fifteenth day. Order XX Rule 6A(1) is a target, and the Code contains no provision that is triggered by its being missed — no deemed decree on the sixteenth day, no extension of the appeal period, and no provision requiring the court to inform the party that the decree is now ready. The amendments of 1999 solved the appellant's problem by making the appeal independent of the decree, not by making the decree punctual.

## What that leaves for the decree-holder

The appellant's position is therefore provided for. The successful party's is provided for in part, and the part that is left out is identifiable.

Execution in the court that passed the decree does not, on the text, wait for the decree to be drawn. Order XXI Rule 11(2) requires every written application for execution to contain a list of particulars in tabular form, and the particulars include "the date of the decree" — a date which Rule 7 of Order XX supplies on the day of pronouncement — but the sub-rule does not require a copy of the decree to be annexed. Sub-rule (3) then provides that the Court to which such an application is made "may require the applicant to produce a certified copy of the decree." It is a power, not a condition. And Rule 11(1) goes further in the opposite direction: where the decree is for the payment of money, the Court may on the decree-holder's oral application "at the time of the passing of the decree, order immediate execution thereof by the arrest of the judgment-debtor, prior to the preparation of a warrant if he is within the precincts of the Court." That sub-rule contemplates execution beginning before anything has been drawn up at all.

Transfer of the decree to another court for execution is different, and it is where the interval bites. Order XXI Rule 6 provides that the Court sending a decree for execution "shall send— (a) a copy of the decree" together with the certificates specified in clauses (b) and (c), and Rule 7 requires the receiving court to file those copies and certificates without further proof of the decree. A copy cannot be sent of a document that has not been drawn. So where execution has to be sought outside the jurisdiction of the court that decided the suit, the decree-holder's remedy is held up by the drawing, and the Code supplies no interim equivalent of the copy that Rule 6 requires to be sent.

There is also a drafting consequence that falls on both sides. The decree is the document that states the relief in the form the Code prescribes: the description of immovable property sufficient to identify it under Order XX Rule 9, the alternative money figure where a decree for delivery of movable property may not be satisfied in specie under Rule 10, the instalments under Order XX Rule 11, and the costs and their incidence under Rule 6(2). Those are precisely the particulars that a judgment often leaves to the drawing. For as long as the drawing is outstanding, the operative terms of the relief remain unexpressed in the only document the Code requires to express them clearly.

## Three things that follow at the Bar

**Apply for the certified copy on the day of pronouncement, not when the decree is ready.** The Explanation to section 12 of the Limitation Act is explicit that time taken by the court to prepare the decree before the copy application is made is not excluded. The application is the event that starts the exclusion. The application can be made the same day, against the prescribed folio and copying charges; the alternative is to have to plead sufficient cause under section 5 for a delay that was never the party's.

**Treat the ninety days under Article 116 as ninety days from pronouncement.** The decree bears the date of the judgment under Order XX Rule 7, so no limitation accrues from the drawing being slow. Where instructions to appeal are forthcoming, there is no reason to hold the memorandum: Order XLI Rule 1(1) requires only a copy of the judgment, Rule 6B requires that copy to be made available immediately, and Rule 6A(2) puts the competence of such an appeal beyond argument.

**Read the decree against the judgment when it does arrive, and read it for the particulars.** Order XX Rule 6(1) requires the decree to agree with the judgment, and Rule 7 makes the Judge's signature a certificate that the decree has been drawn up in accordance with it. But the drawing itself is office work done after the hearing has ended, and Rule 8 permits the certificate to be given by a successor who never heard the suit. A decree that departs from the judgment in the description of property, in costs, in the instalments, or in the alternative value of movables is correctible under section 152 only so far as the departure is clerical, arithmetical, or an accidental slip or omission. The window for noticing it closes when the appeal period does.
